Mughal Vansh: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 15 min read · BPSC SSC

The Mughal Empire (1526–1857 CE) was the largest and most powerful empire in medieval Indian history. Founded by Babur after the First Battle of Panipat (1526 CE), it reached its zenith under Akbar, Jahangir, Shah Jahan and Aurangzeb. The Mughals ruled most of the Indian subcontinent for over three centuries and left behind an extraordinary legacy of art, architecture, administration and culture. For BPSC and SSC, the Mughals are the single most important topic in medieval Indian history.

Babur (1526–1530 CE)

Founder of the Mughal Empire. Descended from Timur on his father's side and Genghis Khan on his mother's side.

  • Lost his ancestral kingdom Ferghana; captured Kabul in 1504 CE.
  • Invaded India on the invitation of Daulat Khan Lodi and Rana Sanga.
  • First Battle of Panipat (21 April 1526 CE) — defeated Ibrahim Lodi, the last Lodi Sultan. Used gunpowder, artillery and the Tulughma tactic (flanking).
  • Battle of Khanwa (1527 CE) — defeated Rana Sanga of Mewar.
  • Battle of Chanderi (1528 CE) — defeated Medini Rai.
  • Battle of Ghaghra (1529 CE) — defeated the Afghan chiefs of Bihar and Bengal (Mahmud Lodi and Nusrat Shah).
  • Died in 1530 CE at Agra; buried first at Agra, later at Kabul (Bagh-e-Babur).
  • Wrote the Baburnama — one of the great autobiographies in world literature.
Remember: The First Battle of Panipat (1526 CE) marks the beginning of the Mughal Empire and the end of the Delhi Sultanate. Babur's use of field artillery was decisive.

Humayun (1530–1540, 1555–1556 CE)

Son of Babur. His reign was marked by constant struggle with the Afghans and his half-brothers.

  • Battle of Chausa (1539 CE) — defeated by Sher Shah Suri.
  • Battle of Kannauj (1540 CE) — decisive defeat; Humayun fled to Persia.
  • Spent 15 years in exile — in Sindh, Persia and Afghanistan.
  • Received help from Shah Tahmasp of Persia; captured Kandahar and Kabul.
  • Regained Delhi in 1555 CE after Sher Shah's successors weakened.
  • Died in 1556 CE after falling from the stairs of his library (Sher Mandal, Purana Qila, Delhi).
  • His tomb, Humayun's Tomb (Delhi), was built by his widow Bega Begum (Haji Begum) — a precursor to the Taj Mahal.

Akbar (1556–1605 CE) — The Greatest Mughal

Son of Humayun; ascended the throne at the age of 13 under the regency of Bairam Khan. His reign is considered the true foundation of the Mughal Empire.

Early Conquests

  • Second Battle of Panipat (1556 CE) — defeated Hemu (general of Adil Shah Suri).
  • Defeated the Sur Afghans, annexed Malwa (1561), Gondwana (1564), Chittor (1568), Ranthambore (1569), Gujarat (1572), Bengal (1576), Kabul (1585), Sindh (1591), Kandahar (1595), and parts of the Deccan.
  • Battle of Haldighati (1576 CE) — defeated Maharana Pratap of Mewar.

Rajput Policy

  • Married Jodha Bai (daughter of Raja Bharmal of Amber) in 1562 CE.
  • Gave high mansabs to Rajput chiefs; many became trusted generals.
  • Raja Man Singh and Raja Birbal were among his most trusted courtiers.
  • Abolished the Jizya (tax on non-Muslims) in 1564 CE.
  • Abolished Pilgrimage Tax in 1563 CE.

Religious Policy & Din-i-Ilahi

  • Founded Ibadat Khana (1575 CE) at Fatehpur Sikri for religious discussions.
  • Promulgated Din-i-Ilahi (1582 CE) — a syncretic faith combining elements of Islam, Hinduism, Christianity, Jainism and Zoroastrianism. Birbal was the only Hindu to accept it.
  • Issued the Mazhar (Infallibility Decree) in 1579 CE — gave him supreme authority in religious matters.
  • Patronised all religions; his wife Mariam-uz-Zamani was a Rajput; his court had Hindu, Muslim, Jain, Parsi and Christian members.

Administration

  • Mansabdari system — ranked officials (Mansabdars) held Zat (personal status) and Sawar (cavalry) ranks; they maintained troops for the emperor.
  • Jagirdari system — Mansabdars were paid through land assignments (Jagirs).
  • Dahsala (Zabti) system — a land revenue system developed by Raja Todar Mal (1580 CE); fixed cash revenue based on 10-year average produce.
  • Divided the empire into 15 Subahs (provinces), each governed by a Subadar.
  • Faujdar — military commander of a district; Kotwal — city police chief; Amil — revenue collector.
  • Introduced the Ilahi era and standardised coinage.
Exam Tip: Raja Todar Mal developed the Dahsala/Zabti land revenue system. Abul Fazl wrote the Akbarnama and Ain-i-Akbari. The Navratnas (Nine Gems) of Akbar's court: Todar Mal, Abul Fazl, Faizi, Birbal, Tansen, Raja Man Singh, Abdul Rahim Khan-i-Khanan, Mulla Do-Piyaza, Fakir Aziao-Din.

Art, Architecture & Culture under Akbar

  • Built Fatehpur Sikri (1571 CE) — his new capital, near Agra; included the Buland Darwaza (1576 CE), Panch Mahal, Diwan-i-Aam, Diwan-i-Khas, Jama Masjid, and Tomb of Salim Chishti.
  • Built the Agra Fort in red sandstone.
  • Patronised the musician Tansen — one of the Navratnas.
  • Patronised Mughal painting — the Akbarnama and Razmnama (Persian translation of the Mahabharata) contain famous illustrations.
  • Daswanth and Basawan were famous painters of his court.
  • Akbar's tomb at Sikandra (near Agra) — completed by Jahangir.

Jahangir (1605–1627 CE)

Son of Akbar; his reign was relatively peaceful but marked by the growing influence of his wife Nur Jahan.

  • Set up a Chain of Justice (Zanjir-i-Adl) outside Agra Fort — anyone could pull it to seek justice.
  • Famous for his 12 ordinances (1605 CE), including prohibition of wine, animal slaughter on certain days, and a ban on illegal taxes.
  • Married Nur Jahan (born Mehr-un-Nisa) in 1611 CE; she became the most powerful woman in Mughal history.
  • Nur Jahan's family (father Itimad-ud-Daula, brother Asaf Khan) dominated the court; her niece Mumtaz Mahal married Prince Khurram (Shah Jahan).
  • Executed the Sikh Guru Arjan Dev (1606 CE) — first Mughal-Sikh conflict.
  • Lost Kandahar to the Persians in 1622 CE.
  • Rebellion by his son Prince Khurram (Shah Jahan) — briefly subdued.
  • Died in 1627 CE; buried at Shahdara Bagh, Lahore.
  • Wrote the Tuzuk-i-Jahangiri — his autobiography.

Art under Jahangir

  • Mughal painting reached its peak of naturalism under Jahangir.
  • Famous painters: Ustad Mansur (birds and animals), Abul Hasan, Bishandas (portraits).
  • Patronised the Shalimar Gardens and the Tomb of Itimad-ud-Daula (Agra) — the first Mughal tomb made entirely of white marble with pietra dura inlay.

Shah Jahan (1628–1658 CE) — The Golden Age

Son of Jahangir; his reign is called the Golden Age of Mughal architecture and one of the most prosperous periods in Indian history.

  • Took the title Shah Jahan ("King of the World"); earlier known as Prince Khurram.
  • Annexed Ahmednagar, parts of Bijapur and Golconda.
  • Lost Kandahar permanently to Persia in 1649 CE.
  • Faced famine and peasant revolts in Gujarat and the Deccan.
  • His wife Mumtaz Mahal died in 1631 CE during childbirth at Burhanpur; he built the Taj Mahal at Agra in her memory (1632–1653 CE).

Exam Tip: The Taj Mahal was designed by Ustad Ahmad Lahori and completed in 1653 CE. It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of the Seven Wonders of the World. Shah Jahan is called the "Engineer King" for his architectural patronage.

War of Succession (1657–1658 CE)

Shah Jahan fell ill in 1657, triggering a war of succession among his four sons: Dara Shikoh, Shuja, Aurangzeb and Murad.

  • Battle of Dharmat (1658) — Aurangzeb and Murad defeated Dara Shikoh's army.
  • Battle of Samugarh (1658) — decisive defeat for Dara Shikoh.
  • Battle of Khajwa (1659) — Aurangzeb defeated Shuja.
  • Aurangzeb executed Murad and later Dara Shikoh (1659 CE) — Dara was charged with apostasy and executed.
  • Shah Jahan was imprisoned in the Agra Fort until his death in 1666 CE; buried in the Taj Mahal.

Aurangzeb (1658–1707 CE)

The last of the great Mughals; his reign saw the largest territorial extent of the empire — but also the beginning of its decline.

  • Took the title Alamgir ("Conqueror of the World").
  • Ruled for nearly 50 years; empire stretched from Kashmir to the Kaveri and from Gujarat to Assam.
  • Annexed Bijapur (1686) and Golconda (1687).
  • Reimposed the Jizya on non-Muslims in 1679 CE — a major point of controversy.
  • Destroyed many Hindu temples, including Vishwanath Temple (Kashi) and Keshava Deva Temple (Mathura).
  • Executed the Sikh Guru Tegh Bahadur in 1675 CE.
  • Faced the rebellion of Shivaji and the Marathas, the Jats, the Satnamis, the Sikhs, and the Rajputs.
  • Waged a long, costly Deccan War (1681–1707) against the Marathas — often called the "ulcer" that destroyed the Mughal treasury.
  • His religious intolerance and constant warfare strained the empire.
  • Died in 1707 CE at Ahmadnagar; buried at Khuldabad (near Aurangabad).
BPSC Must-Know: Aurangzeb's Deccan policy is often cited as the main reason for the decline of the Mughal Empire. His 25-year war against the Marathas drained the treasury and exposed the weakness of the Mughal military.

Mughal Administration

  • Emperor — supreme authority; assisted by a council of ministers.
  • Wazir (Prime Minister) — head of the council; looked after finance.
  • Diwan-i-Aam — hall of public audience; Diwan-i-Khas — hall of private audience.
  • Mansabdari system — ranking system; Mansabdars held Zat and Sawar ranks.
  • Jagirdari system — Mansabdars were paid through Jagirs (land assignments).
  • Subahs — provinces, governed by Subadar.
  • Faujdar — military commander; Kotwal — police chief; Amil — revenue officer.
  • Zabti/Dahsala — land revenue system by Raja Todar Mal.
  • Karkhanas — royal workshops for manufacturing.
  • Qazi — judge; Mufti — legal advisor.

Key Features of Mughal Architecture

  • Red sandstone — dominant material in early Mughal architecture; white marble — later phase.
  • Pietra dura — inlay of semi-precious stones.
  • Char Bagh — four-quartered Persian garden layout.
  • Bulbous dome, minarets, pishtaq (rectangular frame), chhatris (domed pavilions).
  • Jali — perforated screens.
  • Blend of Persian, Indian, Central Asian and Islamic styles.

Mughal Painting

  • Akbar period — Akbarnama, Razmnama; painters Daswanth and Basawan.
  • Jahangir period — naturalism at its peak; Ustad Mansur (birds/animals), Abul Hasan, Bishandas.
  • Shah Jahan period — formal portraits; courtly elegance.
  • Aurangzeb period — decline of painting due to religious orthodoxy.

Music

  • Tansen — the greatest musician of Akbar's court; one of the Navratnas.
  • Mughal court music blended Indian and Persian traditions.

Mughal-Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • Babur's Battle of Ghaghra (1529 CE) — fought in Bihar; defeated Afghan chiefs of Bihar and Bengal.
  • Sher Shah Suri (born in Sasaram, Bihar) — the Afghan ruler who briefly ended Mughal rule (1540–1555 CE). He was a great administrator; the Grand Trunk Road was his creation.
  • Bihar was a Subah (province) of the Mughal Empire — initially part of Bengal, later a separate province.
  • Man Singh (Akbar's general) was Governor of Bihar and Bengal; he put down Afghan revolts in Bihar.
  • Battle of Buxar (1764 CE) — fought in Bihar; Mughal emperor Shah Alam II, Nawab of Awadh and Mir Qasim were defeated by the British. This effectively ended Mughal political power.
  • Mughal architecture in Bihar: the Maner Sharif (tomb of Sufi saint Makhdoom Daulat) — early Mughal style; Rohtas Fort (Rohtasgarh, Bihar) — earlier Afghan structure; various Mughal-era mosques and tombs.
  • Patna (Pataliputra) was an important Mughal city; the Patna Museum houses Mughal coins and inscriptions.
  • The Mughal Empire's decline in Bihar opened the door for the British East India Company.
BPSC Must-Know: Sher Shah Suri was born in Sasaram (Bihar) and briefly overthrew Mughal rule. The Battle of Buxar (1764 CE), fought in Bihar, is often considered the true beginning of British rule in India. Both topics are essential for BPSC.

Decline of the Mughal Empire

Immediate Causes

  • Aurangzeb's religious intolerance — Jizya, temple destruction, execution of Sikh Gurus.
  • Costly Deccan War (1681–1707) — drained the treasury.
  • Weak successors — after Aurangzeb, no able ruler could hold the empire together.
  • War of succession after every emperor's death.

External Invasions & Internal Rebellions

  • Nadir Shah's invasion (1739 CE) — defeated Muhammad Shah at the Battle of Karnal; sacked Delhi; took the Peacock Throne and Koh-i-Noor diamond.
  • Ahmad Shah Abdali's invasions — defeated the Mughals at the Third Battle of Panipat (1761 CE) against the Marathas; though Abdali won, the Mughals were already powerless.
  • Rise of the Marathas — under Shivaji and the Peshwas; became the dominant power in central and western India.
  • Rise of the Sikhs — under Guru Gobind Singh and later Maharaja Ranjit Singh.
  • Rise of the Jats, Rohillas and Rajputs — carved out independent states.
  • British East India Company — gradually took control of Bengal (Battle of Plassey 1757), Bihar and Awadh (Battle of Buxar 1764).

End of the Mughal Empire

  • By the early 19th century, the Mughal emperor was a pensioner of the British with authority limited to the Red Fort.
  • Bahadur Shah Zafar was the last Mughal emperor.
  • During the Revolt of 1857, the rebels proclaimed him their leader.
  • After the revolt was crushed, the British exiled him to Rangoon (Burma) in 1858, where he died in 1862.
  • The Government of India Act, 1858 ended the Mughal dynasty and transferred power to the British Crown.
  • Thus ended the Mughal Empire (1526–1857 CE) after 331 years.
