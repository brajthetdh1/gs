Khalji Dynasty: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 12 min read · BPSC SSC

The Khalji Dynasty (1290–1320 CE) was the second dynasty of the Delhi Sultanate. Though short-lived, it produced one of the most powerful rulers in Indian history — Alauddin Khalji. His market control reforms, successful defence against the Mongol invasions, and ambitious Deccan campaigns make the Khalji period one of the most studied topics for BPSC and SSC. The dynasty also saw the first successful Turkish conquest of large parts of South India.

Exam Tip: Zia-ud-din Barani's Tarikh-i-Firuz Shahi and Amir Khusrau's Khazain-ul-Futuh are the two most important sources for the Khalji period. Barani was a contemporary of Alauddin and his account is the basis for most of our knowledge.

Background — The Khaljis

  • The Khaljis were of Turkish-Afghan origin — they had long settled in Afghanistan before coming to India.
  • They were not considered "pure Turks" by the Turkish nobles, and were looked down upon at the Slave court.
  • By the late 13th century, the Khalji nobles had gained considerable power in the Delhi Sultanate.
  • After Balban's death (1287 CE), the throne passed to the incompetent Kaiqubad.
  • In 1290 CE, the Khalji commander Jalal-ud-din Khalji overthrew Kaiqubad and founded the Khalji Dynasty.

Jalal-ud-din Khalji (1290–1296 CE)

Founder of the Khalji Dynasty. He was a mild, generous ruler whose policy of conciliation ultimately cost him his life.

  • Took the title Jalal-ud-din; was around 70 years old when he ascended the throne.
  • Adopted a policy of conciliation towards the Turkish nobles — a departure from Balban's harsh methods.
  • Suppressed the revolt of Malik Chhajju (a descendant of Balban) but pardoned him.
  • Captured the fort of Ranthambore from the Rajputs; appointed his nephew Alauddin as governor of Kara-Manikpur.
  • Sent Alauddin to invade the Deccan — this proved to be a fatal mistake.
  • Alauddin invaded Devagiri (1296 CE), plundered vast wealth, and returned to Kara without sending the treasure to Delhi.
  • Jalal-ud-din went to meet Alauddin at Kara — where he was murdered by Alauddin's men in July 1296 CE.
Remember: Jalal-ud-din Khalji was murdered by his own nephew Alauddin Khalji in 1296 CE after returning from the successful Devagiri campaign. His mildness and misplaced trust caused his downfall.

Alauddin Khalji (1296–1316 CE) — The Greatest Khalji

Alauddin Khalji is regarded as one of the greatest rulers of the Delhi Sultanate — a brilliant military commander, an innovative administrator, and a ruler whose reforms are still studied today.

Accession & Early Challenges

  • Assumed the throne in 1296 CE after murdering Jalal-ud-din.
  • Faced opposition from the nobles and from his own family — his brother-in-law Almas Beg and other relatives were suppressed.
  • Took the title Sikandar-i-Sani ("Alexander the Second").

Mongol Invasions — Alauddin's Greatest Achievement

Alauddin successfully defended India against a series of devastating Mongol invasions — a feat unmatched by any other Sultan.

Mongol InvasionYearResult
First invasion1292 CERepelled by Jalal-ud-din (earlier)
Second invasion1297 CEDefeated by Alauddin's general Ulugh Khan
Third invasion (Qutlugh Khwaja)1299 CEDefeated near Delhi; Mongols defeated at Amroha
Fourth invasion (Ali Beg & Tartaq)1303 CEDefeated; Ali Beg and Tartaq captured and executed
Fifth invasion (Kebek)1304 CEDefeated at Amroha
Sixth invasion (Iqbalmand)1305 CEDefeated; 8,000 Mongols killed
Seventh invasion (Kubak)1306 CEDefeated; Kubak captured and executed
  • Alauddin fortified Delhi — built the Siri Fort (also called Qila-i-Rai Pithora) to defend against the Mongols.
  • He maintained a large standing army — the first Sultan to do so on such a scale.
  • Adopted the policy of massacre of Mongol prisoners and building a wall of skulls near Delhi — a grim warning to future invaders.
  • Appointed trusted generals like Ulugh Khan (Almas Beg), Zafar Khan, and Malik Kafur.
Exam Tip: Alauddin Khalji's defence against the Mongols is his most celebrated achievement. He built the Siri Fort and adopted a policy of ruthless retaliation, which successfully deterred future Mongol invasions for decades.

Conquests — Northern India

  • Gujarat (1299 CE) — conquered; Rani Kamala Devi captured; Malik Kafur (a slave) entered Alauddin's service here.
  • Ranthambore (1301 CE) — captured after a long siege; the Rajput chief Hamir Deva was killed.
  • Mewar / Chittor (1303 CE) — captured after a famous siege; Rani Padmini's jauhar (mass self-immolation) is associated with this event. Alauddin appointed his son Khizr Khan as governor.
  • Malwa (1305 CE) — conquered; the Paramara ruler Mahlakadeva was defeated.
  • Siwana and Jalore — the Rajput chiefs Sital Deva and Kanhadadeva were defeated.

Deccan Campaigns — Malik Kafur's Expeditions

Alauddin was the first Delhi Sultan to send a major expedition into South India — a landmark achievement led by his trusted general and slave Malik Kafur.

CampaignYearKingdomResult
First Deccan expedition1306–07 CEDevagiri (Yadava)Raja Ramachandra defeated; became a vassal
Second Deccan expedition1309–10 CEWarangal (Kakatiya)Raja Prataparudra defeated; heavy tribute paid
Third Deccan expedition1310–11 CEDwarasamudra (Hoysala)King Ballala III defeated; tribute paid
Fourth Deccan expedition1311 CEMadurai (Pandya)Ravaged the Pandya kingdom; huge booty including the famous Koh-i-Noor diamond
Second Devagiri campaign1313 CEDevagiri (Yadava)Ramachandra's successor revolted; Devagiri annexed
  • Malik Kafur — a former slave — was appointed Malik Naib (regent) and given the title Kafur Hazar Dinari.
  • The Deccan campaigns brought enormous wealth to Delhi — including gold, silver, elephants, horses and precious stones.
  • However, the Deccan kingdoms were not permanently annexed — they remained vassals.
BPSC Must-Know: Malik Kafur's Deccan campaigns (1306–1313 CE) were the first major Turkish expeditions into South India. The Koh-i-Noor diamond is said to have been brought to Delhi during the Madurai campaign. Alauddin's Deccan policy was not fully successful — permanent annexation was never achieved.

Alauddin's Market Control Reforms

Alauddin's most famous administrative innovation was his market control policy — a comprehensive system of price regulation designed to keep the army well-supplied and the capital stable.

Four Markets of Delhi

  • Mandai — grain market.
  • Sarai-i-Adl — cloth and general merchandise market.
  • Shahna-i-Mandi — market for horses, slaves, cattle.
  • Shahna-i-Mandi (second) — market for miscellaneous goods.

Key Features

  • Fixed prices were set for all commodities — grain, cloth, horses, cattle, and slaves.
  • Shahna-i-Mandi — a special officer was appointed to supervise the markets.
  • Barid and Munhiyan — spies reported on prices and violations.
  • Merchants had to register with the government and obtain licenses.
  • Hoarding was banned — severe punishments, including death, were prescribed.
  • Special concessions were given to merchants to encourage the supply of goods.

Purpose of Market Reforms

  • To maintain a large army at low cost — the Sultan needed to pay soldiers well but keep costs down.
  • To ensure low prices for the army and the capital.
  • To prevent the hoarding of grain during Mongol invasions.
  • To strengthen the Sultan's control over the economy.

Other Economic Measures

  • Land revenue was raised to 50% (from 33%) in the Doab region.
  • Measurement (Masahat) of land was introduced for assessment.
  • Taxation of grazing and cattle was increased.
  • Confiscation of wealth from nobles and merchants was common.
  • Ban on liquor and gambling in Delhi.
Exam Tip: Alauddin Khalji's market control policy is one of the most frequently asked topics in BPSC and SSC exams. Remember the four markets, the Shahna-i-Mandi, and the 50% land revenue in the Doab.

Other Administrative Reforms

  • Standing army — the first Sultan to maintain a large standing army; soldiers were paid in cash.
  • Dagh (branding of horses) and Chehra (description of soldiers) — introduced to prevent fraud in the army.
  • Ban on social gatherings — nobles could not hold feasts or meetings without permission.
  • Ban on wine and gambling — moral reforms aimed at keeping the nobility disciplined.
  • Registration of nobles' marriages — to prevent political alliances through marriage.
  • Spy system strengthened — Barid and Munhiyan reported to the Sultan directly.
  • Confiscation of wealth — Alauddin confiscated the wealth of nobles to keep them dependent.

Alauddin's Architecture

  • Siri Fort (Delhi) — built to defend against the Mongols; also called Qila-i-Rai Pithora.
  • Alai Darwaza — the gateway to the Quwwat-ul-Islam Mosque at Delhi; famous for its true arch and beautiful calligraphy.
  • Jamait Khana Masjid (Nizam-ud-din, Delhi) — built by Alauddin; one of the earliest mosques with a proper dome.
  • Hauz Khas — a reservoir built by Alauddin; later developed by Firoz Shah Tughlaq.
  • Alai Minar — started by Alauddin; intended to be twice the height of the Qutb Minar but left incomplete.
  • Madrasa and Tomb of Alauddin Khalji — built near the Qutb Minar; his tomb was a simple structure.
Exam Tip: The Alai Darwaza at Delhi is famous for its true arch — one of the earliest in India. The Alai Minar was started by Alauddin but left incomplete after his death.

Amir Khusrau — The "Parrot of India"

Amir Khusrau (1253–1325 CE) was the greatest poet of the Khalji period and one of the most celebrated figures in Indian cultural history.

  • Was a disciple of the Sufi saint Nizam-ud-din Auliya.
  • Wrote in Persian and Hindavi (early Hindi).
  • Famous works: Khazain-ul-Futuh, Ashiqa, Tughlaq-nama, Qiran-us-Sadain.
  • Credited with the invention of the Tabla (or its popularisation) and Qawwali.
  • Called Tuti-i-Hind ("Parrot of India") for his eloquence.
  • Also known as the "Father of Urdu literature" for his Hindavi verses.

Decline of the Khalji Dynasty

  • Alauddin's death (1316 CE) — the immediate cause of decline.
  • Malik Kafur's regency — he became regent for the minor prince Shihab-ud-din Omar but was killed within a year.
  • Qutb-ud-din Mubarak Shah (1316–1320) — a weak ruler who abandoned Alauddin's strict policies; was murdered by his own general Khusrau Khan.
  • Khusrau Khan — the last Khalji ruler; a converted Hindu (Parwari) who ruled for a few months but was killed by Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq in 1320 CE.
  • The dynasty ended with the founding of the Tughlaq Dynasty.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • Bihar was an important province of the Delhi Sultanate during the Khalji period.
  • Alauddin Khalji appointed governors for Bihar — the region was part of the Bengal-Bihar administrative unit.
  • Bihar Sharif (Nalanda district) — continued as a major Sufi centre during the Khalji period; the Chishti order was very active here.
  • Maner Sharif (near Patna) — flourished as a Sufi centre; the tomb of Makhdoom Daulat was built later (Mughal period).
  • Alauddin's Deccan campaigns passed through central India — Bihar was not directly involved but remained an important rear base.
  • Mongol invasions affected northwest India including parts of the Punjab — Bihar was relatively safe.
BPSC Must-Know: Bihar remained a peaceful province under the Khaljis. Bihar Sharif emerged as a major Sufi centre in this period. The Chishti Sufi order spread across Bihar during the 13th–14th centuries.
