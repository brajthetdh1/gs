Emergency Provisions: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 14 min read · BPSC SSC

The Emergency Provisions are contained in Part XVIII (Articles 352 to 360) of the Indian Constitution. They empower the President to assume extraordinary powers in times of war, external aggression, armed rebellion or financial instability. The Constitution provides for three types of emergencies — National Emergency (Article 352), President's Rule or State Emergency (Article 356), and Financial Emergency (Article 360). The emergency provisions were borrowed from the Weimar Constitution of Germany. For BPSC and SSC, this is one of the most important topics — especially the three types of emergencies, the 44th Amendment changes, and the Bihar connection (the Emergency of 1975 and its impact on Bihar).

Three Types of Emergencies

TypeArticleGrounds
National EmergencyArticle 352War, external aggression, armed rebellion
President's Rule (State Emergency)Article 356Failure of constitutional machinery in a state
Financial EmergencyArticle 360Threat to the financial stability or credit of India
Exam Tip: The emergency provisions were borrowed from the Weimar Constitution of Germany. During an emergency, the federal structure of India becomes unitary — the Centre assumes extraordinary powers over the States.

National Emergency (Article 352)

Grounds

  • War
  • External aggression
  • Armed rebellion (added by the 44th Amendment, 1978 — replacing "internal disturbance")

Note: The President can proclaim a National Emergency even on the ground of "imminent danger" of war, external aggression or armed rebellion.

Proclamation

  • Proclaimed by the President — on the written recommendation of the Union Cabinet (44th Amendment, 1978).
  • The Cabinet must recommend in writing — this is a key safeguard introduced by the 44th Amendment.
  • The proclamation must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within one month — by a special majority (two-thirds of members present and voting + majority of total membership).
  • Once approved, the Emergency continues for 6 months — and can be extended indefinitely, with approval every 6 months.

Revocation

  • The President can revoke the Emergency at any time — no parliamentary approval needed.
  • Parliament can revoke it by a simple majority — a resolution of the Lok Sabha (with at least 1/10 of its members giving notice).
  • This provision was added by the 44th Amendment (1978).

Effects of National Emergency

Effect on Centre-State Relations

  • The Union can give directions to States on any matter.
  • Parliament can make laws on State List subjects.
  • The President can modify the distribution of revenues between the Centre and States.

Effect on Lok Sabha

  • The term of the Lok Sabha can be extended by one year at a time — but not beyond 6 months after the Emergency ends.
  • The same applies to State Legislative Assemblies.

Effect on Fundamental Rights

  • Article 358 — during National Emergency, the six freedoms under Article 19 are automatically suspended.
  • Article 359 — the President can suspend the right to move any court for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights (except Articles 20 and 21).
  • The 44th Amendment (1978) made Articles 20 and 21 non-suspendable — a major safeguard.
  • The 44th Amendment also provided that Article 358 applies only when the Emergency is declared on grounds of war or external aggression — not armed rebellion.

Note: National Emergency has been declared three times in India — in 1962, 1971, and 1975. The 1975 Emergency is the most controversial — it was declared on grounds of "internal disturbance" and led to widespread curtailment of civil liberties.

BPSC Must-Know: National Emergency has been declared three times in India: 1962 (Chinese aggression), 1971 (Indo-Pak War), and 1975 (internal disturbance). The 1975 Emergency was declared by Prime Minister Indira Gandhi and lasted 21 months — it was one of the most controversial periods in Indian democratic history.

President's Rule (Article 356)

Grounds

  • Failure of constitutional machinery in a state — the Governor's report or otherwise.
  • If the President is satisfied that the government of a state cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution.
  • The President can also assume any function of the state government — this is called "President's Rule".

Proclamation

  • Proclaimed by the President — on the recommendation of the Governor of the state (though not mandatory).
  • Must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within two months — by a simple majority.
  • Once approved, it continues for 6 months — extendable by 6 months at a time — up to a maximum of 3 years.
  • Beyond one year, it can be extended only if:
    • A National Emergency is in operation in the whole of India or in the state, AND
    • The Election Commission certifies that elections cannot be held in the state.

Effects of President's Rule

  • The President assumes the functions of the State Government.
  • The Governor administers the state on behalf of the President.
  • The State Legislature is either suspended or dissolved.
  • Parliament can make laws on State List subjects for that state.
  • The President can authorise expenditure from the State Consolidated Fund.
  • Fundamental Rights of the people of the state are not automatically suspended.

President's Rule — Facts

  • Used more than 125 times in India since 1950.
  • First use: Punjab in 1951 (during a period of instability).
  • Most frequent use: Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Manipur, Punjab.
  • The Sarkaria Commission (1988) recommended that Article 356 should be used sparingly — only as a last resort.
  • The S.R. Bommai case (1994) — the Supreme Court laid down guidelines:
    • The proclamation is subject to judicial review.
    • The President's satisfaction must be based on relevant material.
    • The floor test is the appropriate method to test majority.
    • The proclamation can be struck down if found mala fide.
Remember: President's Rule is imposed under Article 356. It must be approved by Parliament within 2 months by a simple majority. The S.R. Bommai case (1994) is the landmark judgment on Article 356 — it established that the proclamation is subject to judicial review.

Financial Emergency (Article 360)

Grounds

  • A situation has arisen whereby the financial stability or credit of India or of any part of its territory is threatened.

Proclamation

  • Proclaimed by the President.
  • Must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within two months — by a simple majority.
  • Once approved, it continues indefinitely — until revoked by the President.
  • No maximum duration is specified in the Constitution.

Effects of Financial Emergency

  • The Union can give directions to States to observe financial propriety.
  • The President can direct that all Money Bills passed by a State Legislature be reserved for his consideration.
  • The salaries and allowances of all government servants — including judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts — can be reduced.
  • The Union can reduce the salaries of its own employees.

Note: Financial Emergency has never been declared in India.

Exam Tip: Financial Emergency (Article 360) has never been declared in India. It must be approved by Parliament within 2 months by a simple majority. During a Financial Emergency, the salaries of judges can also be reduced — unlike in a National Emergency.

44th Amendment Act, 1978 — Safeguards Against Emergency

The 44th Amendment was passed after the traumatic experience of the 1975 Emergency. Its main objective was to prevent the misuse of emergency provisions.

Key Changes Introduced by the 44th Amendment

  • "Internal disturbance" was replaced by "armed rebellion" — a narrower and more specific ground.
  • The President can proclaim a National Emergency only on the written recommendation of the Union Cabinet.
  • The proclamation must be approved by both Houses of Parliament within one month — by a special majority.
  • Article 19 freedoms can be suspended only in the case of war or external aggression — not armed rebellion.
  • Articles 20 and 21 cannot be suspended during a National Emergency.
  • The Lok Sabha can revoke the Emergency by a simple majority — with 1/10 of its members giving notice.
  • The term of the Lok Sabha can be extended by only one year at a time — and not beyond 6 months after the Emergency ends.

Landmark Judgments on Emergency

ADM Jabalpur v. Shivkant Shukla (1976)

  • Also known as the Habeas Corpus Case.
  • Held during the 1975 Emergency — the Supreme Court ruled that during a National Emergency, a person's right to life and liberty could be suspended.
  • The Court held that no one could move a court for the enforcement of Articles 20 and 21 during an Emergency.
  • This judgment was widely criticised — it was effectively overruled by the 44th Amendment (1978).

Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)

  • Struck down parts of the 42nd Amendment that had given primacy to DPSP over Fundamental Rights.
  • Reaffirmed that the harmony between FR and DPSP is part of the Basic Structure.

S.R. Bommai v. Union of India (1994)

  • The most important judgment on President's Rule (Article 356).
  • Held that the President's satisfaction is subject to judicial review.
  • The proclamation can be struck down if it is based on irrelevant or mala fide grounds.
  • Introduced the doctrine of floor test to determine majority in a hung assembly.
  • Established that the proclamation is justiciable — the courts can examine it.

Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006)

  • Held that the dissolution of the Bihar Assembly by the Governor in 2005 was unconstitutional.
  • Reaffirmed that the Governor cannot dissolve an Assembly based on his subjective assessment of a "fractured mandate" without giving a chance to the government to prove its majority.
  • This is an important Bihar case — highly relevant for BPSC.
BPSC Must-Know: The Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006) case involves the dissolution of the Bihar Legislative Assembly by Governor Buta Singh in 2005. The Supreme Court held the dissolution to be unconstitutional — a landmark judgment on Article 356 and Bihar's political history.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

Emergency of 1975 and Bihar

  • The 1975 Emergency was declared by Prime Minister Indira Gandhi on grounds of "internal disturbance".
  • Bihar was one of the states most affected by the Emergency — with widespread arrests of political leaders, activists and students.
  • Jayaprakash Narayan (JP) — the socialist leader from Bihar (born in Sitabdiara, Saran district) — led the movement against the Emergency.
  • JP's movement (the JP Movement or Sampoorna Kranti) called for "Total Revolution" and became the focal point of opposition to the Emergency.
  • Thousands of JP supporters were arrested under the Maintenance of Internal Security Act (MISA) — a preventive detention law used during the Emergency.
  • The Emergency was lifted in 1977 — and the Janata Party came to power in the 1977 general elections.

President's Rule in Bihar

  • President's Rule has been imposed in Bihar multiple times — the state has been under Article 356 at various points in its political history.
  • 1968, 1977, 1980, 1995, 2000, 2005 — Bihar has been under President's Rule several times.
  • The 2005 President's Rule was challenged in the Supreme Court — leading to the landmark Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006) judgment.
  • In 2005, the Governor Buta Singh recommended dissolution of the Bihar Assembly — the Supreme Court later held this to be unconstitutional.

Bihar and the 44th Amendment

  • The 44th Amendment was a direct response to the Emergency excesses — many of which were felt most acutely in Bihar.
  • The Amendment strengthened safeguards against misuse of Article 356 — a benefit for states like Bihar that had seen frequent misuse of President's Rule.

Notable Bihar Leaders and the Emergency

  • Jayaprakash Narayan (JP) — born in Sitabdiara, Saran (Bihar) — led the anti-Emergency movement.
  • Lalu Prasad Yadav — a student leader during the JP Movement; later became Chief Minister of Bihar.
  • Nitish Kumar — also a student leader during the JP Movement; later became Chief Minister of Bihar.
  • Sushil Kumar Modi — a prominent Bihar politician influenced by the JP Movement.
  • Several current and former Bihar leaders were active in the JP Movement — which shaped the state's political direction for decades.
BPSC Must-Know: Jayaprakash Narayan (JP) — born in Sitabdiara, Saran (Bihar) — led the movement against the 1975 Emergency. The Rameshwar Prasad v. Union of India (2006) case — involving the dissolution of the Bihar Assembly in 2005 — is the most important Supreme Court judgment on Article 356. Both topics are essential for BPSC.
