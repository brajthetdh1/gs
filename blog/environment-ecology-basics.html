Ecology & Ecosystem — Basic Concepts: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 14 min read · BPSC SSC

Ecology is the study of the relationships between living organisms and their environment. An ecosystem is a community of living organisms interacting with each other and with the non-living components of their environment. These concepts form the foundation of the entire Environment section — for BPSC and SSC, questions on biotic and abiotic components, food chains, trophic levels, ecological pyramids, and biogeochemical cycles are extremely frequent. This post gives you a clean, exam-ready foundation.

What is Ecology?

Ecology — from the Greek words oikos (house) and logos (study) — is the study of the relationships between organisms and their environment.

Levels of Ecological Organisation

LevelDefinition
OrganismIndividual living being
PopulationGroup of individuals of the same species in a given area
CommunityAll populations of different species living together in a given area
EcosystemCommunity + its physical (abiotic) environment
BiomeLarge ecological area with similar climate and vegetation
BiosphereThe zone of life on Earth — land, water and atmosphere

Father of Ecology

  • Ernst Haeckel — German biologist — first coined the term "ecology" in 1869.
  • Eugene Odum — called the "Father of Modern Ecology".
  • Ramdeo Misra — called the "Father of Indian Ecology".
Exam Tip: The term "ecology" was first coined by Ernst Haeckel in 1869. Eugene Odum is called the Father of Modern Ecology. Ramdeo Misra is called the Father of Indian Ecology — remember this for BPSC.

What is an Ecosystem?

An ecosystem is a self-regulating, self-sustaining unit in which living organisms interact with each other and with the physical environment. The term was coined by Arthur Tansley in 1935.

Components of an Ecosystem

An ecosystem has two main components:

1. Abiotic Components (Non-living)

  • Physical factors — sunlight, temperature, rainfall, wind, humidity.
  • Chemical factors — soil, water, nutrients, pH, salinity.
  • Inorganic substances — carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulphur, etc.
  • Organic substances — proteins, carbohydrates, lipids — formed from dead organisms.

2. Biotic Components (Living)

CategoryDescriptionExamples
Producers (Autotrophs)Prepare their own food through photosynthesisGreen plants, algae, cyanobacteria
Consumers (Heterotrophs)Depend on others for foodHerbivores, carnivores, omnivores
Decomposers (Saprotrophs)Break down dead matterBacteria, fungi

Types of Consumers

  • Primary Consumers (Herbivores) — feed directly on producers — e.g. deer, rabbit, grasshopper.
  • Secondary Consumers (Primary Carnivores) — feed on herbivores — e.g. fox, frog, snake.
  • Tertiary Consumers (Top Carnivores) — feed on secondary consumers — e.g. lion, tiger, eagle.
  • Omnivores — feed on both plants and animals — e.g. human, bear, crow.
Remember: Producers — green plants. Consumers — animals. Decomposers — bacteria and fungi. The term "ecosystem" was coined by Arthur Tansley (1935). This is a frequently asked BPSC question.

Food Chain & Food Web

Food Chain

A food chain is a linear sequence of organisms through which energy and nutrients pass as one organism eats another.

Types of Food Chains

TypeDescriptionExample
Grazing Food Chain (GFC)Starts with producers (green plants)Grass → Deer → Tiger
Detritus Food Chain (DFC)Starts with dead organic matterDead leaves → Earthworm → Bird

In most ecosystems, the grazing food chain dominates in aquatic systems, while the detritus food chain dominates on land.

Food Web

A food web consists of interconnected food chains — reflecting the reality that most organisms feed on more than one type of prey, and are themselves eaten by more than one predator.

Trophic Levels

Trophic LevelCategoryExamples
First (T1)ProducersGreen plants
Second (T2)Primary Consumers (Herbivores)Deer, rabbit
Third (T3)Secondary ConsumersFox, frog
Fourth (T4)Tertiary Consumers (Top Carnivores)Tiger, eagle

10% Law (Lindeman's Law)

  • Proposed by Raymond Lindeman (1942).
  • Only about 10% of the energy at one trophic level is transferred to the next.
  • The remaining 90% is lost as heat, used in respiration, or remains in undigested matter.
  • Due to this energy loss, food chains are usually limited to 4–5 trophic levels.
Exam Tip: Only about 10% of energy is transferred from one trophic level to the next — this is Lindeman's 10% Law. This energy loss explains why food chains are short — usually 4–5 levels. This is one of the most frequently asked BPSC and SSC questions.

Ecological Pyramids

Ecological pyramids are graphical representations of the relationship between different trophic levels in an ecosystem.

Pyramid TypeRepresents
Pyramid of NumbersNumber of individuals at each trophic level
Pyramid of BiomassTotal biomass at each trophic level
Pyramid of EnergyEnergy content at each trophic level

Characteristics

  • The pyramid of energy is always upright — because energy decreases at each successive trophic level.
  • The pyramid of numbers can be upright or inverted — e.g. in a tree ecosystem, a single tree supports many herbivores (inverted).
  • The pyramid of biomass can also be inverted — e.g. in an ocean ecosystem, phytoplankton biomass is small but supports many consumers.

Biogeochemical Cycles

Biogeochemical cycles are the pathways through which chemical elements circulate between living organisms and the environment.

Gaseous Cycles

CycleKey Feature
Carbon CycleCarbon moves through photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, and combustion
Nitrogen CycleNitrogen fixation, nitrification, assimilation, ammonification, denitrification
Oxygen CyclePhotosynthesis releases O₂; respiration uses O₂

Sedimentary Cycles

CycleKey Feature
Phosphorus CycleNo gaseous phase; phosphorus moves from rocks → soil → organisms → back to rocks
Sulphur CycleMoves through rocks, water, soil and organisms

Nitrogen Cycle — Key Steps

  1. Nitrogen Fixation — conversion of atmospheric N₂ to ammonia by bacteria (Rhizobium, Azotobacter).
  2. Nitrification — conversion of ammonia to nitrites and nitrates by Nitrosomonas and Nitrobacter.
  3. Assimilation — plants absorb nitrates; animals consume plants.
  4. Ammonification — decomposition of organic matter releases ammonia.
  5. Denitrification — conversion of nitrates back to N₂ by Pseudomonas — returns nitrogen to the atmosphere.
BPSC Must-Know: The nitrogen cycle includes nitrogen fixation, nitrification, assimilation, ammonification and denitrification. Rhizobium (in legume root nodules) fixes nitrogen. Nitrosomonas and Nitrobacter are nitrifying bacteria. Pseudomonas is a denitrifying bacterium. This is a very high-yield topic.

Ecological Succession

Ecological succession is the gradual and directional change in the species composition of an ecological community over time.

Types of Succession

TypeDescription
Primary SuccessionOccurs in areas where no life existed before — e.g. bare rock, volcanic lava
Secondary SuccessionOccurs in areas where life existed before but was destroyed — e.g. abandoned farmland, burned forest

Stages of Succession

  1. Pioneer Stage — first organisms (lichens, mosses) colonise the bare area.
  2. Intermediate Stage — grasses, shrubs and small trees appear.
  3. Climax Stage — a stable, mature community (forest) establishes itself.

Hydrarch vs Xerarch Succession

  • Hydrarch Succession — starts in water; progressively becomes drier; ends in a mesic (moderate) condition.
  • Xerarch Succession — starts on dry land; progressively becomes wetter; ends in a mesic condition.
  • Both converge towards the same climax community.

Biomes of the World

A biome is a large ecological area with a distinct climate, vegetation, and animal life.

BiomeClimateKey Features
Tropical RainforestHot and humid year-roundHigh biodiversity; dense canopy; Amazon, Congo, Western Ghats
Tropical Deciduous ForestMonsoon climateSeasonal shedding of leaves; India's monsoon forests
Temperate ForestModerate climateOak, maple, beech; four distinct seasons
Taiga (Boreal Forest)Cold; long wintersConiferous trees — pine, fir, spruce
GrasslandModerate rainfallPrairies (USA), Steppes (Eurasia), Savanna (Africa), Pampas (S. America)
DesertVery dryCacti, succulents; Sahara, Thar, Gobi
TundraVery cold; permafrostMosses, lichens; Arctic regions
Aquatic—Freshwater (lakes, rivers), Marine (oceans, seas), Estuaries

Biomes of India

  • Tropical Rainforest — Western Ghats, Northeast India, Andaman & Nicobar Islands.
  • Tropical Deciduous Forest — most of India's forests (monsoon forests).
  • Thorn Forest — Rajasthan, Gujarat, parts of Madhya Pradesh.
  • Montane Forest — Himalayas (temperate, alpine).
  • Mangrove Forest — Sundarbans (West Bengal), Bhitarkanika (Odisha), Pichavaram (Tamil Nadu).
  • Grasslands — Banni grasslands (Gujarat), Kanha grasslands (MP).

Ecological Interactions

Positive Interactions

InteractionDescriptionExample
Mutualism (+/+)Both species benefitLichen (algae + fungi); Rhizobium and legumes
Commensalism (+/0)One benefits; other unaffectedBarnacles on whales; orchid on tree
Protocooperation (+/+)Both benefit but not obligatorySea anemone and hermit crab

Negative Interactions

InteractionDescriptionExample
Predation (+/-)One species kills and eats anotherLion and deer
Parasitism (+/-)Parasite benefits; host harmedCuscuta on plants; tapeworm in humans
Competition (-/-)Both species harmedTwo species competing for same resource
Amensalism (-/0)One harmed; other unaffectedPenicillin killing bacteria; shading of small plants by large trees
AllelopathyOne plant releases chemicals harming anotherParthenium (Congress grass) suppressing other plants

Ecosystem Services

Ecosystem services are the benefits that humans derive from ecosystems. They are classified into:

TypeDescriptionExamples
ProvisioningProducts obtained from ecosystemsFood, water, timber, fibre, medicines
RegulatingBenefits obtained by regulating ecosystem processesClimate regulation, flood control, water purification
CulturalNon-material benefitsRecreation, tourism, spiritual values, education
SupportingServices necessary for other servicesNutrient cycling, soil formation, primary production

Ecological Footprint

  • The ecological footprint measures the demand humans place on nature — in terms of land and water required to produce the resources they consume and absorb the waste they generate.
  • Measured in global hectares (gha).
  • If the ecological footprint exceeds the biocapacity of the region, it results in ecological overshoot.
  • The Earth is currently in ecological overshoot — we consume more than the planet can regenerate.
  • Earth Overshoot Day — the date each year when humanity's demand exceeds what the Earth can regenerate in that year.
Exam Tip: The ecological footprint is measured in global hectares. When the footprint exceeds biocapacity, it results in ecological overshoot. The date when human demand exceeds Earth's regeneration is called Earth Overshoot Day. This is a frequently asked question in BPSC and SSC exams.

Habitat & Niche

  • Habitat — the physical place where an organism lives.
  • Ecological Niche — the role and position of a species in its ecosystem — including its interactions with other species and its use of resources. Called the "profession" of an organism (while habitat is its "address").
  • Two species cannot occupy the same niche indefinitely — this is the Competitive Exclusion Principle (Gause's Law).

Key Terminology

TermMeaning
Biotic CommunityAll living organisms in a given area
BiotopeThe physical (non-living) part of an ecosystem
Standing CropTotal biomass of organisms at a given time
Primary ProductivityRate at which producers convert solar energy into biomass
Secondary ProductivityRate at which consumers convert food into biomass
Keystone SpeciesSpecies with disproportionately large effect on ecosystem — e.g. tiger, elephant, fig tree
Indicator SpeciesSpecies that indicate environmental conditions — e.g. lichens indicate air quality
Invasive SpeciesNon-native species that harm the ecosystem — e.g. Lantana, Water Hyacinth, Parthenium
Endemic SpeciesSpecies found only in a particular region — e.g. Nilgiri Tahr, Lion-tailed Macaque
Flagship SpeciesCharismatic species used to promote conservation — e.g. Tiger, Panda

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

Bihar's Ecosystems

  • Bihar is predominantly an agro-ecosystem — with a heavily cultivated landscape.
  • Forest cover in Bihar is low — around 7.8% of the state's geographical area (well below the national target of 33%).
  • Bihar's natural ecosystems include:
    • Gangetic plains — fertile alluvial land.
    • Terai region — along the Nepal border — moist deciduous forests.
    • Kaimur Hills — dry deciduous forests.
    • Vikramshila Gangetic Dolphin Sanctuary — the only sanctuary for the Gangetic Dolphin — a keystone species.
    • Kanwar Lake (Kabartal) — a wetland of international importance (Ramsar site).
    • Udaipur Lake (West Champaran) — important wetland.
    • Gogabeel — oxbow lake in Bhagalpur and Katihar districts.
  • Bihar's forests are found mainly in the southern districts — Kaimur, Rohtas, Gaya, Nawada, Jamui, Munger, Banka.

Bihar's Protected Areas

  • Valmiki National Park and Tiger Reserve — West Champaran district; the only National Park and Tiger Reserve in Bihar.
  • Vikramshila Gangetic Dolphin Sanctuary — Bhagalpur; the only sanctuary dedicated to the Gangetic Dolphin.
  • Kanwar Lake Bird Sanctuary (Kabartal) — Begusarai; a Ramsar site.
  • Bhimbandh Wildlife Sanctuary — Munger district.
  • Rajgir Wildlife Sanctuary — Nalanda district.
  • Nagi Dam and Nakti Dam Bird Sanctuaries — Jamui district.
  • Kaimur Wildlife Sanctuary — Kaimur district — Bihar's largest sanctuary.
  • Gautam Buddha Wildlife Sanctuary — Gaya district.
  • Pant Wildlife Sanctuary — Nalanda district.
  • Udaipur Wildlife Sanctuary — West Champaran.

Bihar's Flora and Fauna

  • State Animal — Gaur (Indian Bison).
  • State Bird — House Sparrow.
  • State Tree — Peepal.
  • State Flower — Kachnar.
  • Key species — Gangetic Dolphin, Tiger, Leopard, Sloth Bear, Wild Boar, Sambar, Chital, Nilgai, Indian Porcupine.
  • Gangetic Dolphin — declared the National Aquatic Animal of India in 2009.
BPSC Must-Know: Valmiki National Park and Tiger Reserve (West Champaran) is Bihar's only National Park. Vikramshila Gangetic Dolphin Sanctuary (Bhagalpur) is the only sanctuary for the Gangetic Dolphin — India's National Aquatic Animal. Kanwar Lake (Kabartal) is Bihar's only Ramsar site. Gangetic Dolphin was declared the National Aquatic Animal in 2009. These are among the most frequently asked BPSC Environment questions.
