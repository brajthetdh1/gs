Panchayati Raj & Local Self-Government: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 14 min read · BPSC SSC

Panchayati Raj is the system of local self-government in rural India. It was given constitutional status by the 73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992, which added Part IX (Articles 243 to 243O) and the Eleventh Schedule (29 subjects) to the Constitution. For urban areas, the 74th Amendment Act, 1992 added Part IX-A and the Twelfth Schedule (18 subjects). For BPSC and SSC, this is one of the highest-yield topics — especially the three-tier structure, Gram Sabha, reservation for women, and Bihar's pioneering role in Panchayati Raj.

Historical Background of Panchayati Raj

  • Village panchayats have existed in India since ancient times — mentioned in Rigveda and Arthashastra.
  • During British rule, Lord Ripon (1882) is called the "Father of Local Self-Government in India" for his resolution on local self-government.
  • The Government of India Act, 1919 made local self-government a transferred subject.
  • The Government of India Act, 1935 gave provinces more autonomy over local bodies.
  • Mahatma Gandhi advocated Gram Swaraj — village self-rule — as the foundation of Indian democracy.

73rd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992

The most important amendment for rural local government. It came into force on 24 April 1993.

Key Features

  • Added Part IX (Articles 243 to 243O) to the Constitution.
  • Added the Eleventh Schedule — with 29 subjects for Panchayats.
  • Made Panchayati Raj a constitutional obligation for all states (except Nagaland, Meghalaya, Mizoram and certain other areas).
  • Provided for a three-tier structure — village, intermediate and district levels.
  • Mandated regular elections every 5 years.
  • Provided for reservation of seats for SCs and STs in proportion to population.
  • Provided for reservation of not less than one-third of seats for women.
  • Set up a State Election Commission to conduct panchayat elections.
  • Set up a State Finance Commission every 5 years to review panchayat finances.
  • Provided for a Gram Sabha at the village level.
  • Established minimum age of 21 years for contesting panchayat elections.

Three-Tier Structure

LevelNameComposition
Village LevelGram PanchayatElected members from wards; headed by Sarpanch (Mukhiya)
Block / Intermediate LevelPanchayat Samiti (Mandal)Elected members from Gram Panchayats; headed by Pramukh
District LevelZila ParishadElected members from Panchayat Samitis; headed by Adhyaksh

Note: States with a population of less than 20 lakh may not have the intermediate level. The three-tier structure is mandatory for larger states.

Bihar's Panchayati Raj Structure

  • Gram Panchayat — at the village level; headed by Mukhiya.
  • Panchayat Samiti — at the block level; headed by Pramukh.
  • Zila Parishad — at the district level; headed by Adhyaksh.
Remember: The three-tier structure is: Gram Panchayat → Panchayat Samiti → Zila Parishad. In Bihar, the Gram Panchayat is headed by the Mukhiya, the Panchayat Samiti by the Pramukh, and the Zila Parishad by the Adhyaksh.

Gram Sabha — Article 243A

The Gram Sabha is the foundation of the Panchayati Raj system.

  • Consists of all persons registered in the electoral rolls of a village.
  • It is a body of all voters — not an elected body.
  • It exercises powers and performs functions as provided by the State Legislature.
  • Approves the annual budget of the Gram Panchayat.
  • Reviews the accounts of the Gram Panchayat.
  • Discusses development plans for the village.
  • Can question the elected representatives — the Mukhiya and ward members.
  • It is the only direct democracy institution in India at the grassroots level.

Bihar: Bihar was the first state to implement 50% reservation for women in Panchayati Raj institutions (2006). This is a landmark achievement in women's political empowerment.

BPSC Must-Know: The 73rd Amendment mandates 1/3 reservation for women in Panchayati Raj. However, Bihar was the first state to increase this to 50% — in 2006. This makes Bihar a pioneer in women's political empowerment at the grassroots level. This is one of the most important BPSC facts.

PESA Act, 1996

The Panchayats (Extension to Scheduled Areas) Act, 1996 — known as the PESA Act — extends Panchayati Raj to the Fifth Schedule areas (predominantly tribal regions).

Key Features

  • Applies to Fifth Schedule areas in 10 states: Andhra Pradesh, Chhattisgarh, Gujarat, Himachal Pradesh, Jharkhand, Madhya Pradesh, Maharashtra, Odisha, Rajasthan and Telangana.
  • Gives special powers to the Gram Sabha — including the power to:
    • Approve plans and programmes for the village.
    • Manage minor minerals.
    • Prevent land alienation in Scheduled Areas.
    • Manage minor forest produce.
    • Control money-lending.
    • Manage intoxicants.
  • Ensures tribal representation in Panchayats at all levels.
  • The Gram Sabha's recommendation is mandatory before granting prospecting licenses for minor minerals.
  • Reservation for STs in proportion to their population — and for women.
  • Protects the customary laws and traditions of tribal communities.
Exam Tip: The PESA Act, 1996 extends Panchayati Raj to the Fifth Schedule areas — giving special powers to the Gram Sabha. It is a landmark in tribal self-governance. This is a frequent BPSC and SSC question.

State Election Commission & State Finance Commission

State Election Commission

  • Established under Article 243K.
  • Conducts elections to Panchayats and Municipalities.
  • Consists of a State Election Commissioner — appointed by the Governor.
  • The Commissioner can be removed only by the President — on the same grounds as a High Court judge.
  • Ensures free and fair elections to local bodies.

State Finance Commission

  • Established under Article 243I.
  • Constituted by the Governor every 5 years.
  • Reviews the financial position of Panchayats and Municipalities.
  • Recommends the principles governing:
    • Distribution of taxes between the state and local bodies.
    • Assignment of taxes to local bodies.
    • Grants-in-aid from the state to local bodies.
  • Recommendations are advisory — not binding.

74th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1992

The 74th Amendment gave constitutional status to urban local bodies (municipalities). It came into force on 1 June 1993.

Key Features

  • Added Part IX-A (Articles 243P to 243ZG) to the Constitution.
  • Added the Twelfth Schedule — with 18 subjects for Municipalities.
  • Provided for three types of Municipalities:
    • Nagar Panchayat — for transitional areas (rural to urban).
    • Municipal Council — for smaller urban areas.
    • Municipal Corporation — for larger urban areas.
  • Mandated regular elections every 5 years.
  • Provided for reservation of seats for SCs and STs in proportion to population.
  • Provided for 1/3 reservation for women.
  • Set up State Election Commission and State Finance Commission for urban bodies.
  • Provided for Wards Committees in larger municipal areas.

Twelfth Schedule — 18 Subjects

Key subjects include: Urban planning, regulation of land use, water supply, fire services, urban forestry, slum improvement, urban poverty alleviation, public health, sanitation, public amenities, burial grounds, cattle pounds, and regulation of slaughterhouses.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

Bihar — Pioneer in Panchayati Raj

  • Bihar was the first state to implement 50% reservation for women in Panchayati Raj institutions (2006) — going beyond the 73rd Amendment's mandate of 1/3.
  • Bihar also implemented the Panchayati Raj system early — in 1961 at Bagha (West Champaran) on an experimental basis.
  • The Bihar Panchayat Raj Act, 1947 was one of the earliest panchayat laws in India.
  • Bihar's Panchayati Raj structure:
    • Gram Panchayat — headed by Mukhiya.
    • Panchayat Samiti — headed by Pramukh.
    • Zila Parishad — headed by Adhyaksh.
  • Gram Sabha in Bihar includes all registered voters of a village — and plays a key role in approving budgets and reviewing accounts.
  • Bihar has implemented several rural development schemes through Panchayati Raj institutions — including MGNREGA, PMAY-G, and Swachh Bharat Mission.

Bihar's Landmark Reforms

  • 50% reservation for women in Panchayati Raj (2006) — Bihar was the first state in India to do so.
  • Reservation for OBCs and EBCs in Panchayati Raj — implemented along with the Karpoori Thakur model of social justice.
  • Bihar Panchayati Raj Act, 2006 — consolidated the modern panchayat system in the state.
  • Bihar has one of the largest number of panchayats in India — with over 8,000 Gram Panchayats.
  • E-Governance in Panchayats — Bihar has introduced digital platforms for panchayat administration and transparency.

Notable Initiatives

  • Jeevika — Bihar Rural Livelihoods Project, working with women's self-help groups through Panchayats.
  • Mukhyamantri Gram Parivahan Yojana — rural transport scheme implemented through Panchayats.
  • Bihar Panchayat Election — conducted by the State Election Commission; the 2021 panchayat elections were conducted smoothly.
  • Panchayat Sarkar Bhawan — Bihar has built modern Panchayat Bhawans to house Gram Panchayat offices.
BPSC Must-Know: Bihar was the first state in India to implement 50% reservation for women in Panchayati Raj institutions (2006). This is one of the most important Bihar-related facts for BPSC. Bihar also implemented the Panchayati Raj system early — in 1961 at Bagha (West Champaran) on an experimental basis.

Significance of Panchayati Raj

  • Grassroots democracy — brings governance to the village level.
  • Political empowerment — of women, SCs, STs, and OBCs.
  • Decentralisation — of power and resources.
  • Local participation — in planning and development.
  • Accountability — of elected representatives to the Gram Sabha.
  • Rural development — through schemes and programmes.
  • Social justice — through reservation and welfare programmes.
  • Capacity building — of local leadership.
  • Better implementation of central and state schemes.

Challenges of Panchayati Raj

  • Inadequate devolution of powers — many states have not transferred all 29 subjects.
  • Financial dependency — on state and central grants.
  • Lack of capacity — in terms of staff, training and infrastructure.
  • Elite capture — powerful local elites dominate many panchayats.
  • Sarpanch-pati / Mukhiya-pati — the phenomenon where elected women are proxies for their husbands or male relatives.
  • Political interference — from state and central governments.
  • Corruption — in the implementation of schemes.
  • Inadequate representation — of the most marginalised sections.
  • Delays in elections — some states have postponed panchayat elections.
  • Lack of awareness — among voters and elected representatives.
