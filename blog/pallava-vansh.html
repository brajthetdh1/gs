Pallava Vansh: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 12 min read · BPSC SSC

The Pallava Dynasty (c. 275 – 897 CE) ruled the Tondaimandalam region of South India with their capital at Kanchipuram. They were the great rivals of the Chalukyas of Badami and the pioneers of Dravidian temple architecture. Their rock-cut monuments at Mahabalipuram and structural temples at Kanchipuram — both UNESCO World Heritage Sites — remain the finest examples of early South Indian art. For BPSC and SSC, the Pallavas are essential for questions on art, architecture and South Indian history.

Exam Tip: The Mandagapattu Inscription of Mahendravarman I is a landmark record — it states that the king (called Vichitrachitta) built a temple for Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva without using brick, timber, metal, or mortar. This is often asked as the earliest evidence of Pallava rock-cut architecture.

Origin & Early Pallavas

  • The Pallavas are mentioned in Sangam literature and in Ashokan inscriptions.
  • The name "Pallava" is believed to mean "creeper", but some historians link it to Pahlava (Persian) origins.
  • Some theories suggest they were Brahmins from Andhra who rose to power after the decline of the Satavahanas.
  • Their early capital was Kanchipuram (Tamil Nadu) — also called Kanchi, one of the seven sacred cities of India.
  • Their territory was called Tondaimandalam — the region between the Krishna and Kaveri rivers.
  • Early rulers: Simhavarman, Skandavarman, Budhavarman — not much is known about their reigns.

Simhavishnu (c. 555–590 CE) — Revival

The real founder of Pallava greatness. He revived the dynasty's power and extended territory up to the Kaveri river.

  • Titles: Avanisimha (Lion of the Earth), Simhavishnu.
  • Defeated the Kalabhras, who had disrupted South Indian politics for centuries.
  • Defeated the Cholas, Pandyas and the rulers of Malaya (Kerala).
  • Patronised Sanskrit and Tamil literature.

Mahendravarman I (c. 600–630 CE)

Son of Simhavishnu; a great scholar, musician and temple-builder — but his reign was overshadowed by conflict with the Chalukyas.

  • Titles: Mahendravikramavarman, Gunabhara, Mattavilasa, Chetthakari, Vichitrachitta.
  • Was initially a Jain, but converted to Shaivism under the influence of the saint Appar (Tirunavukkarasar).
  • Author of the Sanskrit farce Mattavilasa Prahasana and the play Bhagavadajjuka.
  • Patronised music — credited with developing the Kudumiyamalai musical inscription.
  • Defeated by Pulakeshin II (Chalukya) at the Battle of Pullalur (618 CE) — a major setback.
  • Built several rock-cut temples in the Mahendra style — including the famous Mandagapattu cave temple.
Remember: The Battle of Pullalur (618 CE) saw the Chalukya king Pulakeshin II defeat Mahendravarman I. This began a long Pallava–Chalukya rivalry that lasted for over two centuries.

Narasimhavarman I Mamalla (630–668 CE)

The greatest Pallava ruler — a warrior, a patron of the arts, and the hero who avenged his father's defeat.

  • Titles: Mamalla ("great wrestler"), Vatapikonda ("conqueror of Vatapi"), Mahamalla.
  • Defeated and killed Pulakeshin II in 642 CE, and captured the Chalukyan capital Vatapi (Badami) — hence the title Vatapikonda.
  • Sent a naval expedition to Sri Lanka to restore the Sinhalese prince Manavarman to the throne.
  • Hiuen Tsang (Xuanzang) visited Kanchipuram during his reign and praised it as a great centre of learning.
  • Built the famous Mamallapuram (Mahabalipuram) monuments — the Pancha Rathas, Arjuna's Penance, and several cave temples.
  • Called Mamallan in Tamil tradition — the "great warrior."
Exam Tip: Narasimhavarman I's title Vatapikonda is one of the most frequently asked Pallava facts. The capture of Vatapi (Badami) in 642 CE was the high point of Pallava power.

Later Pallava Rulers

Mahendravarman II (668–670 CE)

Short and uneventful reign. The Chalukyas under Vikramaditya I invaded Pallava territory during this period.

Paramesvaravarman I (670–700 CE)

  • Defeated the Chalukyas under Vikramaditya I at the Battle of Peruvalanallur.
  • Took the title Ugradanda.
  • Consolidated Pallava power in the south.

Narasimhavarman II Rajasimha (700–728 CE)

The greatest Pallava temple-builder — his reign is called the golden age of Pallava architecture.

  • Built the Shore Temple at Mahabalipuram — the earliest structural stone temple in South India.
  • Built the Kailasanatha Temple at Kanchipuram — the finest example of the Rajasimha style of architecture.
  • Sent ambassadors to China — evidence of Pallava maritime and diplomatic reach.
  • Patronised the Sanskrit scholar Dandin, author of Dashakumaracharita and Kavyadarsha.
  • Titles: Rajasimha, Narasimhavarman, Atyantakama, Kailasanatha.

Paramesvaravarman II (728–731 CE)

Weak ruler. His reign saw the Chalukyan king Vikramaditya II invade Kanchipuram — but the Chalukyan king, impressed by the beauty of the Kailasanatha Temple, spared it and left a commemorative inscription.

Nandivarman II Pallavamalla (731–796 CE)

  • Was a young prince from the Kadamba or Ganga family, elected by the ministers — a rare instance of an adopted heir.
  • Founded the Vaikuntha Perumal Temple at Kanchipuram — an important Vaishnava temple with historical relief panels.
  • Faced invasions from the Pandyas and Rashtrakutas (under Dantidurga and Krishna I).
  • Patronised Tamil literature and the Vaishnava saint Tirumangai Alvar.

Dantivarman (796–847 CE)

  • A weak ruler — Pallava power declined significantly.
  • Lost territory to the Pandyas under Varaguna I and the Rashtrakutas.
  • His reign marks the beginning of the end of Pallava supremacy.

Nandivarman III (846–869 CE)

  • Attempted to revive Pallava power with the help of the Western Gangas.
  • Defeated the Pandyas at the Battle of Sripurambiyam (c. 880 CE) alongside the Cholas.
  • Titles: Tellarerinda Nandivarman ("Nandivarman who knew the truth").

Aparajitavarman (c. 880–897 CE) — Last Ruler

  • The last great Pallava ruler.
  • Fought against the Pandyas and the rising Cholas under Aditya I.
  • Was defeated and killed by Aditya I Chola around 897 CE, ending Pallava rule.

Pallava Administration

  • King was the head of state; assisted by a council of ministers.
  • Empire divided into Rashtras (provinces) → Vishayas (districts) → Nadus → Villages.
  • Rashtrikas governed provinces; Vishayapatis governed districts.
  • Village assemblies — similar to the Chola period — managed local affairs.
  • Land revenue was the main income — usually 1/6 to 1/10 of produce.
  • Land grants to Brahmins (Brahmadeya) and temples (Devadana) became common.
  • Ayudhajivis (soldier-agriculturists) and Sabha (assembly) are mentioned in inscriptions.

Pallava Art & Architecture (Most Important Section)

The Pallavas were the pioneers of Dravidian temple architecture. Their architecture evolved through four phases, each named after a great ruler.

Phase 1 — Mahendra Style (c. 600–630 CE)

  • Rock-cut cave temples — excavated from living rock.
  • Pillars with lotus-capital and corbel brackets.
  • Key examples: Mandagapattu, Dalavanur, Pallavaram, Vallam.
  • The Mandagapattu inscription records Mahendravarman I's boast of building "without brick, timber, metal or mortar."

Phase 2 — Mamalla Style (c. 630–700 CE)

  • Monolithic rathas — temples carved out of single rocks (like chariots).
  • Famous Pancha Rathas at Mahabalipuram — named after the Pandavas: Dharmaraja Ratha, Bhima Ratha, Arjuna Ratha, Nakula-Sahadeva Ratha, Draupadi Ratha.
  • Arjuna's Penance (Descent of the Ganges) — the largest open-air bas-relief in the world.
  • Govardhanadhari and Krishna Mandapa — famous mandapas.
  • Mamallapuram (Mahabalipuram) — a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
Exam Tip: Mahabalipuram (Mamallapuram) is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Its Pancha Rathas are monoliths named after the Pandavas. Arjuna's Penance is the largest open-air bas-relief in the world — frequently asked in BPSC and SSC exams.

Phase 3 — Rajasimha Style (c. 700–730 CE)

  • Structural stone temples — built from cut stone blocks, not rock-cut.
  • Shore Temple at Mahabalipuram — built by Narasimhavarman II Rajasimha; the earliest structural stone temple in South India.
  • Kailasanatha Temple at Kanchipuram — the finest example of Pallava architecture; known for its intricate sculptures and frescoes.
  • Introduction of the gopuram (entrance gateway) and prakara (enclosure wall).
  • Emphasis on the vimana (tower over the sanctum).

Phase 4 — Nandivarman Style (c. 730–900 CE)

  • Continued structural temple building with more elaborate decoration.
  • Vaikuntha Perumal Temple at Kanchipuram — built by Nandivarman II Pallavamalla; famous for its historical relief panels depicting Pallava dynastic history.
  • Later Pallava temples influenced Chola and Vijayanagara architecture.

Pallava Sculpture & Other Arts

  • Pallava bronzes — though fewer than Chola bronzes, they are exquisite.
  • Pallava Grantha script — ancestral to many Southeast Asian scripts (Thai, Javanese, Khmer, Burmese).
  • Music — the Kudumiyamalai inscription of Mahendravarman I is one of the earliest records of Indian musical notation.
  • Painting — traces of Pallava paintings survive at the Kailasanatha Temple, Kanchipuram.

Literature & Religion

  • Bharavi — author of Kiratarjuniya, a Sanskrit mahakavya; associated with the Pallava court.
  • Dandin — author of Dashakumaracharita and Kavyadarsha; patronised by Rajasimha.
  • Mahendravarman I — author of Mattavilasa Prahasana (a Sanskrit farce) and Bhagavadajjuka.
  • Kanchipuram — a great centre of Sanskrit and Tamil learning; Hiuen Tsang studied there.
  • Religion: initially Jainism and Buddhism; gradually became Shaiva, though Vaishnavism also flourished.
  • The Alvars and Nayanars (Bhakti saints) were active during this period.
  • Appar (Tirunavukkarasar) converted Mahendravarman I from Jainism to Shaivism.

Pallava–Chalukya Conflict

The rivalry between the Pallavas and the Chalukyas of Badami was one of the longest and most consequential in South Indian history.

Decline of the Pallava Empire

  • Constant wars with the Chalukyas exhausted the treasury and army.
  • Rise of the Pandyas in the far south — they emerged as a major power.
  • Rise of the Rashtrakutas — they invaded Pallava territory from the Deccan.
  • Rise of the Cholas — the most decisive factor. Aditya I Chola gradually annexed Pallava territory.
  • Weak successors after Nandivarman III — no able ruler could hold the empire together.
  • Aparajitavarman, the last Pallava ruler, was defeated and killed by Aditya I Chola around 897 CE, ending Pallava rule.
