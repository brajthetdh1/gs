Gupta Vansh: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 14 min read · BPSC SSC

The Gupta Empire (c. 319 – 550 CE) is called the "Golden Age of India" because of its remarkable achievements in art, literature, science, and administration. Founded by Sri Gupta and consolidated by Chandragupta I, the empire reached its peak under Samudragupta and Chandragupta II Vikramaditya. For BPSC, the Gupta–Bihar connection (Nalanda, Pataliputra, Bodh Gaya) is extremely important.

Exam Tip: The Prayag Prashasti (Allahabad Pillar Inscription) composed by Harisena is the single most important source for Samudragupta's reign. It is written in Chaste Sanskrit using the Brahmi script.

Gupta Rulers — Chronology

1. Sri Gupta (c. 240–280 CE)

Founder of the Gupta dynasty. Ruled a small principality around Magadha (Bihar). Known as Maharaja — a title suggesting a feudatory or a minor ruler.

2. Ghatotkacha (c. 280–319 CE)

Son of Sri Gupta. Also called Maharaja. Not much is known about his reign, but he maintained the family's position in Magadha.

3. Chandragupta I (319–335 CE)

The first great ruler of the dynasty and the real founder of the Gupta Empire.

  • Assumed the title Maharajadhiraja — the first Gupta ruler to do so.
  • Married Kumaradevi, a Licchavi princess — this alliance strengthened his position in north Bihar and Nepal.
  • Issued gold coins depicting himself and Kumaradevi — the famous Chandragupta–Kumaradevi type coins.
  • Started the Gupta Era in 319–320 CE (also called the Gupta Samvat).
  • Capital: Pataliputra (Patna, Bihar).
Remember: The Gupta Era began in 319–320 CE. The Licchavi marriage gave Chandragupta I legitimacy and territory in Bihar — a fact BPSC loves to ask.

4. Samudragupta (335–380 CE)

Called the "Napoleon of India" by historian V.A. Smith for his extensive military conquests.

  • His conquests are recorded in the Allahabad Pillar Inscription (Prayag Prashasti) by Harisena.
  • Conquered over 12 rulers of South India — known as Dharmavijaya (conquest for righteousness).
  • Defeated kingdoms from the Himalayas to the Narmada, and up to the Deccan.
  • Performed the Ashvamedha Yajna — depicted on his coins.
  • Titles: Maharajadhiraja, Parakramanka, Vikramanka.
  • Patron of Hinduism, but tolerant of Buddhism; tradition holds he was a poet and musician (called Kaviraja).
  • Issued gold coins of many types — Archer, Tiger-slayer, Ashvamedha, Lyrist.

5. Chandragupta II Vikramaditya (380–415 CE)

The greatest Gupta ruler — his reign is considered the true "Golden Age".

  • Titles: Vikramaditya, Chandragupta, Devagupta, Devashri.
  • Defeated the Shakas of western India — annexed Gujarat, Malwa and Saurashtra.
  • Moved the capital (or a second capital) to Ujjain for administrative convenience.
  • Married his daughter Prabhavatigupta to Rudrasena II of the Vakataka dynasty — a strategic alliance.
  • His court had the Navratnas (Nine Gems) — including Kalidasa, Amarasimha, Varahamihira, Dhanvantari, and Kshapanaka.
  • The Mehrauli Iron Pillar (Delhi) carries an inscription attributed to him — famous for its rust-resistant metallurgy.
  • Fa-Hien, the Chinese pilgrim, visited India during his reign (399–414 CE).
Exam Tip: Navratnas of Chandragupta II — Kalidasa, Dhanvantari, Kshapanaka, Amarasimha, Shanku, Vetalabhatta, Ghatakarpara, Varahamihira, Vararuchi. This list is often asked directly.

6. Kumaragupta I (415–455 CE)

  • Son of Chandragupta II; titles Mahendraditya and Shakraditya.
  • Founded the Nalanda Mahavihara (Nalanda University) around 427 CE — a landmark event in Indian education history.
  • Performed the Ashvamedha Yajna.
  • Successfully repelled early Pushyamitra (not the Shunga king, a tribal chief) and minor Huna incursions.
  • Issued a large number of gold, silver and copper coins.

7. Skandagupta (455–467 CE)

  • The last great Gupta ruler.
  • Famous for repelling the Huna (Hephthalite) invasions from Central Asia.
  • Took titles Vikramaditya and Kramaditya.
  • The Bhitari Pillar Inscription records his victories over the Hunas.
  • His death marked the beginning of rapid decline of the empire.

Later Guptas

After Skandagupta, the empire fragmented. Rulers like Purugupta, Narasimhagupta, Kumaragupta II and Vishnugupta could not hold the empire together. By c. 550 CE, the Gupta Empire had collapsed into smaller kingdoms.

Gupta Administration

  • Centralised but decentralised in practice — the king was the head, but provinces were governed by Kumaramatyas and Uparikas.
  • Mantriparishad — council of ministers advised the king.
  • Provincial governors were often princes (Kumaras).
  • Land grants to Brahmins and officials became common — a sign of decentralisation.
  • Vishayapati — district head; Gramika — village head.
  • Judicial system: king was the highest court of appeal; punishments were generally mild.
  • Revenue: 1/4 to 1/6 of produce as land tax; additional taxes on trade, crafts, and irrigation.

Economy

  • Agriculture — the backbone, with state-supported irrigation.
  • Gold coins (Dinara), silver (Rupaka) and copper (Karshapana) issued — the purity of Gupta gold coins was legendary.
  • Trade flourished with the Roman Empire, Southeast Asia, and China.
  • Guilds (Shrenis) controlled crafts and trade.
  • Silk, cotton, ivory, spices were major exports.
  • Sea ports: Tamralipti (Bengal), Broach, Cambay.

Art, Architecture & Literature (The Golden Age)

Architecture & Sculpture

  • Dashavatara Temple, Deogarh — the finest example of Gupta temple architecture (early Nagara style).
  • Mahabodhi Temple at Bodh Gaya (Bihar) — reached its present form during the Gupta period.
  • Nalanda Mahavihara (Bihar) — founded by Kumaragupta I; became the world's greatest centre of learning.
  • Bhitargaon Temple (UP) — earliest surviving brick temple in India.
  • Sarnath Buddha — the famous Dharmachakra Pravartana Buddha image.
  • Ajanta Caves — paintings of the Gupta period (Caves 16, 17, 19).

Literature

  • Kalidasa — Abhijnanashakuntalam, Meghaduta, Raghuvamsha, Kumarasambhava, Vikramorvashiyam, Malavikagnimitram, Ritusamhara.
  • Vishakhadatta — Mudrarakshasa, Devichandraguptam.
  • Shudraka — Mrichchhakatika (The Little Clay Cart).
  • Bhasa — early Sanskrit dramatist.
  • Amarasimha — Amarakosha, the first Sanskrit dictionary.
  • Harisena — Prayag Prashasti.

Exam Tip: Aryabhata wrote the Aryabhatiya and Aryabhatiya-bhashya. He was the first to explain that the earth rotates on its axis and that eclipses are caused by shadows — not by demons (Rahu-Ketu).

Religion

  • Gupta rulers were primarily Vaishnavas (Hindu) but were tolerant of all faiths.
  • Buddhism — Mahayana Buddhism flourished; Nalanda became its greatest centre.
  • Jainism — continued to receive patronage.
  • Bhagavad Gita and the Puranas were compiled in their present form during this period.
  • Temple worship and Bhakti grew — image worship became common.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • Pataliputra (Patna) was the Gupta capital — the political and cultural heart of the empire.
  • Nalanda Mahavihara — founded by Kumaragupta I around 427 CE in present-day Nalanda district, Bihar. It was the world's first residential university.
  • Mahabodhi Temple, Bodh Gaya — rebuilt and expanded during the Gupta period.
  • Bihar Sharif (Nalanda district) — an important Gupta-era town.
  • Gupta gold coins have been discovered at several sites in Bihar — proof of prosperity.
  • Kumaragupta I's inscriptions mention his rule over Magadha.
  • Gupta-era sculptures have been found at Bodh Gaya, Nalanda, Sarnath — Bihar being a major centre.
BPSC Must-Know: Nalanda University in Bihar was founded by Kumaragupta I (Shakraditya) in the early 5th century CE. It attracted students from China, Korea, Japan, Tibet and Southeast Asia — including the famous Chinese pilgrim Hiuen Tsang.

Decline of the Gupta Empire

  • Huna invasions — though Skandagupta repelled them, they continued to harass the empire.
  • Weak successors after Skandagupta — no able ruler could hold the empire together.
  • Feudatories declared independence — the Maitrakas of Valabhi, the Vakatakas, and the Maukharis broke away.
  • Yashodharman of Malwa defeated the Hunas in 528 CE and asserted independence.
  • Decentralisation — land grants to officials weakened central authority.
  • Economic decline — debasement of coins, decline in trade with Rome.
  • By c. 550 CE, the Gupta Empire had disintegrated into small regional kingdoms.
