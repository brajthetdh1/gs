Indian Economy — Planning & NITI Aayog: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 13 min read · BPSC SSC

India adopted economic planning after Independence to achieve rapid growth, self-reliance and social justice. The Planning Commission was set up in 1950 to formulate Five Year Plans. From 1951 to 2017, India implemented Twelve Five Year Plans. In 2015, the Planning Commission was replaced by NITI Aayog — a think tank that promotes cooperative federalism and bottom-up planning. For BPSC and SSC, this topic is extremely high-yield — especially the Five Year Plans, the Mahalanobis model, and Bihar's planning history.

What is Economic Planning?

Economic planning is a process in which the government sets specific targets and priorities for the economy — and allocates resources to achieve them within a defined time frame.

Objectives of Indian Planning

  • Rapid economic growth — increase GDP and per capita income.
  • Full employment — generate jobs for the growing workforce.
  • Self-reliance — reduce dependence on foreign aid and imports.
  • Social justice — reduce inequality and poverty.
  • Modernisation — adopt new technology and industrial practices.
  • Balanced regional development — reduce regional disparities.
  • Industrialisation — build a strong industrial base.
  • Agricultural development — achieve food security.

Exam Tip: The Planning Commission was set up on 15 March 1950 — by a resolution of the Union Cabinet (not by the Constitution or an Act of Parliament). The First Five Year Plan was launched on 1 April 1951. The 12th Plan was the last Five Year Plan — ending on 31 March 2017. NITI Aayog replaced the Planning Commission on 1 January 2015.

Planning Commission (1950–2014)

Composition

  • Chairman — the Prime Minister (ex-officio).
  • Deputy Chairman — appointed by the Prime Minister.
  • Members — full-time members and part-time members.
  • Secretary — senior civil servant.
  • Finance Minister, Home Minister and other ministers were associated members.

Functions of the Planning Commission

  • Assess the material, capital and human resources of the country.
  • Formulate Five Year Plans for the most effective and balanced utilisation of resources.
  • Determine priorities, stage of planning and allocation of resources.
  • Determine the conditions for successful implementation of the Plans.
  • Appraise the progress of the Plans from time to time.
  • Advise the Central and State Governments on planning matters.
  • Coordinate with the National Development Council (NDC).

National Development Council (NDC)

  • Set up in 1952 — an extra-constitutional and non-statutory body.
  • Chairman — Prime Minister.
  • Members — Chief Ministers of all States, Administrators of Union Territories, and members of the Planning Commission.
  • Functions: Approves the Five Year Plans, reviews their progress, and gives guidelines for national development.
  • Called the "super cabinet" or the "highest body for economic planning".
Remember: The Planning Commission was NOT a constitutional body — it was set up by an executive resolution. Similarly, the National Development Council (NDC) was also an extra-constitutional body. The Prime Minister was the Chairman of both.

Key Highlights of Major Plans

First Plan (1951–56)

  • Based on the Harrod-Domar model.
  • Focus on agriculture, irrigation and power — to rebuild the economy after Partition.
  • Target: 2.1% growth; achieved 3.6% — most successful Plan in terms of targets met.
  • Established the Indian Institutes of Technology (IITs) — starting with IIT Kharagpur (1951).
  • Started major irrigation projects — Bhakra Nangal, Hirakud, Damodar Valley.

Second Plan (1956–61)

  • Based on the Mahalanobis model — named after P.C. Mahalanobis.
  • Focus on rapid industrialisation — heavy and basic industries.
  • Set up steel plants at Bhilai, Rourkela and Durgapur.
  • Industrial Policy Resolution, 1956 — gave public sector a dominant role.
  • Target: 4.5%; achieved 4.27% — a moderate success.

Third Plan (1961–66)

  • Focused on making India a self-reliant and self-generating economy.
  • Failed due to the Chinese aggression (1962) and Indo-Pak War (1965).
  • Target: 5.6%; achieved only 2.4% — the biggest failure among the Plans.
  • Resulted in a Plan Holiday (1966–69) — three Annual Plans.

Fourth Plan (1969–74)

  • Focus on growth with stability and self-reliance.
  • Nationalisation of 14 banks (1969) and the Green Revolution.
  • Target: 5.7%; achieved 3.3% — affected by the 1971 war, drought and oil crisis.

Fifth Plan (1974–78)

  • Focus on poverty removal and self-reliance.
  • Introduced the Minimum Needs Programme (MNP).
  • Implemented the slogan "Garibi Hatao".
  • Terminated early in 1978 by the Janata Government.

Sixth Plan (1980–85)

  • Focus on direct attack on poverty, employment, and modernisation.
  • Introduced the National Rural Employment Programme (NREP).
  • Target: 5.2%; achieved 5.7% — a moderate success.

Seventh Plan (1985–90)

  • Slogan: "Food, Work, Productivity".
  • Emphasis on employment generation and poverty alleviation.
  • Launched the Jawahar Rozgar Yojana (JRY).
  • Target: 5.0%; achieved 6.0% — a success.

Eighth Plan (1992–97)

  • Period marked by the LPG reforms (1991) — Liberalisation, Privatisation, Globalisation.
  • Focus on human resource development and rapid industrialisation.
  • Target: 5.6%; achieved 6.8% — a success.

Ninth Plan (1997–2002)

  • Theme: "Growth with social justice and equity".
  • Focus on agriculture, employment, and empowerment of women.
  • Target: 6.5%; achieved 5.4% — fell short.

Tenth Plan (2002–07)

  • Target of 8% growth — with focus on reducing poverty by 5% by 2007.
  • Emphasis on monitoring outcomes rather than allocating resources.
  • Target: 8.0%; achieved 7.6% — near success.

Eleventh Plan (2007–12)

  • Theme: "Faster and More Inclusive Growth".
  • Focus on inclusive growth, education, health, and infrastructure.
  • Target: 9.0%; achieved 8.0% — partly affected by the 2008 global financial crisis.

Twelfth Plan (2012–17)

  • Theme: "Faster, More Inclusive and Sustainable Growth".
  • Last Five Year Plan — ended on 31 March 2017.
  • Target: 8.0%; achieved around 6.7% — affected by global slowdown.
BPSC Must-Know: The First Plan (1951–56) was based on the Harrod-Domar model and was the most successful in meeting its target. The Second Plan (1956–61) was based on the Mahalanobis model — focusing on heavy industries. The Third Plan (1961–66) was the biggest failure — leading to a Plan Holiday (1966–69). The 12th Plan (2012–17) was the last Five Year Plan.

Types of Plans Adopted in India

  • Imperative Planning — the government directs the allocation of resources (used in India up to 1991).
  • Indicative Planning — the government provides guidance, but markets decide (used since the 8th Plan).
  • Rolling Plan — revised every year (recommended by Gunnar Myrdal; implemented in 1978–80).
  • Perspective Plan — long-term plan (e.g. 15–20 years).
  • Annual Plan — yearly plan within the framework of the Five Year Plan.

Plan Holiday

  • 1966–69 — Three Annual Plans (after the Chinese and Pakistan wars).
  • 1990–92 — Two Annual Plans (political instability and BoP crisis).
  • During a Plan Holiday, the government does not launch a new Five Year Plan — instead, it runs Annual Plans.

NITI Aayog (2015 — Present)

NITI Aayog — National Institution for Transforming India — was set up on 1 January 2015, replacing the Planning Commission.

Composition

  • Chairperson — Prime Minister (ex-officio).
  • Vice-Chairperson — appointed by the Prime Minister.
  • CEO — appointed by the Prime Minister; the rank of Secretary to Government of India.
  • Full-time members and ex-officio members — including up to 4 Union Ministers nominated by the PM.
  • Special Invitees — experts and specialists.
  • Governing Council — Chief Ministers of all States, Chief Ministers of UTs with legislatures, and Lieutenant Governors of other UTs.
  • Regional Councils — for balanced regional development.

Objectives of NITI Aayog

  • To evolve a shared vision of national development priorities with active involvement of States.
  • To foster cooperative federalism — a partnership between the Centre and States.
  • To provide strategic and technical advice to the Centre and States.
  • To promote bottom-up planning — from village to national level.
  • To serve as a think tank for the Government of India.
  • To monitor and evaluate the implementation of programmes.
  • To provide a platform for resolution of inter-sectoral and inter-departmental issues.

Exam Tip: NITI Aayog was set up on 1 January 2015. Unlike the Planning Commission, NITI Aayog has no power to allocate funds — that power now rests with the Ministry of Finance. NITI Aayog promotes cooperative federalism and bottom-up planning. The Prime Minister is the Chairperson.

Aspirational Districts Programme (ADP)

  • Launched in January 2018 by NITI Aayog.
  • Covers 112 aspirational districts across 28 states.
  • Bihar has 13 aspirational districts — one of the largest shares among states.
  • Focuses on 5 themes — Health & Nutrition, Education, Agriculture & Water Resources, Financial Inclusion & Skill Development, and Basic Infrastructure.
  • Uses a ranking system to encourage competition among districts.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

Bihar's Planning History

  • Bihar was one of the earliest states to set up a State Planning Board — in the 1950s.
  • The Bihar State Planning Board was constituted to prepare and coordinate the state's Five Year Plans.
  • Bihar's development plans focused on agriculture, irrigation, education and rural development.

Bihar and the Five Year Plans

  • Bihar received significant central assistance through the Gadgil Formula — which allocated Plan funds based on population, per capita income, and other criteria.
  • Bihar benefited from the Green Revolution (from the 4th Plan onward) — but less than Punjab and Haryana.
  • Several major irrigation projects — including the Gandak Canal, Kosi Project and Son Canal — were developed under various Five Year Plans.
  • The Sone Command Area Development was a major Plan initiative in Bihar.

Bihar and NITI Aayog

  • Bihar has 13 Aspirational Districts under the NITI Aayog's ADP — including:
  • Araria, Aurangabad, Banka, Begusarai, Gaya, Jamui, Katihar, Khagaria, Muzaffarpur, Nawada, Purnia, Sheikhpura, Sitamarhi.
  • Bihar participates actively in NITI Aayog's Governing Council — through the Chief Minister.
  • Bihar has utilised NITI Aayog platforms for SDG India Index, Health Index, and School Education Quality Index — to benchmark its progress against other states.

Bihar — Key Development Initiatives

  • Saat Nishchay (Seven Resolves) — the Bihar Government's flagship programme covering 7 areas: Aarthik Hal, Yuvaon Ko Bal; Aarakshit Rozgar Mahilaon Ka Adhikar; Har Ghar Bijli; Har Ghar Nal Ka Jal; Ghar Tak Pakki Gali-Naliyan; Shauchalay Nirman Ghar-Ghar; and Awas Samaan.
  • Jeevika — Bihar Rural Livelihoods Project, supported by the World Bank.
  • Mukhyamantri Cycle Yojana — a scheme for girls' education.
  • Bihar Student Credit Card Scheme — for higher education.
  • Mukhyamantri Kanya Utthan Yojana — for girls' education and empowerment.
  • Bihar Industrial Investment Promotion Policy — for industrial development.
  • Bihar Startup Policy — to promote entrepreneurship.

Bihar's Development Challenges

  • Low per capita income — among the lowest in India.
  • High population density — putting pressure on resources.
  • Floods and droughts — recurring natural disasters.
  • Low industrialisation — limited manufacturing base.
  • Out-migration — large-scale migration for employment.
  • Low HDI — despite recent improvements.
  • Infrastructure deficit — though being addressed through Saat Nishchay.
BPSC Must-Know: Bihar has 13 Aspirational Districts under NITI Aayog's ADP — one of the largest shares among Indian states. Bihar's key development initiatives include Saat Nishchay, Jeevika, and the Bihar Student Credit Card Scheme. The Bihar State Planning Board was one of the earliest state planning bodies in India. These are very high-yield BPSC topics.
