Advent of Europeans in India: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 14 min read · BPSC SSC

The advent of Europeans in India marks the beginning of Modern Indian history. It started with the arrival of the Portuguese navigator Vasco da Gama at Calicut in 1498 CE, and led to the establishment of European trading companies — Portuguese, Dutch, English, Danish and French. Over the next 250 years, these trading companies fought each other, interfered in Indian politics, and ultimately the English East India Company emerged as the ruler of India. For BPSC and SSC, this topic is the foundation of Modern Indian history.

Why Europeans Came to India

  • Fall of Constantinople (1453 CE) — the Ottoman Turks captured Constantinople, blocking the traditional land route between Europe and Asia.
  • High demand for Indian spices — pepper, cloves, cinnamon and cardamom were in great demand in Europe.
  • Monopoly of Arab and Italian traders — they controlled the spice trade and charged exorbitant prices.
  • Renaissance spirit of exploration — Europeans were curious about new lands and sea routes.
  • Support from European monarchs — especially Portugal and Spain, who sponsored voyages of exploration.
  • New navigational technology — the compass, astrolabe, and improved shipbuilding made long sea voyages possible.
Exam Tip: The fall of Constantinople in 1453 CE is considered the immediate trigger for European voyages of discovery. It forced Europeans to find a new sea route to India, bypassing the Ottoman-controlled land route.

1. The Portuguese (1498–1961 CE)

The Portuguese were the first Europeans to reach India by sea, and the last to leave (Goa, 1961 CE).

Portuguese Administration

  • The Portuguese established their capital at Goa — with a Viceroy as the head of administration.
  • Established a chain of fortified settlements — Goa, Diu, Daman, Cochin, Salsette, Bombay (later given to England).
  • Introduced cartaz — a naval pass system that required Indian ships to buy passes to trade.
  • Monopolised the trade of pepper, cloves, cinnamon and other spices.
  • Encouraged missionary activity — the Jesuits under Francis Xavier converted many Indians to Christianity.
  • The Portuguese did not establish a strong territorial empire — they focused on trade and coastal settlements.

Decline of Portuguese Power

  • Small kingdom — Portugal could not sustain a large empire in India.
  • Religious intolerance — the Inquisition and forced conversions alienated Indians.
  • Corruption and inefficiency — Portuguese officials were notoriously corrupt.
  • Rise of the Dutch and English — they captured most of Portugal's Asian trade.
  • Capture of Hugli (Bengal) by the Mughals in 1632 CE — the Mughals punished the Portuguese for their piracy.
  • By the 17th century, the Portuguese were confined to Goa, Diu and Daman.
BPSC Must-Know: Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in 1498 CE and was welcomed by the Zamorin (ruler of Calicut). Afonso de Albuquerque (1509–1515) is considered the real founder of Portuguese power in India. Goa was captured in 1510 CE and remained Portuguese until 1961 CE.

2. The Dutch (1602–1759 CE)

The Dutch East India Company (VOC) was formed in 1602 CE and became the most powerful trading company in Asia for a brief period.

  • Formation of the Dutch East India Company (VOC) — 1602 CE; received a monopoly on Dutch trade in the East.
  • First Dutch factory in India — Masulipatnam (1605 CE) in present-day Andhra Pradesh.
  • Other Dutch settlements: Pulicat, Surat, Cochin, Chinsura, Nagapattinam, Bimilipatnam.
  • Defeated the Portuguese in many encounters — captured Malacca (1641) and other Portuguese possessions.
  • Focused on the spice trade — especially in the Moluccas (present-day Indonesia).
  • Defeated by the English at the Battle of Bedara (1759 CE) — the Dutch never recovered from this defeat.
  • By the early 19th century, the Dutch had withdrawn from India — their main interest shifted to Indonesia.
Remember: The Dutch East India Company (VOC) was formed in 1602 CE. Their first Indian factory was at Masulipatnam (1605). They were defeated by the English at the Battle of Bedara (1759 CE), ending their influence in India.

3. The English East India Company (1600–1858 CE)

The English East India Company (EIC) was founded on 31 December 1600 CE by a royal charter of Queen Elizabeth I. It eventually became the ruler of India.

English Settlements — The Three Presidencies

  • Madras Presidency — Fort St. George (1639) — the first English fortified settlement.
  • Bombay Presidency — transferred to England in 1661 CE; developed as a major port.
  • Bengal Presidency — Calcutta founded in 1690 CE; became the most important English settlement.

Farman of 1717 CE

  • Issued by the Mughal Emperor Farrukhsiyar in 1717 CE.
  • Granted the English East India Company duty-free trade in Bengal.
  • The Company could issue dastaks (passes) for the movement of goods.
  • This farman became a major source of conflict between the Company and the Nawabs of Bengal — leading to the Battle of Plassey (1757).
Exam Tip: The Farman of 1717 CE issued by Farrukhsiyar was a turning point — it gave the English East India Company duty-free trade in Bengal. The misuse of this farman by the Company's officials led to conflict with the Nawabs of Bengal and eventually the Battle of Plassey (1757 CE).

4. The Danes (1616–1845 CE)

The Danish East India Company was established in 1616 CE. The Danes were the least significant of the European powers in India.

  • Danish East India Company founded — 1616 CE.
  • Established settlements at Tranquebar (1620 CE) on the Coromandel Coast, and at Serampore (1755 CE) in Bengal.
  • The Danish settlements were small and never became major trading centres.
  • In 1845 CE, the Danes sold all their Indian settlements to the English East India Company.
  • Serampore is remembered as the place where William Carey and the Serampore Missionaries worked on education and printing.

5. The French (1664–1954 CE)

The French were the last Europeans to enter the Indian trade — but they became the most serious rivals of the English.

Joseph François Dupleix (1697–1763 CE)

  • Governor-General of French India from 1742 CE.
  • Was the first European to interfere in Indian political disputes — he supported rival claimants to the thrones of Hyderabad and Carnatic.
  • Dreamed of a French empire in India but was defeated by the superior tactics of Robert Clive and Eyre Coote.
  • Was recalled to France in 1754 CE after failing to achieve his objectives.

Robert Clive

  • The English commander who defeated the French in the Carnatic Wars.
  • Architect of the Battle of Plassey (1757 CE) — which made the English the real power in Bengal.
  • Established the dual government in Bengal (1765–1772) — the Company held real power, the Nawab was a figurehead.
BPSC Must-Know: Joseph François Dupleix was the greatest French governor — he first showed Europeans how to interfere in Indian politics. He was defeated by Robert Clive. The Battle of Wandiwash (1760 CE) was the decisive battle that ended French ambitions in India.

The Carnatic Wars (1740–1763 CE)

The Carnatic Wars were a series of three wars fought between the English and the French in South India. They were an extension of European conflicts (Austrian Succession, Seven Years' War) into India.

First Carnatic War (1740–1748 CE)

  • Part of the War of Austrian Succession in Europe.
  • The English under Captain Paradise defeated the French at St. Thome.
  • The Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748 CE) ended the war — Madras was returned to the English.
  • Result: A stalemate — but both sides realised the importance of Indian alliances.

Second Carnatic War (1749–1754 CE)

  • Dupleix interfered in the succession disputes of Hyderabad and the Carnatic.
  • Supported Chanda Sahib for the Carnatic throne and Muzaffar Jang for Hyderabad.
  • Robert Clive supported Muhammad Ali (English candidate) and Nasir Jang (Hyderabad).
  • Clive's Siege of Arcot (1751 CE) — a turning point; Clive's brilliant defence won the war for the English.
  • The Treaty of Pondicherry (1755 CE) ended the war — Dupleix was recalled to France.

Third Carnatic War (1758–1763 CE)

  • Part of the Seven Years' War in Europe.
  • The French under Count de Lally attacked Madras but were defeated.
  • Battle of Wandiwash (1760 CE) — English under Eyre Coote decisively defeated the French.
  • The Treaty of Paris (1763 CE) ended the war — French settlements were restored but the French were no longer a political power in India.

Why the English Succeeded

  • Superior naval power — the English Royal Navy dominated the Indian Ocean.
  • Better financial resources — the English had access to the wealth of Bengal after 1757 CE.
  • Stronger organisational structure — the English Company was a joint-stock company with professional management.
  • Support from the British government — the government backed the Company with military force when needed.
  • Diplomatic skill — the English made strategic alliances with Indian rulers.
  • Superior military tactics — disciplined armies, modern weapons, and better training.
  • Lack of unity among Indian rulers — the Mughal Empire was weak; Indian rulers fought each other.
  • Capture of Bengal — the richest province of India — gave the English the resources to dominate India.

From Traders to Rulers — Bengal

The transformation of the English East India Company from a trading company to a ruling power happened in Bengal.

Battle of Plassey (1757 CE)

  • Fought on 23 June 1757 between the English East India Company (under Robert Clive) and the Nawab of Bengal Siraj-ud-Daulah.
  • The immediate cause was the misuse of the farman of 1717 and the Black Hole Incident (though its authenticity is disputed).
  • Mir Jafar — the commander of Siraj-ud-Daulah's army — betrayed the Nawab and joined the English.
  • The English won a decisive victory; Siraj-ud-Daulah was killed; Mir Jafar was made the new Nawab.
  • The English gained enormous wealth — including compensation of ₹1.7 crore and the right to duty-free trade in Bengal.
  • Plassey established the English as the real power in Bengal — though the Nawab remained a figurehead.

Battle of Buxar (1764 CE)

  • Fought on 22 October 1764 at Buxar (in present-day Bihar).
  • The English (under Hector Munro) defeated the combined forces of:
    • Mir Qasim — the Nawab of Bengal
    • Shuja-ud-Daulah — the Nawab of Awadh
    • Shah Alam II — the Mughal Emperor
  • The English won a decisive victory — the combined Indian army was completely routed.
  • The Treaty of Allahabad (1765 CE) gave the English the Diwani (revenue rights) of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa.
  • This marked the beginning of the dual government (1765–1772) — the Company held real power, the Nawab was a puppet.
  • Buxar is often considered more important than Plassey — it was a fair battle where the English won against a large, well-equipped Indian army.
BPSC Must-Know: The Battle of Buxar (1764 CE) was fought in Bihar — making it one of the most important events in Bihar's history. The Treaty of Allahabad (1765 CE) gave the English the Diwani (revenue rights) of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa — the foundation of British rule in India. This is a critical BPSC topic.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • Battle of Buxar (1764 CE) — fought in Buxar, Bihar; the English defeated Mir Qasim (Bengal), Shuja-ud-Daulah (Awadh) and Shah Alam II (Mughal Emperor).
  • Treaty of Allahabad (1765 CE) — gave the English the Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. This was the true beginning of British rule in India.
  • Patna — an important trading centre during the early English period; became a major centre of English trade.
  • Munger (Monghyr) — became a major English military station after 1765 CE; the site of the first English factory in Bihar.
  • Hooghly — the Portuguese were defeated by the Mughals in Bengal — their trade shifted to Bihar and other regions.
  • Sasaram, Bhojpur — the region around Buxar saw major events of the Battle of Buxar and subsequent British consolidation.
  • Bihar became a part of the Bengal Presidency — under the direct administration of the East India Company.
  • The Battle of Buxar is a landmark in the history of Bihar — it marked the end of Mughal power and the beginning of British rule in Bihar.
