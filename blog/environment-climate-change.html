Climate Change & Global Warming: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 15 min read · BPSC SSC

Climate change is one of the most critical global challenges of our time. It refers to long-term shifts in temperatures and weather patterns — primarily caused by human activities that release greenhouse gases (GHGs) into the atmosphere. Global warming — the rise in Earth's average temperature — is the most visible symptom of climate change. For BPSC and SSC, this topic is extremely high-yield — especially the greenhouse effect, major GHGs, Kyoto Protocol, Paris Agreement, COP summits, India's climate commitments (NDCs, Panchamrit), and Bihar's climate vulnerability. This is also linked to Current Affairs — so read carefully.

What is Climate Change?

Climate change refers to long-term changes in temperature, precipitation, wind patterns and other measures of climate over decades or centuries. While the Earth's climate has always changed naturally, the current rate of change is unprecedented — driven primarily by human activities.

The Greenhouse Effect

The greenhouse effect is a natural process by which certain gases in the Earth's atmosphere trap heat from the sun — keeping the planet warm enough to support life. Without the greenhouse effect, the Earth's average temperature would be around -18°C instead of the current +15°C.

How It Works

  1. Solar radiation reaches the Earth's surface.
  2. Some is reflected back into space; most is absorbed and re-emitted as infrared radiation.
  3. Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere absorb this infrared radiation and re-emit it — trapping heat.
  4. The result: the Earth stays warm.

Enhanced Greenhouse Effect

  • Human activities have increased the concentration of greenhouse gases — intensifying the natural greenhouse effect.
  • This enhanced greenhouse effect is the primary cause of global warming and climate change.
Exam Tip: The greenhouse effect is a natural process — without it, the Earth would be too cold for life. The enhanced greenhouse effect — caused by human activities — is the problem. This distinction is frequently asked in BPSC and SSC.

Major Greenhouse Gases (GHGs)

GasSourceContribution to Global Warming

Methane (CH₄)Agriculture, livestock, landfills, rice paddies~20% — 25x more potent than CO₂
Nitrous Oxide (N₂O)Fertilisers, industrial processes~6% — 300x more potent than CO₂
Water Vapour (H₂O)NaturalMost abundant — but not directly human-caused
Fluorinated Gases (CFCs, HFCs, PFCs, SF₆)Refrigerants, aerosols, industrial processesSmall quantity — but extremely potent (thousands of times more than CO₂)
Ozone (O₃)—Greenhouse gas in the troposphere; protective in the stratosphere

Global Warming Potential (GWP)

  • GWP is a measure of how much a gas contributes to global warming relative to CO₂ (which has a GWP of 1).
  • Methane (CH₄) — GWP ~25 (over 100 years).
  • Nitrous Oxide (N₂O) — GWP ~300.
  • CFCs, HFCs, PFCs, SF₆ — GWP thousands to tens of thousands times that of CO₂.
  • Though these gases are present in small quantities, their high GWP makes them significant contributors to global warming.
BPSC Must-Know: CO₂ is the largest contributor to global warming (~60%) — but methane (CH₄) is 25 times more potent, and N₂O is 300 times more potent. The most potent GHGs are fluorinated gases (CFCs, HFCs, PFCs, SF₆) — but they are present in very small quantities. These are very frequently asked BPSC and SSC questions.

Global Warming

Global warming is the long-term rise in the average temperature of the Earth's climate system — primarily due to human-induced greenhouse gas emissions.

Key Facts

  • The Earth's average temperature has risen by about 1.1°C since pre-industrial times (1850–1900).
  • The last decade (2014–2023) was the warmest on record.
  • 2023 was the warmest year on record — with global temperatures exceeding 1.4°C above pre-industrial levels.
  • 2024 is expected to be even warmer — crossing the 1.5°C threshold temporarily.
  • The Paris Agreement (2015) aims to limit global warming to well below 2°C — preferably to 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels.

Impacts of Global Warming

  • Rising sea levels — due to melting glaciers and thermal expansion of oceans.
  • Melting glaciers and ice caps — Arctic, Antarctica, Himalayas.
  • Extreme weather events — heat waves, floods, droughts, cyclones, storms.
  • Shifting monsoon patterns — affecting agriculture.
  • Loss of biodiversity — species unable to adapt are threatened with extinction.
  • Ocean acidification — due to absorption of CO₂ by oceans.
  • Coral bleaching — widespread damage to coral reefs.
  • Spread of diseases — vector-borne diseases like malaria, dengue spreading to new areas.
  • Water scarcity — as freshwater sources shrink.
  • Food insecurity — crop yields affected by heat and unpredictable rainfall.
  • Displacement of people — climate refugees.

Global Warming and India

  • India is one of the most vulnerable countries to climate change.
  • Rising temperatures are affecting agriculture — especially wheat, rice, and pulses.
  • Increased frequency of floods and droughts.
  • Glacial retreat in the Himalayas — threatening water supply from Himalayan rivers.
  • Sea-level rise — threatening coastal cities like Mumbai, Chennai, Kolkata.
  • Heat waves — becoming more frequent and intense.
  • Coral bleaching — affecting the Gulf of Mannar, Lakshadweep, Andaman & Nicobar.
Exam Tip: India is one of the most vulnerable countries to climate change. The Himalayas are particularly sensitive — glacial retreat threatens water supply for millions. Sea-level rise threatens coastal cities. India's monsoon-dependent agriculture is highly vulnerable to climate change.

International Climate Change Framework

United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC)

  • Adopted at the Rio Earth Summit in 1992.
  • Came into force in 1994.
  • India is a founding member.
  • Objective: to stabilise GHG concentrations in the atmosphere at a level that prevents dangerous anthropogenic interference with the climate system.
  • Principle: "Common But Differentiated Responsibilities" (CBDR) — developed and developing countries have different responsibilities.
  • The Conference of Parties (COP) is the supreme decision-making body of the UNFCCC.

Kyoto Protocol (1997)

  • Adopted at COP-3 in Kyoto, Japan (1997).
  • Came into force in 2005.
  • Set legally binding emission reduction targets for developed (Annex-I) countries.
  • Developing countries (including India and China) were not required to reduce emissions — but encouraged to do so voluntarily.
  • Introduced three market-based mechanisms:
    • Emissions Trading — carbon credits.
    • Clean Development Mechanism (CDM) — developed countries invest in clean projects in developing countries.
    • Joint Implementation (JI) — between developed countries.
  • The US did not ratify the Kyoto Protocol.
  • Canada withdrew from the Kyoto Protocol in 2012.

Paris Agreement (2015)

  • Adopted at COP-21 in Paris (2015).
  • Came into force in 2016.
  • Objective: to limit global warming to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels, and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5°C.
  • Legally binding for all parties — but the targets (NDCs) are nationally determined.
  • Key features:
    • Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) — every country submits its own climate action plan.
    • Global Stocktake — every 5 years, to review progress.
    • Climate Finance — $100 billion per year from developed countries to developing countries.
    • Loss and Damage — compensation for climate-related losses.
    • Adaptation and Mitigation — both are emphasised.

India's Climate Commitments

India's Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs)

  • India submitted its first NDC in 2015 — before the Paris Agreement.
  • Updated NDC submitted in 2022.

India's Key Climate Targets (Updated NDC 2022)

TargetYear
Reduce emissions intensity of GDP by 45% (from 2005 levels)By 2030
Achieve 50% cumulative electric power installed capacity from non-fossil fuel sourcesBy 2030
Create an additional carbon sink of 2.5–3 billion tonnes of CO₂ equivalent through forest and tree coverBy 2030
Reduce emissions intensity of GDP by 33–35% (from 2005 levels) — earlier targetBy 2030

Panchamrit (Five Nectar Elements) — Announced at COP-26 (2021)

Prime Minister Narendra Modi announced India's "Panchamrit" — five climate commitments — at the Glasgow COP-26:

  1. 500 GW non-fossil fuel capacity by 2030.
  2. 50% of energy requirements from renewable energy by 2030.
  3. Reduction of total projected carbon emissions by 1 billion tonnes by 2030.
  4. Reduction of the carbon intensity of the economy by 45% by 2030.
  5. Achieve Net Zero by 2070.

Other Key Commitments

  • Net Zero by 2070 — India's long-term goal.
  • International Solar Alliance (ISA) — co-founded by India and France in 2015 — headquartered in Gurugram, India.
  • Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure (CDRI) — launched by India in 2019.
  • Mission LiFE (Lifestyle for Environment) — launched by India in 2022 — promoting sustainable lifestyles.
  • National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC) — launched in 2008 — 8 national missions.
  • Green Hydrogen Mission — launched in 2023.
  • International Big Cat Alliance (IBCA) — launched by India in 2023.
BPSC Must-Know: India's Panchamrit targets (COP-26, 2021) — 500 GW non-fossil capacity by 2030; 50% renewable energy; 1 billion tonnes carbon emission reduction; 45% carbon intensity reduction; Net Zero by 2070. India co-founded the International Solar Alliance (ISA) with France (2015) — HQ in Gurugram. NAPCC (2008) has 8 national missions. These are very frequently asked BPSC topics.

National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC) — 2008

The NAPCC has 8 National Missions:

  1. National Solar Mission — solar energy.
  2. National Mission for Enhanced Energy Efficiency — energy efficiency.
  3. National Mission on Sustainable Habitat — sustainable urbanisation.
  4. National Water Mission — water conservation.
  5. National Mission for Sustaining the Himalayan Ecosystem — Himalayan ecology.
  6. National Mission for a Green India — afforestation.
  7. National Mission for Sustainable Agriculture — climate-resilient agriculture.
  8. National Mission on Strategic Knowledge for Climate Change — research and knowledge.

Ozone Depletion

  • The ozone layer — in the stratosphere — protects the Earth from harmful ultraviolet (UV) radiation.
  • Ozone depletion — thinning of the ozone layer — caused mainly by chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), halons, carbon tetrachloride, and methyl chloroform.
  • Effects:
    • Increased UV radiation — skin cancer, cataracts, weakened immune systems.
    • Damage to crops and marine life.
    • Climate change.
  • The Antarctic Ozone Hole — discovered in 1985.
  • Montreal Protocol (1987) — international treaty to phase out ozone-depleting substances (ODS).
  • Kigali Amendment (2016) — phasing out HFCs (which do not deplete ozone but are potent greenhouse gases).
  • International Day for the Preservation of the Ozone Layer — 16 September.
Exam Tip: The Montreal Protocol (1987) is the most successful international environmental treaty — it has led to the phase-out of over 99% of ozone-depleting substances. India ratified the Montreal Protocol in 1992 and the Kigali Amendment in 2021. The Ozone Day is celebrated on 16 September.

Climate Finance

  • Climate finance refers to financing for climate change mitigation and adaptation.
  • Green Climate Fund (GCF) — established in 2010 at COP-16 (Cancun) — to support climate action in developing countries.
  • Global Environment Facility (GEF) — established in 1991 — finances environmental projects.
  • Loss and Damage Fund — established at COP-27 (2022) — to compensate vulnerable countries for climate-related losses.
  • Developed countries committed to provide $100 billion per year by 2020 — this commitment has largely not been met.
  • New Collective Quantified Goal (NCQG) — a new climate finance target under negotiation.

Carbon Markets & Carbon Credits

  • Carbon credit — a permit that allows the holder to emit one tonne of CO₂ or equivalent.
  • Carbon market — a trading system where carbon credits are bought and sold.
  • Emissions Trading System (ETS) — cap-and-trade system for emissions.
  • Clean Development Mechanism (CDM) — under Kyoto Protocol — allows developed countries to invest in emission-reduction projects in developing countries.
  • Carbon Tax — tax on carbon emissions.
  • Carbon Offsetting — compensating for emissions by investing in environmental projects.
  • Carbon Sequestration — capturing and storing atmospheric CO₂.
  • Carbon Sink — a natural reservoir (forests, oceans, soil) that absorbs more carbon than it releases.

Climate Change and Bihar (BPSC Focus)

Bihar — Climate Vulnerability

  • Bihar is one of India's most climate-vulnerable states.
  • Bihar faces:
    • Recurring floods — especially in north Bihar (Kosi, Gandak, Bagmati, Burhi Gandak).
    • Droughts — in south Bihar (Magadh, parts of the Chhotanagpur plateau region).
    • Heat waves — increasingly frequent and intense — especially in the Gangetic plains.
    • Cold waves — in winter, affecting north Bihar.
    • Erratic monsoon — affecting agriculture.
    • Groundwater depletion — especially in south Bihar.
    • Arsenic and fluoride contamination — in groundwater.
    • Soil erosion — especially in the Kosi region.
  • Bihar's agriculture-dependent economy makes it particularly vulnerable to climate change.

Bihar's Climate Action Initiatives

  • Bihar State Action Plan on Climate Change (BSAPCC) — prepared in line with the National Action Plan on Climate Change (NAPCC).
  • Jal-Jeevan-Hariyali — flagship state programme for water conservation and tree plantation.
  • Mission Harit Bihar — for increasing tree cover in the state.
  • Bihar Water Policy — for water resource management.
  • Solar Energy Policy — Bihar has installed solar power plants and promoted rooftop solar.
  • Bihar Renewable Energy Development Agency (BREDA) — promotes renewable energy in the state.
  • Flood Management Programme — for flood control in north Bihar.
  • Kosi Rehabilitation and Reconstruction Programme — for the Kosi flood-affected areas.
  • Climate-Resilient Agriculture — promotion of drought-resistant crops, drip irrigation.
  • Bihar Plastic Ban — state-level ban on single-use plastics.
  • Namami Gange Programme — for cleaning the Ganga in Bihar.
  • Swachh Bharat Mission — for sanitation and cleanliness.

Bihar's Renewable Energy Potential

  • Solar Energy — Bihar has significant solar potential; the state has set ambitious solar targets.
  • Biomass Energy — Bihar has abundant biomass (agricultural residue, cattle dung) for biogas and biomass power.
  • Biogas — Bihar has a strong biogas programme — with lakhs of family-size biogas plants.
  • Small Hydro Power — limited potential in Bihar.
  • Wind Energy — limited potential due to low wind speeds.

Bihar's Climate Commitments

  • Bihar has committed to increasing forest and tree cover under Mission Harit Bihar.
  • Bihar aims to expand renewable energy — especially solar and biomass.
  • Bihar has been a key partner in India's Panchamrit targets — through state-level actions.
  • Bihar's Jal-Jeevan-Hariyali is one of the most ambitious state-level water and climate programmes in India.
BPSC Must-Know: Bihar is one of India's most climate-vulnerable states — facing floods, droughts, heat waves, and erratic monsoons. The Bihar State Action Plan on Climate Change (BSAPCC) aligns with the NAPCC. Jal-Jeevan-Hariyali and Mission Harit Bihar are the state's flagship climate action programmes. Bihar has significant solar and biomass energy potential. BREDA promotes renewable energy in the state. These are frequently asked BPSC Environment questions.
