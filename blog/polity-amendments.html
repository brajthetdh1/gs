Important Constitutional Amendments: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 15 min read · BPSC SSC

The Constitution of India is a living document — it can be amended to meet the changing needs of society. The procedure for amendment is laid down in Article 368 (Part XX). Since 1950, Parliament has passed over 106 Constitutional Amendment Acts. The most important amendments — like the 1st (1951), 42nd (1976), 44th (1978), 73rd and 74th (1992), 86th (2002), 101st (2016), 103rd (2019), 104th (2019), and 106th (2023) — are frequently asked in BPSC and SSC exams. This is the final post in our Polity series — a perfect revision resource.

Article 368 — Amendment Procedure

Article 368 provides for the procedure for amending the Constitution. It states that Parliament may amend the Constitution by way of addition, variation or repeal of any provision.

Three Types of Amendment Procedures

TypeProcedureExamples
Simple MajorityLike passing an ordinary law — majority of members present and votingCreation of new states, abolition/creation of Legislative Councils, citizenship, salaries of judges
Special Majority2/3 of members present and voting + majority of total membership of each HouseFundamental Rights, DPSP, all other provisions not covered in the other two categories
Special Majority + Ratification by StatesSpecial majority in Parliament + ratification by half the State LegislaturesElection of President, distribution of legislative powers, representation of States in Parliament, Article 368 itself, Supreme Court and High Courts
Exam Tip: Article 368 provides for the amendment procedure. There are three types of amendment procedures — simple majority, special majority, and special majority + ratification by half the states. The provision for ratification by states is a federal feature — it protects the interests of the states.

Important Points About Article 368

  • An amendment bill can be introduced in either House of Parliament — but not in a State Legislature.
  • There is no provision for a joint sitting of both Houses for a Constitutional Amendment Bill.
  • The President must give assent to a Constitution Amendment Bill — he cannot withhold it or return it (24th Amendment, 1971).
  • An amendment can be challenged in court if it violates the Basic Structure of the Constitution (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973).
  • The 44th Amendment (1978) made it clear that the President cannot return a Constitution Amendment Bill.

Landmark Constitutional Amendments

1st Amendment Act, 1951

  • Added Article 15(4) — reservation for backward classes.
  • Added Article 31A and 31B — protection of land reform laws.
  • Added the Ninth Schedule — to protect land reform laws from judicial review.
  • Added Article 19(6) — reasonable restrictions on freedom of trade.
  • Provided for special provisions for the advancement of backward classes.
  • Passed in response to the Champakam Dorairajan case (1951).

7th Amendment Act, 1956

  • Reorganised states on the basis of language — following the States Reorganisation Commission (Fazl Ali Commission, 1953).
  • Abolished the classification of states into Part A, B, C, D — introduced a uniform system of states and Union Territories.
  • Provided for the creation of Zonal Councils.
  • Allowed the same person to be appointed as Governor of two or more states.

24th Amendment Act, 1971

  • Made it clear that Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution — including Fundamental Rights.
  • Made the President's assent mandatory for Constitution Amendment Bills.
  • Passed in response to the Golaknath case (1967).

25th Amendment Act, 1971

  • Added Article 31C — laws giving effect to DPSP under Article 39(b) and 39(c) cannot be challenged.
  • Changed the compensation provision for property acquisition — introduced the word "amount" instead of "compensation".

42nd Amendment Act, 1976 — "Mini Constitution"

The most comprehensive amendment in Indian history — often called the "Mini Constitution". Passed during the Emergency (1975–77).

  • Added "Socialist", "Secular" and "Integrity" to the Preamble.
  • Added Fundamental Duties (Article 51A) — 10 duties.
  • Added Article 43A — workers' participation in management.
  • Added Article 48A — protection of environment, forests and wildlife.
  • Added Article 39A — equal justice and free legal aid.
  • Extended Article 31C to all DPSP — giving them primacy over Fundamental Rights.
  • Transferred education and forests from the State List to the Concurrent List.
  • Increased the term of the Lok Sabha from 5 to 6 years (later reversed by 44th Amendment).
  • Provided for the creation of Administrative Tribunals.
  • Froze the seats in the Lok Sabha and State Assemblies on the basis of the 1971 census (until 2000, later extended to 2026).
  • Curtailed the power of judicial review.
  • Made the President bound to accept the advice of the Council of Ministers.

44th Amendment Act, 1978

Passed after the Emergency to reverse many of the changes made by the 42nd Amendment. Often called the "Anti-Emergency Amendment".

  • Removed the Right to Property from Fundamental Rights (Articles 19(1)(f) and 31) — made it a legal right under Article 300A.
  • Restored the term of the Lok Sabha to 5 years.
  • Replaced "internal disturbance" with "armed rebellion" as a ground for National Emergency.
  • Made the written recommendation of the Cabinet mandatory for a National Emergency.
  • Made Articles 20 and 21 non-suspendable during a National Emergency.
  • Provided that Article 19 freedoms can be suspended only in case of war or external aggression.
  • Allowed the Lok Sabha to revoke an Emergency by a simple majority.
  • Restored the President's discretion in certain matters.
  • Restored the judicial review power of the courts.
BPSC Must-Know: The 42nd Amendment (1976) is called the "Mini Constitution" — it made the most extensive changes. The 44th Amendment (1978) is called the "Anti-Emergency Amendment" — it reversed many of the 42nd Amendment's provisions and strengthened civil liberties. Both are extremely high-yield for BPSC and SSC.

52nd Amendment Act, 1985 — Anti-Defection Law

  • Added the Tenth Schedule — Anti-Defection Law.
  • Defines defection — when a member voluntarily gives up party membership or votes against party directions.
  • Disqualification decided by the Speaker or Chairman of the House.
  • The 91st Amendment (2003) later removed the "one-third split" exemption — now only a merger by two-thirds of members is allowed.

61st Amendment Act, 1988

  • Reduced the voting age from 21 to 18 years.
  • A landmark in youth empowerment and democratic participation.

73rd and 74th Amendment Acts, 1992

  • 73rd Amendment — gave constitutional status to Panchayati Raj; added Part IX and the Eleventh Schedule.
  • 74th Amendment — gave constitutional status to Municipalities; added Part IX-A and the Twelfth Schedule.
  • Both are landmarks in democratic decentralisation.

86th Amendment Act, 2002

  • Added Article 21A — Right to Education for children aged 6–14.
  • Amended Article 45 — early childhood care and education for children below 6.
  • Added the 11th Fundamental Duty — parents/guardians to provide education to children aged 6–14.
  • Implemented through the Right to Education Act (RTE), 2009.

91st Amendment Act, 2003

  • Capped the size of the Council of Ministers at 15% of the total strength of the Lok Sabha (or Vidhan Sabha).
  • Removed the exemption for one-third split in the Anti-Defection Law.
  • Strengthened the Anti-Defection Law by making merger by two-thirds the only valid exemption.

97th Amendment Act, 2011

  • Added Article 43B — promotion of cooperative societies.
  • Added Part IX-B — provisions for cooperative societies.
  • Introduced a new constitutional framework for cooperatives.

99th Amendment Act, 2014 — NJAC

  • Established the National Judicial Appointments Commission (NJAC).
  • Struck down by the Supreme Court in 2015 (Fourth Judges Case) — held unconstitutional.
  • The collegium system was restored.

100th Amendment Act, 2015 — India-Bangladesh Land Boundary

  • Ratified the India–Bangladesh Land Boundary Agreement, 1974.
  • Exchanged certain enclaves between India and Bangladesh.

101st Amendment Act, 2016 — GST

  • Introduced the Goods and Services Tax (GST) in India.
  • Added Article 279A — GST Council.
  • GST was implemented on 1 July 2017.
  • One of the most significant economic reforms in Indian history.

102nd Amendment Act, 2018 — NCBC

  • Gave constitutional status to the National Commission for Backward Classes (NCBC).
  • Added Article 338B — NCBC.
  • Added Article 342A — power of the President to specify backward classes.

103rd Amendment Act, 2019 — EWS Reservation

  • Provided 10% reservation for Economically Weaker Sections (EWS) in education and public employment.
  • Added Article 15(6) and Article 16(6).
  • Applicable to general category candidates who meet the economic criteria.
  • Upheld by the Supreme Court in 2022.

104th Amendment Act, 2019 — SC/ST Reservation Extension

  • Extended SC/ST reservation in the Lok Sabha and State Assemblies for another 10 years — until 2030.
  • Ended the provision for Anglo-Indian nominated members in the Lok Sabha and State Assemblies.
  • Continued SC/ST reservation in the Lok Sabha and State Assemblies.

105th Amendment Act, 2021 — OBC Reservation

  • Restored the power of States to identify Socially and Educationally Backward Classes (SEBCs).
  • Amended Article 338B and Article 342A — restoring the states' role in OBC identification.

106th Amendment Act, 2023 — Women's Reservation

  • Provided for 33% reservation for women in the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies.
  • Also applicable to the Delhi Legislative Assembly.
  • Reservation will come into effect after the next census and delimitation.
  • Reservation is for 15 years — extendable by Parliament.
  • Also known as the Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam.
Exam Tip: The 106th Amendment (2023) — the Women's Reservation Bill — provides 33% reservation for women in the Lok Sabha and State Legislative Assemblies. It is one of the most significant amendments in recent Indian history. This is a very high-yield topic for BPSC and SSC.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

44th Amendment and Bihar

  • The 44th Amendment (1978) was passed after the Emergency (1975–77) — which had a profound impact on Bihar.
  • Jayaprakash Narayan (JP) — born in Sitabdiara, Saran (Bihar) — led the movement against the Emergency.
  • The JP Movement was the defining political struggle of the 1970s — it called for "Total Revolution" and mobilised lakhs of people across Bihar and India.
  • Many of Bihar's current political leaders — including Lalu Prasad Yadav and Nitish Kumar — were student leaders during the JP Movement.
  • The 44th Amendment's safeguards against Emergency are directly linked to the trauma faced by Bihar and other states during 1975–77.

73rd/74th Amendments and Bihar

  • Bihar was the first state to implement 50% reservation for women in Panchayati Raj institutions (2006) — going beyond the 1/3 mandate of the 73rd Amendment.
  • Bihar's Panchayati Raj structure: Gram Panchayat (Mukhiya), Panchayat Samiti (Pramukh), Zila Parishad (Adhyaksh).
  • Bihar Vidhan Parishad (75 seats) — one of the largest in India.

106th Amendment and Bihar

  • The 106th Amendment (2023) — Women's Reservation Bill — provides 33% reservation for women in the Lok Sabha and State Assemblies.
  • Bihar has been a pioneer in women's political empowerment — the state implemented 50% reservation for women in Panchayati Raj in 2006.
  • The Women's Reservation Amendment (106th) is expected to significantly increase women's representation in Bihar Vidhan Sabha (243 seats) and Lok Sabha from Bihar (40 seats).

103rd Amendment and Bihar

  • The 103rd Amendment (2019) — EWS Reservation — provides 10% reservation for economically weaker sections.
  • Bihar has a large population of economically weaker sections across all communities — this amendment is significant for the state's social justice landscape.
  • Bihar's Karpoori Thakur model of reservation (1970s) was a pioneering effort in social justice — the 103rd Amendment is part of this larger constitutional journey.

Other Amendments and Bihar

  • 1st Amendment (1951) — added Article 15(4) for backward classes. Bihar was one of the first states to implement OBC reservation — the Mungeri Lal Commission (1971–1976) recommended reservation for backward classes, and the Karpoori Thakur government implemented it.
  • 73rd/74th Amendments (1992) — Bihar implemented Panchayati Raj and Municipal administration reforms — including the Bihar Panchayat Raj Act, 2006.
  • 101st Amendment (2016) — GST — Bihar was one of the first states to ratify the GST Constitutional Amendment.
  • 102nd Amendment (2018) — NCBC — the constitutional status for the National Commission for Backward Classes strengthens Bihar's OBC welfare framework.
  • 105th Amendment (2021) — OBC Reservation — restored states' power to identify OBCs. This is very significant for Bihar — which has a large OBC population.
BPSC Must-Know: Bihar's connection to Constitutional Amendments is deep — the 44th Amendment (1978) was a response to the Emergency, which was strongly resisted in Bihar by Jayaprakash Narayan (Sitabdiara, Saran). Bihar was the first state to implement 50% reservation for women in Panchayati Raj (2006), going beyond the 73rd Amendment. The 106th Amendment (2023) — Women's Reservation — will significantly impact Bihar's political representation.
