Tughlaq Dynasty: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 14 min read · BPSC SSC

The Tughlaq Dynasty (1320–1414 CE) was the third dynasty of the Delhi Sultanate. It produced one of the most fascinating rulers in Indian history — Muhammad bin Tughlaq, whose bold but disastrous experiments earned him the title "the wisest fool in Indian history." His successor Firoz Shah Tughlaq brought stability and built extensively. The dynasty ended with Timur's devastating invasion (1398 CE). For BPSC and SSC, the Tughlaqs are the single most important dynasty of the Delhi Sultanate for objective questions.

Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq (1320–1325 CE) — The Founder

Founder of the Tughlaq Dynasty. He was a capable military commander of Turkish origin who rose to power during the later Khalji period.

  • Titles: Ghazi ("slayer of infidels"), Malik Ghazi.
  • Was appointed governor of the frontier provinces by Alauddin Khalji; later served under Qutb-ud-din Mubarak Shah.
  • Overthrew Khusrau Khan (the last Khalji ruler) in 1320 CE.
  • Restored the prestige of the Sultan and reversed many of Alauddin's harsh policies.
  • Adopted a policy of conciliation with the nobles and the Rajputs.
  • Reformed the judiciary, currency and land revenue systems.
  • Suppressed the rebellion of his own son Prince Jauna Khan (later Muhammad bin Tughlaq) in the Deccan — but pardoned him.
  • Recaptured Warangal and Madurai — restoring Sultanate authority in South India.
  • Built the Tughlaqabad Fort (Delhi) — a massive fortified city, now in ruins.
  • Also built the Tomb of Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq — a fortified tomb near Tughlaqabad, famous for its sloping walls.
  • Died in 1325 CE under suspicious circumstances — a wooden pavilion collapsed on him after a military campaign in Bengal. It is widely believed that his son Muhammad bin Tughlaq arranged his death.
Remember: Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq died in 1325 CE when a wooden pavilion collapsed on him at Afghanpur, near Delhi. Most historians believe his son Muhammad bin Tughlaq was responsible.

Muhammad bin Tughlaq (1325–1351 CE) — The "Wisest Fool"

The most controversial ruler of the Delhi Sultanate. He was a scholar of Persian, Arabic, logic, philosophy and mathematics — yet his grand projects failed spectacularly. Historians call him the "wisest fool in Indian history" or "an educated fool."

Accession

  • Known as Prince Jauna Khan before accession.
  • Took the title Muhammad bin Tughlaq and later Juna Khan.
  • Ruled for 26 years — one of the most eventful reigns in Indian history.

Five Disastrous Projects

The following five projects are the most famous and most frequently asked topics in exams:

1. Transfer of Capital to Daulatabad (1327 CE)

  • Muhammad bin Tughlaq decided to shift the capital from Delhi to Daulatabad (Devagiri) in the Deccan.
  • Reasons: Daulatabad was centrally located, better placed to control South India, and safer from Mongol invasions.
  • He ordered the mass migration of the entire population of Delhi — including nobles, officials, craftsmen and their families.
  • The 700-mile journey on foot killed thousands; many died on the way.
  • The new capital failed — Daulatabad lacked water, and Delhi suffered greatly.
  • By 1330 CE, the Sultan ordered everyone back to Delhi — an equally disastrous return.
  • Result: a huge waste of money, life and royal prestige.

2. Introduction of Token Currency (1329–1330 CE)

  • Muhammad bin Tughlaq introduced copper and brass coins as a substitute for silver Tanka coins — the first experiment of its kind in India.
  • The token coins had the same value as silver coins but were made of cheaper metal.
  • Counterfeiting became rampant — anyone could make copper coins at home.
  • The treasury was flooded with fake coins; the Sultan had to withdraw the scheme within a few years.
  • The royal treasury suffered enormous losses — a serious blow to the economy.
Exam Tip: Muhammad bin Tughlaq's token currency experiment (1329–1330 CE) was the first of its kind in India. It failed due to large-scale forgery. The Sultan had to exchange all copper coins for silver ones at great cost to the treasury.

3. Khurasan Expedition (1327 CE)

  • The Sultan planned to conquer Khurasan (modern Iran/Afghanistan) to counter the Mongol threat.
  • Recruited a huge army and paid them one year's salary in advance.
  • The expedition was abandoned when the Mongol threat receded — the army was disbanded and the money wasted.

4. Qarachil Expedition (1330 CE)

  • Sent an army to conquer Qarachil (Kumaon-Garhwal region) in the Himalayas.
  • The aim was to secure the northern frontier and control trade routes to Tibet.
  • The army suffered heavy losses due to guerrilla attacks, disease and the harsh Himalayan terrain.
  • The survivors returned in a broken condition — a complete military disaster.

5. Taxation in the Doab (1328 CE)

  • To fund his military projects, the Sultan increased land revenue in the Doab (Ganga-Yamuna region) by 10–20 times.
  • He also introduced taxation of grazing and cattle.
  • When the peasants could not pay, they revolted — many fled to the forests and joined bands of rebels.
  • The Sultan then launched a harsh campaign against the fleeing peasants, causing great suffering.
  • When the peasants fled, the land lay uncultivated and the Sultan lost revenue — another failed experiment.
BPSC Must-Know: The five disastrous projects of Muhammad bin Tughlaq — Daulatabad transfer, token currency, Khurasan expedition, Qarachil expedition, Doab taxation — are the most frequently asked topics in BPSC and SSC exams. Remember them in order.

Other Aspects of Muhammad bin Tughlaq's Reign

Military Campaigns

  • Rebellions in the Deccan (Warangal, Dwarasamudra) were suppressed but at great cost.
  • The Sultan's harsh treatment of rebels — including mass executions — alienated the nobility and the provinces.
  • By the end of his reign, several provinces (Bengal, Warangal, Ma'bar) had declared independence.

Administration

  • Introduced a system of direct control over provinces — but this weakened the traditional Iqtadari system.
  • Appointed Muslims only to the highest posts — alienating Hindu officials and the Rajputs.
  • Encouraged agriculture and established a Department of Agriculture (Diwan-i-Kohi) — with loans to peasants, but the scheme failed.
  • Patronised scholars — including Ibn Battuta (Moroccan traveller), who became the Qazi of Delhi.

Society under Muhammad bin Tughlaq

  • Society was deeply divided — Turkish nobles, Indian Muslims, and Hindus.
  • Hindu nobles and Rajputs were increasingly excluded from high offices.
  • Several revolts occurred — the Hindu rebellion in the Deccan, the revolt of the Amiran-i-Sada (100 nobles), and the rebellion of his own nephew.
  • The Sultan's reign was marked by constant warfare and rebellions.

Architecture under Muhammad bin Tughlaq

  • Built the fortress of Adilabad (Delhi).
  • Built the Jahanpanah — a fortified city between Delhi and Siri.
  • Constructed the Tomb of Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq (completed for his father).
  • Built the Kushk-i-Shikar (hunting lodge) and Bijai Mandal at Delhi.
  • Introduced the batter (sloping walls) style in Tughlaq architecture.

Death of Muhammad bin Tughlaq

  • Died in 1351 CE at Thatta (Sindh) while campaigning against rebels.
  • His cousin Firoz Shah Tughlaq succeeded him.

Firoz Shah Tughlaq (1351–1388 CE) — The Builder

Firoz Shah Tughlaq was a benevolent ruler and a great builder — his reign brought stability after the chaos of Muhammad bin Tughlaq. He was the last powerful ruler of the Tughlaq dynasty.

Accession

  • Was the son of a Hindu princess (Rajabibi) and a Muslim noble (Sipahsalar Rajab).
  • Was elected Sultan by the nobles after Muhammad bin Tughlaq's death at Thatta.
  • Adopted a policy of conciliation with the nobles, the Ulema and the army.

Administrative Measures

  • Reversed many of Muhammad bin Tughlaq's unpopular policies.
  • Abolished the harsh taxation in the Doab; reduced taxes on the peasantry.
  • Introduced Jizya and other Islamic taxes strictly, but did not persecute Hindus.
  • Established a Department of Charity (Diwan-i-Khairat) for helping the poor and orphans.
  • Established the Diwan-i-Bandagan (Department of Slaves) — slaves were given grants and trained for administrative and military service.
  • Introduced a system of hereditary lands (Wajh) — grants to officials, soldiers and scholars.
  • Organised a standing army — the first Sultan to recruit Indian Muslims and Hindus on a large scale.

Public Works & Infrastructure

  • Built four major canals for irrigation — the most famous being the one from the Yamuna to Hissar.
  • Built 200 towns and numerous sarai (rest houses) across the empire.
  • Built the Firoz Shah Kotla (Delhi) — a fortress with a famous Ashokan pillar.
  • Repaired and restored the Qutb Minar — after it was damaged by lightning.
  • Brought two Ashokan pillars to Delhi — from Meerut and Topra (Ambala).
  • Built the Kali Masjid, Begumpur Mosque and several other mosques.
  • Established the Firozabad and Hisar towns.
  • Built the Baoli (step-well) at Firoz Shah Kotla — with the famous Ashokan pillar standing on top of it.
Exam Tip: Firoz Shah Tughlaq was the greatest builder among the Delhi Sultans. He built four canals, 200 towns, and brought two Ashokan pillars to Delhi. The Firoz Shah Kotla and Kali Masjid are among his most famous monuments.

Religious Policy

  • Was a devout Muslim and a generous patron of the Ulema.
  • Reimposed the Jizya on non-Muslims.
  • Did not allow women to visit the tombs of saints — a restrictive measure.
  • Ordered the execution of Hindus who refused to pay Jizya.
  • Patronised Islamic scholars; encouraged the study of Islamic law.
  • However, his reign was relatively tolerant — many Hindus served in his administration.

Decline and Death

  • Firoz Shah was a mild ruler but lacked military ambition — he lost Bengal and the Deccan provinces.
  • His policy of religious intolerance alienated many Hindu communities.
  • His method of granting hereditary lands to officials weakened the central authority.
  • He divided his empire among his sons and grandsons — a fatal mistake that led to civil war.
  • Died in 1388 CE; buried in the Tomb of Firoz Shah Tughlaq at Delhi.

Later Tughlaqs (1388–1414 CE)

  • After Firoz Shah's death, the empire was plunged into civil war.
  • Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq II (1388–1389) — ruled briefly; deposed by nobles.
  • Abu Bakr Shah (1389–1390) — ruled from Delhi; overthrown.
  • Nasir-ud-din Muhammad Shah (1390–1394) — faced constant revolts; nobles became powerful.
  • Alauddin Sikandar Shah (1394) — ruled briefly.
  • Nasir-ud-din Mahmud Shah (1394–1413) — last effective Tughlaq; ruled from Delhi.
  • The provinces — Bengal, Gujarat, Malwa, Jaunpur, Khandesh, Bahmani, Vijayanagara — declared independence.
  • The Sultanate was reduced to a small area around Delhi.

Timur's Invasion (1398 CE)

The most devastating event of the later Tughlaq period — a blow from which the Delhi Sultanate never recovered.

  • Timur (Tamerlane) — the Mongol conqueror from Central Asia — invaded India in 1398 CE.
  • Crossed the Indus and marched towards Delhi.
  • Battle of Delhi (December 1398) — Sultan Nasir-ud-din Mahmud Shah was defeated; he fled to Gujarat.
  • Timur's army sacked Delhi for several days — thousands were killed; the city was looted, burned and destroyed.
  • Timur spared the Sayyid and Khokhar nobles; returned to Samarkand via the Punjab.
  • He took vast wealth — including the famous Koh-i-Noor diamond — and thousands of artisans to build his capital at Samarkand.
  • The Delhi Sultanate never recovered from this catastrophe — it became a weakened state.
BPSC Must-Know: Timur's invasion of 1398 CE was the death blow to the Tughlaq dynasty and the Delhi Sultanate. Delhi was sacked, its population massacred, and the Sultanate reduced to a small principality. This invasion paved the way for the Sayyid Dynasty (1414–1451 CE).

Tughlaq Architecture

Tughlaq architecture is marked by simplicity, austerity and boldness — a departure from the ornate style of the Khaljis.

Key Features

  • Sloping walls (batter) — the most distinctive feature of Tughlaq architecture; seen in the Tomb of Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq and Tughlaqabad Fort.
  • Use of grey sandstone and quartzite — darker, less decorative than earlier buildings.
  • Combination of arch and lintel — the true arch is used alongside traditional Indian lintels.
  • Minimal ornamentation — decoration is limited to calligraphy and geometric patterns.
  • Fortified tombs and cities — Tughlaq buildings often have defensive features.
  • Ashokan pillars — Firoz Shah Tughlaq brought them to Delhi, blending them into Islamic structures.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • Bihar was an important province of the Delhi Sultanate throughout the Tughlaq period.
  • Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq campaigned in Bengal and Bihar in 1324–1325 CE and died on his return near Delhi.
  • Muhammad bin Tughlaq appointed governors to Bihar; the province was part of the Bengal-Bihar administrative unit.
  • Bihar Sharif — continued as a major Sufi centre; the Chishti order spread widely in this period.
  • Maner Sharif (near Patna) — flourished as a Sufi centre; became a major pilgrimage site in the Tughlaq period.
  • Firoz Shah Tughlaq is said to have built a canal in Bihar — though records are not clear about its exact location.
  • The Diwan-i-Khairat (Department of Charity) established by Firoz Shah helped the poor across the empire, including Bihar.
  • Several mosques and sarai were built in Bihar during the Tughlaq period — many are now in ruins.
  • After Timur's invasion (1398 CE), Bihar became politically unstable — leading to the rise of local dynasties and eventually the Jaunpur Sultanate and the Sharqi dynasty.
BPSC Must-Know: Ghiyas-ud-din Tughlaq's Bengal campaign (1324–1325 CE) passed through Bihar — it is one of the few recorded military movements of the Tughlaq period in the region. Bihar Sharif and Maner Sharif remained major Sufi centres throughout the Tughlaq period.
