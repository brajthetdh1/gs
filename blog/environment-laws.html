Environment

Environmental Laws & Institutions in India: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 14 min read · BPSC SSC

India has one of the most comprehensive environmental legal frameworks in the developing world. From the Wildlife Protection Act (1972) to the Environment Protection Act (1986), the Biological Diversity Act (2002), and the establishment of the National Green Tribunal (2010) — the legal framework has evolved significantly over the past five decades. For BPSC and SSC, this topic is extremely high-yield — especially the major environmental laws, their years, key features, landmark Supreme Court judgments, and the role of institutions like CPCB, NGT, and NBA. Bihar-specific environmental legal issues are also frequently asked.

Constitutional Provisions for Environmental Protection

Fundamental Rights and DPSP

ArticleProvision
Article 21Right to Life — includes the right to a clean and healthy environment (expanded by judicial interpretation)
Article 48ADirective Principle — State shall endeavour to protect and improve the environment and safeguard forests and wildlife
Article 51A(g)Fundamental Duty — every citizen shall protect and improve the natural environment and have compassion for living creatures

Note: Articles 48A and 51A(g) were added by the 42nd Constitutional Amendment Act, 1976 — during the Emergency — on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee.

Legislative Powers

  • Environmental protection is a subject in the Concurrent List — both Centre and States can legislate.
  • Before the 42nd Amendment (1976), it was in the State List — forests, wildlife, and water were state subjects.
  • The 42nd Amendment moved forests and wildlife from the State List to the Concurrent List.
Exam Tip: Articles 48A and 51A(g) were added by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 — this is one of the most frequently asked BPSC questions. Article 21 has been interpreted by courts to include the right to a clean environment. The 42nd Amendment also moved forests and wildlife to the Concurrent List.

Major Environmental Laws in India

Wildlife Protection Act, 1972

  • The first comprehensive wildlife law in India.
  • Enacted to protect wild animals, birds, and plants — and to establish National Parks and Wildlife Sanctuaries.
  • Established the Central Zoo Authority (CZA) and the Wildlife Crime Control Bureau (WCCB).
  • Created six schedules — with different levels of protection:
    • Schedule I — Highest protection (Tiger, Elephant, Rhino, Lion, etc.).
    • Schedule II — High protection (Monkeys, Langurs, etc.).
    • Schedule III & IV — Protected species (not endangered).
    • Schedule V — Vermin (previously — now removed).
    • Schedule VI — Specified plants.
  • Amended in 1991, 2002, and 2006 — to strengthen enforcement.
  • 2006 Amendment — established the National Tiger Conservation Authority (NTCA) and the Tiger and Other Endangered Species Crime Control Bureau.

Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974

  • The first major environmental law to address pollution.
  • Enacted to prevent and control water pollution — and to maintain or restore the wholesomeness of water.
  • Established the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) and State Pollution Control Boards (SPCBs).
  • Powers: to set standards, inspect industries, and prosecute violators.
  • Amended in 1978 and 1988 — to strengthen enforcement.

Forest Conservation Act, 1980

  • Enacted to restrict the diversion of forest land for non-forest purposes.
  • No forest land can be used for non-forest purposes without the prior approval of the Central Government.
  • Applies to reserved forests, protected forests, and other forest land.
  • Helped curb large-scale deforestation — but has also been criticised for slowing development.
  • Amended in 1988.

Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981

  • Enacted to prevent, control, and abate air pollution.
  • Established the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) and State Pollution Control Boards (SPCBs) for air pollution control.
  • Powers: to set emission standards, inspect industries, and prosecute violators.
  • Extended to Union Territories in 1987.

Environment Protection Act, 1986 — The Umbrella Act

  • Called the "Umbrella Act" — because it covers all aspects of environmental protection.
  • Enacted after the Bhopal Gas Tragedy (1984) — the world's worst industrial disaster.
  • Empowers the Central Government to:
    • Set environmental standards.
    • Regulate industrial activities.
    • Issue directions — including closure of polluting industries.
    • Conduct environmental impact assessments.
  • Basis for several rules:
    • Environment Impact Assessment (EIA) Notification, 2006.
    • Coastal Regulation Zone (CRZ) Notification, 1991.
    • Hazardous Waste Management Rules.
    • Bio-Medical Waste Rules.
    • Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016.
    • E-Waste Management Rules.
    • Plastic Waste Management Rules.
  • Amended in 1991 and 2023.

Biological Diversity Act, 2002

  • Enacted to conserve biological diversity, ensure sustainable use of its components, and ensure fair and equitable sharing of benefits.
  • Established a three-tier structure:
    • National Biodiversity Authority (NBA) — at the national level — headquartered in Chennai.
    • State Biodiversity Boards (SBBs) — at the state level.
    • Biodiversity Management Committees (BMCs) — at the local level.
  • Regulates access to biological resources by foreigners and companies.
  • Implements India's obligations under the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD).

Scheduled Tribes and Other Traditional Forest Dwellers (Recognition of Forest Rights) Act, 2006

  • Also called the Forest Rights Act (FRA).
  • Recognises the rights of forest-dwelling communities — especially Scheduled Tribes — over forest land and resources.
  • Rights include:
    • Right to live in the forest.
    • Right to cultivate forest land.
    • Right to collect and use minor forest produce.
    • Community forest rights.
  • Balances conservation with the rights of forest-dependent communities.

Key Environmental Institutions in India

Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB)

  • Established in 1974 under the Water Act.
  • Functions:
    • Monitor and control water and air pollution.
    • Set environmental standards.
    • Provide technical assistance to SPCBs.
    • Conduct research on pollution control.
    • Launch the Air Quality Index (AQI) in 2014.
  • Headquarters: New Delhi.
  • Comes under the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC).

State Pollution Control Boards (SPCBs)

  • Established in each state under the Water Act (1974) and Air Act (1981).
  • Functions: monitor pollution, issue consents, enforce standards, inspect industries.
  • Bihar's SPCB is the Bihar State Pollution Control Board (BSPCB).

National Green Tribunal (NGT)

  • Established in 2010 under the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010.
  • A specialised court for environmental cases — to ensure effective and expeditious disposal of cases relating to environmental protection and conservation.
  • Not bound by the Code of Civil Procedure — guided by principles of natural justice.
  • Powers to:
    • Hear cases on environmental issues.
    • Order compensation and relief.
    • Order restoration of the environment.
  • Principal Bench: New Delhi. Other benches: Bhopal, Pune, Kolkata, Chennai.
  • Appeals against NGT orders go to the Supreme Court.

National Biodiversity Authority (NBA)

  • Established in 2003 under the Biological Diversity Act, 2002.
  • Headquarters: Chennai.
  • Functions:
    • Regulate access to biological resources.
    • Ensure fair and equitable sharing of benefits.
    • Advise the Government on biodiversity conservation.

National Tiger Conservation Authority (NTCA)

  • Established in 2006 — under the Wildlife Protection (Amendment) Act, 2006.
  • Chaired by the Union Minister of Environment, Forest and Climate Change.
  • Functions:
    • Oversee Project Tiger.
    • Approve Tiger Conservation Plans.
    • Ensure tiger reserves are managed scientifically.

Central Zoo Authority (CZA)

  • Established in 1992 — under the Wildlife Protection Act.
  • Functions: regulate zoos, set standards, recognise zoos, and coordinate breeding programmes for endangered species.

Wildlife Crime Control Bureau (WCCB)

  • Established in 2007 — under the Wildlife Protection Act.
  • Functions: combat wildlife crime and illegal wildlife trade.

Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change (MoEFCC)

  • The nodal ministry for environmental protection in India.
  • Formulated in 1985 (as Ministry of Environment and Forests) — renamed in 2014.
  • Formulates environmental policies, laws and programmes.
  • Represents India at international environmental forums.

Forest Survey of India (FSI)

  • Established in 1981 — headquartered in Dehradun.
  • Functions: assess forest cover of the country — publishes the India State of Forest Report (ISFR) every two years.

Zoological Survey of India (ZSI)

  • Established in 1916 — headquartered in Kolkata.
  • Functions: survey and research on India's fauna.

Botanical Survey of India (BSI)

  • Established in 1890 — headquartered in Kolkata.
  • Functions: survey and research on India's flora.

Landmark Supreme Court Judgments on Environment

Rural Litigation and Entitlement Kendra (RLEK) v. State of UP (1985)

  • First case to establish the principle of sustainable development in Indian environmental law.
  • Concerned limestone quarrying in the Doon Valley — the Court ordered closure of some quarries to protect the environment.
  • Introduced the concept of "Sustainable Development" — development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations.

M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1986) — Oleum Gas Leak Case

  • Evolved the principle of Absolute Liability — industries engaged in hazardous activities are absolutely liable for any harm caused, regardless of negligence.
  • This was a departure from the Strict Liability principle of Rylands v. Fletcher (UK).
  • Also evolved the "Polluter Pays Principle" and the "Precautionary Principle".

M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987) — Ganga Pollution Case

  • Concerned pollution of the Ganga by tanneries in Kanpur.
  • The Supreme Court ordered the closure of polluting tanneries and directed them to install effluent treatment plants.
  • Led to the establishment of the Ganga Action Plan.

Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum v. Union of India (1996)

  • Concerned pollution from tanneries in Tamil Nadu.
  • Established the "Precautionary Principle" and the "Polluter Pays Principle" as part of Indian environmental law.
  • Also established the principle of sustainable development.

Virender Gaur v. State of Haryana (1995)

  • Held that the right to a clean and healthy environment is part of the Right to Life under Article 21.

Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar (1991)

  • The Supreme Court held that the right to life under Article 21 includes the right to enjoy pollution-free water and air.
  • This is a landmark judgment from Bihar — frequently asked in BPSC.

M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1998) — Taj Trapezium Case

  • Concerned air pollution affecting the Taj Mahal at Agra.
  • The Supreme Court ordered the closure of polluting industries in the Taj Trapezium Zone and directed the use of cleaner fuels.

M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (2002) — CNG Case

  • The Supreme Court ordered the conversion of all public transport in Delhi to CNG — to reduce air pollution.
  • Led to a significant improvement in Delhi's air quality in the early 2000s.

Vodafone International Holdings B.V. v. Union of India (2012)

  • Notable for the Court's observation on the Precautionary Principle and sustainable development.

Other Important Judgments

  • T.N. Godavarman Thirumulpad v. Union of India (1996) — the "Forest Case" — expansive interpretation of "forest" under the Forest Conservation Act.
  • M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath (1997) — Public Trust Doctrine — the State holds natural resources in trust for the public.
  • A.P. Pollution Control Board v. Prof. M.V. Nayudu (1999) — Precautionary Principle and Polluter Pays Principle.
  • Research Foundation for Science Technology and Natural Resource Policy v. Union of India (2005) — hazardous waste management.
BPSC Must-Know: The three key environmental principles — Sustainable Development (RLEK case, 1985), Absolute Liability (Oleum Gas Leak case, 1986), and Precautionary Principle + Polluter Pays Principle (Vellore Citizens case, 1996). The Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar (1991) case is a landmark Bihar judgment — right to pollution-free water and air is part of Article 21.

Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA)

  • EIA is a process to assess the environmental impact of a proposed project before it is approved.
  • Introduced in India in 1994 — under the Environment Protection Act.
  • EIA Notification, 2006 — the current framework.
  • Projects are categorised as:
    • Category A — handled by the Central Government (MoEFCC).
    • Category B — handled by the State Government.
  • EIA process includes:
    • Screening — determining the level of EIA required.
    • Scoping — defining the scope of the study.
    • Baseline data collection.
    • Impact prediction and assessment.
    • Public consultation.
    • Environmental Management Plan (EMP).
    • Appraisal and clearance.

National Environment Policy

  • National Environment Policy (NEP), 2006 — a comprehensive policy framework for environmental protection.
  • Key principles:
    • Human beings at the centre of sustainable development.
    • Precautionary Principle.
    • Polluter Pays Principle.
    • Public Trust Doctrine.
    • Sustainable Development.
  • Objectives: conservation, sustainable use, equitable sharing, and improved environmental governance.

Environmental Clearance

  • Environmental Clearance (EC) is required for certain categories of projects before they can be set up.
  • Granted by the MoEFCC (Category A) or the State Environment Impact Assessment Authority (SEIAA) (Category B).
  • Based on the EIA and the Environmental Management Plan.
  • Public consultation is a key part of the process.
  • Coastal Regulation Zone (CRZ) clearance is required for projects in coastal areas.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

Bihar's Environmental Legal Framework

  • Bihar follows all central environmental laws — including the Wildlife Protection Act, Water Act, Air Act, EPA, and Biological Diversity Act.
  • Bihar State Pollution Control Board (BSPCB) — established in 1974 under the Water Act — is the state's pollution regulator.
  • Bihar State Biodiversity Board (BSBB) — established under the Biological Diversity Act, 2002 — for biodiversity conservation in the state.
  • Bihar State Environment Impact Assessment Authority (SEIAA) — for environmental clearances for Category B projects in Bihar.
  • Bihar Forest Department — manages the state's forests and wildlife.

Landmark Bihar Environmental Cases

  • Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar (1991) — the Supreme Court held that the right to life under Article 21 includes the right to enjoy pollution-free water and air. This is a landmark Bihar case — frequently asked in BPSC.
  • Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979) — primarily a prison reform case, but it also established the link between Article 21 and the quality of life — including environmental quality.
  • M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987) — the Ganga Pollution Case — the Supreme Court ordered the closure of polluting tanneries and directed the establishment of STPs — affecting Bihar's Ganga clean-up efforts.

Bihar's Environmental Issues and Legal Actions

  • Ganga Pollution — Bihar has been part of the Namami Gange Programme — with several STPs constructed under the NGT's directives and central funding.
  • Flood-Related Pollution — recurring floods in north Bihar (Kosi, Gandak, Bagmati) cause water contamination — legal actions have focused on flood management and water quality.
  • Arsenic Contamination — parts of Bihar (Bhojpur, Patna, Saran, Samastipur, Bhagalpur) face arsenic contamination in groundwater — the state has installed Arsenic Removal Plants (ARPs).
  • Air Pollution in Patna — Patna has been among India's most polluted cities — the NGT has taken suo motu cognisance of air quality issues in Bihar's cities.
  • Solid Waste Management — Bihar has been implementing the Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016 — with a focus on waste segregation, processing, and disposal.
  • Plastic Ban — Bihar has implemented the single-use plastic ban — in line with the central notification.

Bihar's Environmental Programmes

  • Jal-Jeevan-Hariyali — flagship state programme for water conservation and tree plantation.
  • Mission Harit Bihar — for increasing tree cover in the state.
  • Namami Gange Programme in Bihar — cleaning the Ganga — several STPs constructed.
  • Bihar Water Policy — for water resource management.
  • Bihar Plastic Ban — ban on single-use plastics.
  • Swachh Bharat Mission — for sanitation and cleanliness.
  • Solar Energy Policy — for promoting solar power.
  • Bihar Agriculture Roadmap — includes sustainable agriculture practices.
  • Wetland Conservation Programme — for Bihar's wetlands (Kanwar Lake, Gogabeel).
BPSC Must-Know: The Subhash Kumar v. State of Bihar (1991) judgment — right to pollution-free water and air is part of Article 21 — is a landmark Bihar case for BPSC. Bihar State Pollution Control Board (BSPCB) was established in 1974. Bihar State Biodiversity Board (BSBB) was established under the Biological Diversity Act, 2002. Jal-Jeevan-Hariyali and Mission Harit Bihar are the state's flagship environmental programmes. These are frequently asked BPSC questions.
