Moderates and Extremists: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 14 min read · BPSC SSC

The Indian National Congress from its foundation in 1885 until the Surat Split of 1907 was dominated by the Moderates — leaders who believed in gradual reform, constitutional methods, and faith in British justice. But by the early 20th century, a new generation of leaders — the Extremists — emerged with a more radical approach. The Partition of Bengal (1905) was the immediate trigger that transformed Indian nationalism. The split at Surat (1907) was the most serious crisis in the INC's early history — it took nine years and the Lucknow Pact (1916) to reunite the two factions.

Exam Tip: The three great Extremist leaders are called Lal–Bal–Pal: Lala Lajpat Rai (Lal), Bal Gangadhar Tilak (Bal), Bipin Chandra Pal (Pal). They were the architects of the Extremist phase and led the Swadeshi Movement. This is one of the most frequently asked facts.

The Immediate Trigger — Partition of Bengal (1905)

The Partition of Bengal in 1905 was the single most important event that transformed Indian nationalism and gave rise to the Extremist phase.

Background

  • Lord Curzon was the Viceroy of India (1899–1905) — a firm believer in the "divide and rule" policy.
  • Bengal was the largest province in British India — with a population of about 8.5 crore.
  • Curzon proposed to divide Bengal into two provinces — ostensibly for administrative convenience.
  • The real aim was to weaken the nationalist movement by dividing the Bengali population.

The Partition

  • Announced: July 1905; Implemented: 16 October 1905.
  • Bengal was divided into:
    • Eastern Bengal and Assam — with Dhaka as the capital; Muslim-majority.
    • Western Bengal — with Calcutta as the capital; Hindu-majority.
  • The government claimed the partition was for administrative efficiency, but the real aim was to create a Muslim-majority province and weaken the Congress.
  • The Bengali Hindus were particularly angry — they saw it as an attack on their cultural and political unity.
  • Even the Bengali Muslims, initially receptive, were divided — many joined the anti-partition movement.
BPSC Must-Know: The Partition of Bengal (1905) was implemented by Lord Curzon. It divided Bengal into Eastern Bengal & Assam (Muslim-majority, capital Dhaka) and Western Bengal (Hindu-majority, capital Calcutta). The partition was annulled in 1911 after the Swadeshi Movement.

Swadeshi and Boycott Movement (1905–1908)

The Swadeshi Movement — the first mass movement in India — was the response to the Partition of Bengal. It marked the beginning of the Extremist phase.

Key Features

  • Swadeshi — buying and using Indian-made goods.
  • Boycott — refusing to buy British goods, especially cloth.
  • National education — setting up Indian schools and colleges (e.g. Bengal National College).
  • Cultural revival — songs, festivals, and literature promoted Indian identity.
  • Passive resistance — non-cooperation, strikes, and picketing.
  • Mass mobilisation — for the first time, women, students, and the working class joined the movement.

Methods Used

  • Boycott of foreign goods — especially Manchester cloth.
  • Bonfires of foreign cloth — symbolic rejection of British goods.
  • Picketing of shops selling foreign goods.
  • Promotion of swadeshi industries — handloom, charkha, soap, matches, etc.
  • National education — schools and colleges with an Indian curriculum.
  • Use of local languages — Bengali, Marathi, Tamil for political propaganda.
  • Folk festivals — Rabindranath Tagore's Raksha Bandhan (bond of unity) and Vande Mataram as a national song.
  • Boycott of British courts and government schools.

Leaders of the Swadeshi Movement

  • Bal Gangadhar Tilak — Maharashtra; called the "Father of Indian Unrest" by the British.
  • Lala Lajpat Rai — Punjab; called "Punjab Kesari".
  • Bipin Chandra Pal — Bengal; the "Pal" of Lal–Bal–Pal.
  • Aurobindo Ghosh — Bengal; called for complete independence (Purna Swaraj).
  • Rabindranath Tagore — composed songs for the movement; started the Raksha Bandhan festival.
  • Surendranath Banerjee — Moderate leader who supported the boycott.
  • Ashwini Kumar Dutt — led the movement in Barisal.
  • Sister Nivedita — Irish disciple of Vivekananda; promoted swadeshi in Bengal.

Result — Annulment of Partition (1911)

  • The Swadeshi Movement forced the British to annul the Partition of Bengal in 1911.
  • King George V announced the annulment at the Delhi Durbar (December 1911).
  • However, the capital was shifted from Calcutta to Delhi — a sop to the British and a blow to Bengali pride.
  • Bihar and Orissa were separated from Bengal as a new province — this was the origin of modern Bihar as a separate province.
Exam Tip: The Partition of Bengal was annulled in 1911. At the same time, the capital was shifted from Calcutta to Delhi, and Bihar and Orissa were separated from Bengal — creating the modern province of Bihar. This is a crucial BPSC fact.

Key Extremist Leaders

Bal Gangadhar Tilak (1856–1920)

The most prominent Extremist leader — called "Lokmanya" (beloved of the people) and the "Father of Indian Unrest" by the British.

  • Born in Ratnagiri, Maharashtra in 1856.
  • Famous declaration: "Swaraj is my birthright, and I shall have it."
  • Started the newspapers Kesari (Marathi) and Maratha (English).
  • Used religious festivals — Ganesh Chaturthi (1893) and Shivaji Jayanti — as platforms for nationalist propaganda.
  • Founded the Deccan Education Society (1884) with Gopal Ganesh Agarkar.
  • Was tried for sedition three times — imprisoned for 6 years in Mandalay (Burma) in 1908.
  • Famous trial quote: "Swarajya is my birthright and I shall have it."
  • Founded the Home Rule League (April 1916) at Poona.
  • Played a key role in the Lucknow Pact (1916) — reuniting the Moderates and Extremists.
  • Called the "Father of Indian Nationalism" by many historians.
  • Died on 1 August 1920 in Bombay.

Lala Lajpat Rai (1865–1928)

  • Called "Punjab Kesari" (Lion of Punjab) and "Sher-e-Punjab".
  • Born in Dhudike, Punjab in 1865.
  • Was one of the first leaders to demand complete independence.
  • Deported to Mandalay (Burma) in 1907 without trial.
  • Founded the Servants of the People Society (1921).
  • Founded the Punjab National Bank (1894) along with other leaders.
  • Led the protest against the Simon Commission (1928) at Lahore.
  • Was fatally injured in a lathi charge during the Simon Commission protest (30 October 1928) and died on 17 November 1928.
  • His last words: "Every blow on my body will prove a nail in the coffin of the British Empire."

Bipin Chandra Pal (1858–1932)

  • Born in Habiganj, Sylhet (now Bangladesh) in 1858.
  • Called the "Father of Revolutionary Thoughts in India".
  • Was a brilliant orator and writer.
  • Advocated complete independence and the boycott of British goods.
  • Was a strong advocate of swadeshi and national education.
  • Started the newspaper Bande Mataram with Aurobindo Ghosh.
  • Later parted ways with Tilak and joined the Moderates — a rare reversal.
  • Died in 1932 in Calcutta.

Aurobindo Ghosh (1872–1950)

  • Born in Calcutta; educated in England (Cambridge).
  • Was the first leader to publicly demand complete independence (Purna Swaraj) in 1907.
  • Started the newspaper Bande Mataram with Bipin Chandra Pal.
  • Was involved in the Alipore Bomb Case (1908) — acquitted after a famous defense.
  • Later renounced politics and became a spiritual leader — founded the Sri Aurobindo Ashram at Pondicherry (1926).
  • Wrote Savitri, The Life Divine, and other spiritual works.
BPSC Must-Know: Bal Gangadhar Tilak — "Lokmanya Tilak" — said "Swaraj is my birthright, and I shall have it." He started the newspapers Kesari and Maratha and founded the Home Rule League (1916). He is the most important Extremist leader for BPSC.

The Surat Split (1907)

The Surat Split was the formal division of the Indian National Congress into two factions — the Moderates and the Extremists. It was the most serious crisis in the INC's early history.

Background

  • The Moderates and Extremists disagreed on the methods and goals of the Congress.
  • The Extremists wanted Swaraj (complete independence); the Moderates wanted reforms within the British Empire.
  • The Extremists wanted mass mobilisation and agitation; the Moderates preferred constitutional methods.
  • The immediate issue: the presidentship of the 1906 Calcutta session. The Extremists wanted Tilak; the Moderates wanted Dadabhai Naoroji. The Moderates won — Naoroji was elected, and he declared Swaraj as the goal of the Congress (a compromise).
  • The 1907 session was to be held at Nagpur under Tilak's presidency. The Moderates objected and moved the session to Surat — with a Moderate president.

The Split

  • The Surat session was held on 26 December 1907.
  • Rash Behari Ghosh was proposed as president by the Moderates; Tilak was proposed by the Extremists.
  • The session turned violent — chairs were thrown, and the police had to intervene.
  • The Extremists were expelled from the Congress. They formed their own faction.
  • Tilak was later arrested and sentenced to 6 years' imprisonment in Mandalay (1908).

Consequences of the Split

  • The Congress was weakened and divided for nine years (1907–1916).
  • The Extremists lost their leaders — Tilak was in prison, Lajpat Rai was deported, Bipin Chandra Pal retired from politics, and Aurobindo Ghosh withdrew to spiritual life.
  • The Moderates continued to dominate the Congress — but they lost touch with the masses.
  • The British took advantage of the split — the Morley–Minto Reforms (1909) were introduced, giving limited concessions.
  • The split also coincided with the rise of Revolutionary Nationalism (armed struggle) — as many young Indians turned to violence after the failure of the Extremists.
Exam Tip: The Surat Split (26 December 1907) divided the Congress into Moderates and Extremists. It was reunified at the Lucknow Session (1916) — thanks to the efforts of Tilak, Annie Besant, and the Lucknow Pact. This is one of the most frequently asked Modern History topics.

Reunification — Lucknow Session (1916)

The Congress was reunified at the Lucknow Session in December 1916 — after nine years of division.

Reasons for Reunification

  • The Moderates realised that without the Extremists, the Congress had lost its mass base.
  • The Extremists realised that without the Moderates, they lacked organisation and resources.
  • The First World War (1914–1918) created a new political atmosphere — Indians expected reforms in return for their support.
  • The Home Rule Movement (1916) brought Tilak and Annie Besant together — both wanted a united Congress.
  • The Lucknow Pact (1916) between the Congress and the Muslim League created a united front — for the first time, both organisations agreed on a common political programme.

The Lucknow Pact (1916)

  • Signed between the Indian National Congress and the All-India Muslim League at Lucknow in December 1916.
  • Congress was represented by Ambica Charan Mazumdar (president).
  • Muslim League was represented by Muhammad Ali Jinnah and others.
  • Key provisions:
    • Both organisations agreed to demand Swaraj (self-government) for India.
    • Separate electorates for Muslims were accepted by the Congress.
    • Muslims were to have one-third representation in the Central Legislative Council.
    • Muslims were given weightage in provinces where they were a minority.
    • Both agreed to demand more Indian representation in the government.
  • The Lucknow Pact was a landmark in Hindu–Muslim unity — it was the high point of Congress–League cooperation.

Home Rule Movement (1916–1918)

After the Surat Split, the Indian nationalist movement needed a new direction. The Home Rule Movement provided it — and it was the immediate precursor to the Gandhian era.

Two Home Rule Leagues

LeaderLeagueFoundedArea
Bal Gangadhar TilakIndian Home Rule LeagueApril 1916Poona, Maharashtra, Karnataka, Central Provinces, Berar
Annie BesantHome Rule LeagueSeptember 1916Madras, Bombay, rest of India

Objectives

  • To achieve Swaraj (self-government) for India within the British Empire.
  • To promote political education and awareness among Indians.
  • To build a mass movement for self-rule.
  • To unite Moderates and Extremists under a common platform.

Methods

  • Public meetings, speeches, and discussions.
  • Reading rooms, libraries, and newspapers (The Commonweal by Annie Besant, New India).
  • Petitions and demands to the British government.
  • Youth leagues and student participation.

Impact

  • Revived the nationalist movement after the Surat Split.
  • Prepared the ground for Gandhi's Non-Cooperation Movement.
  • Made Indians aware of the demand for Swaraj.
  • Annie Besant became the first woman president of the INC in 1917 (Calcutta session).
  • The movement was eventually merged into the Congress after the Lucknow Session (1916).
BPSC Must-Know: The Home Rule Movement (1916) was launched by Tilak (April 1916, Poona) and Annie Besant (September 1916, Madras). It revived the nationalist movement after the Surat Split. Annie Besant became the first woman president of the INC (1917).

Morley–Minto Reforms (1909)

The Morley–Minto Reforms were introduced by the British in response to the growing nationalist movement. They were the first attempt to introduce partial representation of Indians in the government.

Key Provisions

  • Indian Councils Act, 1909 — increased the size of the Legislative Councils.
  • Indians were given representation in the Imperial Legislative Council and provincial councils.
  • Separate electorates were introduced for Muslims — a major concession to the Muslim League.
  • Election of some members was allowed — through a system of indirect elections.
  • Non-official members could ask questions and discuss the budget.
  • The Governor-General retained the power to override any decision.
  • Indians were admitted to the executive councils for the first time — Satyendra Prasad Sinha became the first Indian to join the Viceroy's Executive Council.

Significance

  • First step towards representative government in India.
  • Introduced the principle of separate electorates — which later became a major cause of communal division.
  • Failed to satisfy the Moderates — the reforms were too limited.
  • Failed to satisfy the Extremists — they wanted Swaraj, not token representation.

Revolutionary Nationalism — A Brief Overview

While the Congress debated Moderates vs Extremists, another strand of nationalism emerged — Revolutionary Nationalism, which believed in armed struggle to overthrow British rule.

Key Features

  • Believed in the overthrow of British rule by force.
  • Organised secret societies — Anushilan Samiti (Bengal), Abhinav Bharat (Maharashtra).
  • Used bombs, assassinations, and armed robberies to fund and spread the movement.
  • Received inspiration from European revolutionary movements (Irish, Russian).
  • Received support from Indian communities abroad — in USA, UK, Europe, and Southeast Asia.

Key Revolutionaries

  • V.D. Savarkar (1883–1966) — founded Abhinav Bharat (1904); wrote The Indian War of Independence (1909).
  • Khudiram Bose (1889–1908) — executed at age 18 for the Muzaffarpur bombing.
  • Bhagat Singh (1907–1931) — the most famous revolutionary; executed on 23 March 1931.
  • Batukeshwar Dutt — threw bombs in the Central Legislative Assembly (1929) with Bhagat Singh.
  • Chandrashekhar Azad (1906–1931) — died fighting at Alfred Park, Allahabad.
  • Surya Sen (1894–1934) — led the Chittagong Armoury Raid (1930).
  • Ashfaqullah Khan — with Ram Prasad Bismil, involved in the Kakori Conspiracy (1925).

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • Bihar's separation from Bengal (1912) — the Swadeshi Movement led to the annulment of the Partition of Bengal (1911), and in 1912, Bihar and Orissa were separated from Bengal as a separate province. This is a landmark event in Bihar's administrative history.
  • Bihar Provincial Conference (1908) — the first major political conference in Bihar; leaders from different parts of India attended.
  • Sachchidananda Sinha (1871–1950) — leading lawyer and politician from Bihar; attended several INC sessions; became the first president of the Constituent Assembly (1946).
  • Dr. Rajendra Prasad (1884–1963) — born in Zeradei (Siwan, Bihar); joined the INC in 1911; became a key leader of the freedom struggle in Bihar.
  • Sri Krishna Sinha (1887–1961) — "Bihar Kesari"; joined the INC during the Swadeshi era; became the first Chief Minister of Bihar.
  • Anugrah Narayan Sinha (1887–1957) — "Bihar Vibhuti"; a major INC leader from Bihar.
  • Champaran Satyagraha (1917) — Mahatma Gandhi's first satyagraha in India, fought in Bihar against the indigo planters. Dr. Rajendra Prasad played a key role in inviting Gandhi to Champaran.
  • Bihar's role in the Home Rule Movement — several Bihari leaders joined the Home Rule League and participated in its activities.
BPSC Must-Know: Bihar was separated from Bengal in 1912 — one of the most important administrative events in Bihar's history. Sachchidananda Sinha — from Bihar — became the first president of the Constituent Assembly (1946). Dr. Rajendra Prasad, Sri Krishna Sinha, and Anugrah Narayan Sinha were the key leaders of the INC in Bihar during this period.
