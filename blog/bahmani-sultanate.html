Bahmani Sultanate: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 13 min read · BPSC SSC

The Bahmani Sultanate (1347–1527 CE) was the first independent Muslim kingdom of the Deccan. Founded by Ala-ud-din Bahman Shah (Hasan Gangu) in revolt against Muhammad bin Tughlaq, it was the great rival of the Vijayanagara Empire for nearly two centuries. At its peak, the Bahmani Sultanate controlled most of the Deccan plateau. Its eventual disintegration into the five Deccan Sultanates set the stage for the Battle of Talikota (1565 CE) that destroyed Vijayanagara. For BPSC and SSC, the Bahmanis are essential for questions on Deccan history, Indo-Islamic culture and the rise of regional powers.

Foundation of the Bahmani Sultanate

  • Founded in 1347 CE by Ala-ud-din Bahman Shah (also known as Hasan Gangu).
  • Hasan Gangu was a former officer of Muhammad bin Tughlaq who rebelled against the Sultanate's harsh policies in the Deccan.
  • The immediate cause of rebellion was the oppressive taxation and the Sultan's decision to transfer the capital to Daulatabad.
  • The name "Bahmani" comes from Bahman — a legendary Persian hero from the Shahnameh.
  • Capital: initially Daulatabad, later moved to Gulbarga (Kalaburagi) in 1347 CE, and finally to Bidar in 1425 CE.
  • Hasan Gangu took the title Ala-ud-din Bahman Shah and ruled until 1358 CE.

Rulers of the Bahmani Sultanate

The Bahmani Sultanate was ruled by 18 rulers over 180 years, spanning five family lines: Hasan Shahi, Muhammad Shahi, Firoz Shahi, Ahmad Shahi, and Mahmud Shahi.

Ala-ud-din Bahman Shah (1347–1358 CE) — The Founder

  • Born as Hasan Gangu; was an officer in the army of Muhammad bin Tughlaq.
  • Was appointed governor of Daulatabad — but rebelled against the Sultanate's policies in the Deccan.
  • Declared independence in 1347 CE and assumed the title Ala-ud-din Bahman Shah.
  • Made Gulbarga his capital — the first Bahmani capital.
  • Divided the kingdom into four provinces (Tarafs) — Daulatabad, Berar, Bidar, and Gulbarga.
  • Fought against the Hindu kingdom of Warangal and the Vijayanagara Empire.
  • Established a strong, centralised administration — the model was followed by his successors.
  • Patronised Persian culture and encouraged the migration of scholars from Persia and Central Asia.
  • Died in 1358 CE and was succeeded by his son Muhammad Shah I.
Remember: Ala-ud-din Bahman Shah (Hasan Gangu) founded the Bahmani Sultanate in 1347 CE with Gulbarga as his first capital. He divided the kingdom into four Tarafs (provinces).

Firoz Shah Bahmani (1397–1422 CE) — The Greatest Ruler

The most powerful and cultured ruler of the Bahmani Sultanate. His reign is considered the golden age of Bahmani history.

  • Was the son of Ahmad Shah Wali; ascended the throne at a young age.
  • Defeated the Vijayanagara Empire multiple times — the famous Battle of Raichur was fought during this period.
  • Married a Vijayanagara princess — a rare instance of inter-dynastic marriage.
  • Defeated the Sultanate of Malwa, Gujarat and the Hindu kingdom of Warangal.
  • Extended Bahmani control over the entire Deccan — from the Narmada to the Tungabhadra.
  • Was a great patron of art, literature and architecture.
  • Encouraged Persian and Deccani culture — his court was a meeting point of Persian, Turkish, Arabic and Indian traditions.
  • Was known for his religious tolerance — he had Hindu ministers and commanders in his court.
  • His reign saw the construction of many mosques, madrasas and palaces.
  • Died in 1422 CE; succeeded by his brother Ahmad Shah Wali.

Ahmad Shah Wali (1422–1436 CE)

  • Shifted the capital from Gulbarga to Bidar in 1425 CE — Bidar remained the capital until the end of the Sultanate.
  • Fought against Vijayanagara, Warangal and the Sultanate of Malwa.
  • Was a devout Muslim — imposed the Jizya strictly and persecuted Hindus.
  • However, he also patronised art and architecture — built the Bidar Fort and the famous Madrasa of Mahmud Gawan (completed by his successor).
  • His reign marked the beginning of the decline of Bahmani power — the nobles became increasingly powerful.
  • Died in 1436 CE.

Mahmud Gawan (1463–1481 CE) — The Great Minister

Mahmud Gawan was the Prime Minister (Wazir) of Muhammad Shah III and the most famous figure of the Bahmani Sultanate. His tenure is considered the high point of Bahmani administration and culture.

  • Was a Persian noble who came to the Deccan as a trader and rose to become the Wazir.
  • Served as Prime Minister under Muhammad Shah III (1463–1482 CE).
  • Reformed the administration — divided the kingdom into eight Tarafs (provinces) instead of four.
  • Reorganised the revenue system — introduced proper measurement and assessment.
  • Reformed the army — introduced new recruitment, training and payment systems.
  • Built the famous Madrasa of Mahmud Gawan at Bidar — one of the finest examples of Bahmani architecture.
  • Patronised poets, scholars, scientists and artists — his court was a cultural centre.
  • Successfully campaigned against Vijayanagara, Orissa and the Sultanate of Malwa.
  • Was executed in 1481 CE on false charges of treason — the nobles had conspired against him because of his reforms.
  • His death marked the beginning of the rapid decline of the Bahmani Sultanate.
Exam Tip: Mahmud Gawan was the greatest Wazir of the Bahmani Sultanate. He divided the kingdom into eight Tarafs, reformed the revenue and army, and built the famous Madrasa of Mahmud Gawan at Bidar. He was executed in 1481 CE — a major turning point in Bahmani history.

Bahmani Administration

Central Administration

  • Sultan — head of state; his authority was limited by the powerful nobles.
  • Wazir — Prime Minister; the most important minister.
  • Amir-ul-Umara — Commander-in-Chief of the army.
  • Wakil-us-Sultanat — Deputy of the Sultan.
  • Dabir — Minister of Correspondence.
  • Qazi — Chief Judge; Mufti — legal advisor.

Provincial Administration

  • The kingdom was divided into Tarafs (provinces) — initially four, later eight under Mahmud Gawan.
  • Each Taraf was governed by a Tarafdar — a powerful noble with military and civil authority.
  • The Tarafdars often became independent-minded and challenged central authority — a major cause of Bahmani decline.
  • Below the Tarafs were Parganas and villages.

Revenue Administration

  • Land revenue was the main source of income — usually 1/3 to 1/2 of produce.
  • Mahmud Gawan introduced proper measurement (Masahat) of land.
  • Taxes on trade, cattle, irrigation and other economic activities.
  • Jizya was imposed on non-Muslims.

Military Administration

  • The Bahmani army was large and well-equipped — including infantry, cavalry, elephants and artillery.
  • Bahmani rulers recruited Persian, Turkish, Arab and Indian soldiers.
  • Mahmud Gawan introduced reforms — fixed payment for soldiers, proper recruitment, and a strong intelligence system.
  • The Bahmanis were among the first Indian rulers to use firearms and artillery extensively.

The Nobility — The Great Weakness

  • The Bahmani nobility was divided into two rival factions:
    • Afagis (Pardesis) — nobles of foreign origin (Persian, Turkish, Arab).
    • Deccanis (Dakhnis) — nobles of local origin (Indian Muslims, converts).
  • The rivalry between these two factions was a constant source of instability.
  • Mahmud Gawan — an Afagi — was a victim of Deccani noble conspiracy.
  • The nobles' power and the Tarafdars' independence ultimately led to the break-up of the Sultanate.
BPSC Must-Know: The Afagis vs Deccanis rivalry is the single most important cause of the decline of the Bahmani Sultanate. The nobility's factionalism and the Tarafdars' independence led to the break-up of the kingdom into five Sultanates.

Bahmani–Vijayanagara Conflict

The rivalry between the Bahmani Sultanate and the Vijayanagara Empire was one of the longest and most consequential in South Indian history.

Causes of the Conflict

  • Territorial disputes — especially the Raichur Doab (fertile land between the Krishna and Tungabhadra rivers).
  • Control of trade routes — especially the routes to the west coast ports.
  • Religious differences — Hindu vs Muslim kingdoms.
  • Mutual interference — both kingdoms interfered in each other's internal politics.

Bahmani Art & Architecture

The Bahmani Sultanate developed a distinctive Deccani style of Indo-Islamic architecture — a blend of Persian, Turkish and local Indian traditions.

Key Features

  • Bidar style — a distinctive style of architecture that developed at Bidar.
  • Use of coloured tiles and glazed bricks.
  • Bidriware — a unique metalwork technique (silver inlay on black metal) that became famous as Bidriware.
  • Domes with high drums — a distinctive feature of Bahmani architecture.
  • Calligraphy — Persian inscriptions used for decoration.
  • Char Bagh layout for gardens around tombs.

Society & Culture

  • Bahmani society was cosmopolitan — Persians, Turks, Arabs, Africans and local Indians lived together.
  • Persian was the court language — but Deccani Urdu (a blend of Urdu, Marathi, Kannada and Telugu) also developed.
  • Sufism flourished — the Chishti and Shattari orders were influential.
  • Bhakti movement — saints like Namdev and Tukaram were active in the Deccan.
  • Literature — Persian and Deccani literature flourished; Firishta and other historians wrote in this period.
  • Music — the Bahmani court patronised both Persian and Indian music.
  • Bidriware, Bidar carpets and other crafts flourished.
  • The Bahmanis were tolerant of Hinduism — many Hindus served in the administration and army.

Decline of the Bahmani Sultanate

Causes

  • Afagi vs Deccani rivalry — the constant conflict between foreign and local nobles weakened the Sultanate.
  • Execution of Mahmud Gawan (1481 CE) — removed the last capable administrator.
  • Weak successors — after Muhammad Shah III, the Sultans were weak and controlled by nobles.
  • Independent Tarafdars — the governors of the eight Tarafs became virtually independent.
  • Constant wars with Vijayanagara — drained the treasury and the army.
  • Rise of the five Deccan Sultanates — the Tarafdars gradually established their own dynasties.

The Five Deccan Sultanates

By 1527 CE, the Bahmani Sultanate had completely disintegrated into five independent Sultanates — collectively called the Deccan Sultanates.

The Battle of Talikota (1565 CE)

  • Fought between the four Deccan Sultanates (Bijapur, Ahmednagar, Golconda, Bidar) and the Vijayanagara Empire.
  • Vijayanagara was led by Rama Raya; the Sultanates were led by Hussain Nizam Shah of Ahmednagar, Ali Adil Shah of Bijapur and Ibrahim Qutb Shah of Golconda.
  • The Sultanates won decisively — Rama Raya was captured and executed.
  • Hampi was sacked and destroyed — the Vijayanagara Empire never recovered.
  • This battle is often considered the "death blow" to Vijayanagara.

End of the Bahmani Sultanate

  • The last Bahmani Sultan, Kalimullah Shah, fled to Bijapur.
  • The Bahmani Sultanate formally ended in 1527 CE — the dynasty died out shortly after.
  • The five Deccan Sultanates continued to rule the Deccan until they were conquered by the Mughals (under Aurangzeb) in the late 17th century.
BPSC Must-Know: The Bahmani Sultanate disintegrated into five Deccan Sultanates by 1527 CE: Bijapur (Adil Shahi), Ahmednagar (Nizam Shahi), Berar (Imad Shahi), Bidar (Barid Shahi), Golconda (Qutb Shahi). These five Sultanates, in alliance, defeated Vijayanagara at the Battle of Talikota (1565 CE).

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • The Bahmani Sultanate was a Deccan kingdom — it had no direct political connection with Bihar.
  • However, the Bahmani administrative model — the Taraf system, revenue reforms, and Indo-Islamic culture — influenced the later Sharqi Sultanate of Jaunpur, which controlled Bihar.
  • Mahmud Gawan's reforms — especially in revenue measurement and land assessment — influenced the administrative systems of later Deccan kingdoms, which in turn influenced Mughal administration, including in Bihar.
  • The Bahmani–Vijayanagara conflict indirectly affected Bihar — as Mughal attention shifted to the Deccan in the 17th century, allowing regional powers in eastern India to emerge.
  • For BPSC, the key point is that Bihar's medieval history is separate from the Bahmani Sultanate — but understanding the Deccan Sultanates helps explain the wider political landscape of medieval India.
