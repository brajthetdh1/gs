Environmental Pollution — Air, Water & Soil: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 14 min read · BPSC SSC

Environmental pollution is the contamination of the natural environment by harmful substances — causing adverse effects on living organisms and ecosystems. It is one of the most critical global challenges of our time. India faces severe pollution problems — air pollution in Delhi-NCR, water pollution in the Ganga, and soil degradation across agricultural regions. For BPSC and SSC, this topic is high-yield — especially the types of pollutants, Air Quality Index, major air and water pollutants, control measures, and Bihar's pollution challenges — including the Ganga's pollution in Patna, the impact of the 2016 prohibition on water quality, and Bihar's flood-related water pollution.

What is Pollution?

Pollution is the introduction of contaminants into the natural environment — causing adverse change. Any substance that causes pollution is called a pollutant.

Types of Pollutants

Exam Tip: Pollutants are classified as primary (directly emitted) and secondary (formed by reactions). Ground-level ozone and PAN (Peroxyacetyl Nitrate) are secondary pollutants — remember this common question in BPSC and SSC.

Particulate Matter — PM10 and PM2.5

  • PM10 — particulate matter with diameter 10 micrometres or less — can enter the respiratory tract.
  • PM2.5 — particulate matter with diameter 2.5 micrometres or less — can enter the bloodstream — most dangerous.
  • PM2.5 is a major concern in Indian cities — especially in Delhi-NCR.
  • Sources: vehicles, industries, construction dust, crop residue burning, brick kilns.

Air Quality Index (AQI)

The Air Quality Index (AQI) is a tool to communicate air quality to the public. It was launched in India on 17 September 2014 — by the Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB).

AQI RangeCategoryHealth Impact
0–50GoodMinimal impact
51–100SatisfactoryMinor breathing discomfort for sensitive people
101–200ModerateBreathing discomfort for people with lung disease
201–300PoorBreathing discomfort on prolonged exposure
301–400Very PoorRespiratory illness on prolonged exposure
401–500SevereAffects healthy people; serious impact on those with existing diseases

Note: AQI in India is measured based on 8 pollutants — PM10, PM2.5, NO₂, SO₂, CO, O₃, NH₃, and Pb.

Smog

  • Smog = Smoke + Fog — a mixture of smoke and fog.
  • Classical Smog (London Smog) — formed in cool, humid conditions — contains SO₂ and smoke.
  • Photochemical Smog (Los Angeles Smog) — formed in warm, sunny conditions — contains NOₓ, O₃ and PAN.
  • Photochemical smog is a serious issue in Indian cities — especially Delhi.

Acid Rain

  • Acid rain is rain with a pH below 5.6 — caused by SO₂ and NOₓ dissolving in rainwater to form sulphuric and nitric acids.
  • Effects: damages buildings (Taj Mahal), acidifies lakes and rivers, damages forests, kills fish.
  • India's Taj Mahal at Agra has been affected by acid rain — leading to the "Taj Trapezium Zone".

Causes of Air Pollution in India

  • Vehicular emissions — a major source in cities.
  • Industrial emissions — power plants, cement, steel, chemical industries.
  • Crop residue burning — especially in Punjab, Haryana (affecting Delhi).
  • Construction dust — PM10 and PM2.5.
  • Brick kilns — release SO₂, particulate matter.
  • Burning of fossil fuels — coal, petrol, diesel.
  • Waste burning — landfills, garbage burning.
  • Firecrackers — especially during Diwali.
  • Natural sources — dust storms, volcanic eruptions, forest fires.

Control Measures for Air Pollution

  • Cleaner fuels — CNG, LPG, electric vehicles.
  • BS-VI emission norms — implemented in India from April 2020.
  • Odd-Even Scheme — restricting vehicles on alternate days (Delhi).
  • Air Quality Management — Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP) in Delhi-NCR.
  • Pollution Control Boards — CPCB (Central) and SPCBs (State).
  • Renewable energy — solar, wind, hydro — reducing fossil fuel dependence.
  • Public transport — metro, buses, cycling.
  • Afforestation — planting trees to absorb pollutants.
  • Industrial scrubbers and filters — to reduce emissions.
  • National Clean Air Programme (NCAP) — launched in 2019 — targeting 20–30% reduction in PM levels by 2024.
BPSC Must-Know: PM2.5 is the most dangerous air pollutant — it can enter the bloodstream. The AQI was launched in 2014 by the CPCB. Photochemical smog (Los Angeles type) contains ozone and PAN. BS-VI emission norms were implemented in India from April 2020. NCAP (2019) aims to reduce PM levels by 20–30% by 2024.

Water Quality Indicators

IndicatorMeaning
BOD (Biochemical Oxygen Demand)Amount of oxygen required by bacteria to decompose organic matter — higher BOD = more pollution
COD (Chemical Oxygen Demand)Amount of oxygen required to oxidise all chemicals in water — higher COD = more pollution
Dissolved Oxygen (DO)Amount of oxygen dissolved in water — lower DO = more pollution
pHMeasure of acidity or alkalinity — normal range 6.5–8.5
Total Dissolved Solids (TDS)Total dissolved substances in water — high TDS = poor quality
Coliform BacteriaIndicator of sewage contamination — E. coli is used as indicator

Eutrophication

  • Eutrophication is the enrichment of water bodies with nutrients — especially nitrogen and phosphorus.
  • Causes excessive growth of algae (algal bloom) — which depletes oxygen when they decompose.
  • Result: "dead zones" — water bodies with very low oxygen — killing fish and other aquatic life.
  • Main cause: agricultural runoff (fertilisers) and sewage discharge.

Bioaccumulation and Biomagnification

  • Bioaccumulation — the gradual build-up of a substance (e.g. pesticide) in an organism's body over time.
  • Biomagnification — the increasing concentration of a substance at each successive trophic level in a food chain.
  • Classic example: DDT — bioaccumulates in fish and birds; causes eggshell thinning in birds.
  • Other examples: mercury (Minamata disease), cadmium (Itai-Itai disease).

Water Pollution Control Measures

  • Sewage Treatment Plants (STPs) — treat domestic wastewater before discharge.
  • Effluent Treatment Plants (ETPs) — for industrial wastewater.
  • Namami Gange Programme — flagship programme to clean the Ganga.
  • Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974.
  • National River Conservation Plan (NRCP) — for cleaning major rivers.
  • Polluter Pays Principle — the polluter must bear the cost of pollution.
  • Precautionary Principle — preventive action in the face of uncertainty.
  • Afforestation of catchment areas.
  • Community participation — awareness and cleanliness drives.
  • Swachh Bharat Mission — cleanliness and sanitation.
Exam Tip: Higher BOD = More polluted water. Lower DO = More polluted water. Eutrophication is caused by excess nutrients (nitrogen, phosphorus) — leading to algal blooms and dead zones. Biomagnification — the classic example is DDT. E. coli is the indicator of sewage contamination.

Causes of Soil Degradation

  • Deforestation — soil erosion.
  • Overgrazing — by livestock.
  • Over-irrigation — waterlogging and salinisation.
  • Excessive use of fertilisers and pesticides.
  • Industrial and mining activities — contamination.
  • Urbanisation — soil sealing.
  • Improper waste disposal — landfills, plastics.

Soil Conservation Measures

  • Contour ploughing — ploughing along the contours of land.
  • Terrace farming — on hilly slopes.
  • Strip cropping — alternating strips of crops.
  • Shelter belts — rows of trees to reduce wind erosion.
  • Crop rotation — alternating crops to maintain fertility.
  • Organic farming — reducing chemical inputs.
  • Afforestation — planting trees to bind soil.
  • Soil Health Card Scheme — for balanced nutrient use.
  • Integrated Nutrient Management (INM) — combining organic and inorganic inputs.
BPSC Must-Know: Soil conservation methods — Contour ploughing, Terrace farming, Strip cropping, Shelter belts, Crop rotation. Soil Health Card Scheme promotes balanced fertiliser use. Over-irrigation causes waterlogging and salinisation — a major problem in the Indo-Gangetic plains, including parts of Bihar and UP.

Noise Pollution

  • Noise pollution — unwanted or excessive sound that disrupts normal activities.
  • Measured in decibels (dB).
  • Sources: transport, industries, construction, loudspeakers, firecrackers.
  • Effects: hearing loss, hypertension, sleep disturbance, stress, reduced concentration.

Permissible Noise Limits in India

Residential Area55 dB45 dB

Silence Zones — areas around hospitals, schools, courts — where noise levels are strictly regulated.

Solid Waste Management

  • India generates over 1.5 lakh tonnes of municipal solid waste per day.
  • Waste is classified into:
    • Biodegradable — food waste, paper, garden waste.
    • Non-biodegradable — plastics, metals, glass, e-waste.
    • Hazardous — medical waste, batteries, chemicals.
  • Solid Waste Management Rules, 2016 — for proper waste handling.
  • Swachh Bharat Mission (2014) — cleanliness and sanitation.
  • Waste-to-Energy — converting waste into energy.
  • 3R Principle — Reduce, Reuse, Recycle.
  • Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) — producers responsible for waste from their products.

Environmental Principles

  • Polluter Pays Principle — the polluter must bear the cost of pollution.
  • Precautionary Principle — preventive action in the face of uncertainty.
  • Public Trust Doctrine — the State holds natural resources in trust for the public.
  • Sustainable Development — development that meets present needs without compromising future generations.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

Bihar's Pollution Challenges

  • Ganga Pollution in Bihar — the Ganga flows over 445 km through Bihar; it is heavily polluted by sewage, industrial effluents and religious offerings. The Namami Gange Programme has been implemented in Bihar to clean the river.
  • Patna's Ganga Pollution — the most polluted stretch in Bihar — with untreated sewage being a major source. Several STPs (Sewage Treatment Plants) have been set up.
  • Air Pollution — Bihar's cities face increasing air pollution due to vehicular emissions, construction dust, and brick kilns. Patna has been among the most polluted cities in India at various points.
  • Water Pollution from Agriculture — excessive use of fertilisers and pesticides in Bihar's agriculture leads to water pollution in rivers, canals and groundwater.
  • Arsenic Contamination — parts of Bihar (especially in the Ganga plains) face arsenic contamination in groundwater — a serious health concern. Districts like Bhojpur, Patna, Saran, Samastipur, Bhagalpur are affected.
  • Flouride Contamination — some districts in Bihar also face fluoride contamination in groundwater.
  • Flood-Related Pollution — recurring floods in north Bihar cause water contamination and spread water-borne diseases.
  • Solid Waste Management — Bihar faces challenges in managing municipal solid waste — especially in Patna and other urban centres.
  • Plastic Pollution — single-use plastics have been a significant problem; Bihar has implemented a plastic ban.

Bihar's Pollution Control Initiatives

  • Bihar State Pollution Control Board (BSPCB) — established in 1974; monitors pollution in the state.
  • Namami Gange Programme in Bihar — for cleaning the Ganga — several STPs constructed at Patna, Bhagalpur, Begusarai, and other towns.
  • National River Conservation Plan (NRCP) — for pollution control in the Ganga and other rivers.
  • Clean Ganga Mission — Bihar has been a key partner in this initiative.
  • Air Quality Monitoring Stations — installed in Patna, Gaya, Muzaffarpur, and other cities.
  • BS-VI Fuel — implemented across Bihar in 2020.
  • Bihar Plastic Ban — single-use plastics banned in the state.
  • Swachh Bharat Mission (Urban and Rural) — for sanitation and cleanliness.
  • Jal-Jeevan-Hariyali — flagship state programme for water conservation and tree plantation.
  • Mission Harit Bihar — for increasing tree cover in the state.
  • Bihar Water Policy — for water resource management.
  • Arsenic Removal Plants — installed in affected districts.
