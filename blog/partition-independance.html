Modern History

Partition and Independence (1947): Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 15 min read · BPSC SSC

The year 1947 marked the culmination of nearly a century of struggle — but it also brought one of the greatest human tragedies in history. On 15 August 1947, India became independent, ending 190 years of British rule. But the freedom came with the Partition of India — the creation of Pakistan as a separate nation for Muslims. The Radcliffe Line divided Punjab and Bengal, leading to the largest mass migration in human history and the death of over a million people. For BPSC and SSC, this is the final chapter of Modern Indian History — essential for understanding the making of modern India.

Background to Partition

The demand for a separate Muslim state grew gradually over the early 20th century.

Roots of the Two-Nation Theory

  • Sir Syed Ahmad Khan (1817–1898) — the founder of the Aligarh Movement — was the first to argue that Hindus and Muslims were two separate nations with different cultures, religions, and interests.
  • Partition of Bengal (1905) — Lord Curzon's attempt to create a Muslim-majority province was seen as a step towards communal division.
  • Morley–Minto Reforms (1909) — introduced separate electorates for Muslims — a major step towards communal politics.
  • Lucknow Pact (1916) — the Congress accepted separate electorates for Muslims — a compromise that later proved costly.
  • Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–22) — saw Hindu–Muslim unity but the Khilafat issue eventually divided the two communities.
  • Simon Commission (1928) — Jinnah's Fourteen Points (1929) demanded separate electorates and one-third representation for Muslims.
  • Allahabad Address (1930) — Muhammad Iqbal first proposed a separate Muslim state in northwestern India.
  • Pakistan Resolution (1940) — the Muslim League formally demanded a separate Muslim state — "Pakistan."

The Muslim League and the Demand for Pakistan

Formation of the Muslim League (1906)

  • Founded on 30 December 1906 at Dacca (Dhaka).
  • Founder: Nawab Salimullah Khan of Dacca.
  • First president: Sir Aga Khan.
  • Initially, the League was loyal to the British and did not demand a separate nation.

Muhammad Ali Jinnah (1876–1948)

  • Born in Karachi on 25 December 1876.
  • Was a successful lawyer and a member of the Indian National Congress in his early years.
  • Was called the "Ambassador of Hindu–Muslim Unity" by Gopal Krishna Gokhale.
  • Left the Congress after the Nagpur Session (1920) — disagreed with Gandhi's Non-Cooperation Movement.
  • Proposed the Fourteen Points (1929) — demanding safeguards for Muslims.
  • By the 1930s, he became the leader of the Muslim League and the chief architect of the demand for Pakistan.
  • Became the first Governor-General of Pakistan (1947–48).
  • Died on 11 September 1948 in Karachi.

Pakistan Resolution (Lahore Resolution, 1940)

  • Passed at the Muslim League session at Lahore on 23 March 1940.
  • Presided by Muhammad Ali Jinnah.
  • Demanded autonomous Muslim-majority states in northwestern and eastern zones of India.
  • The resolution was moved by Fazlul Huq (Premier of Bengal).
  • Called the "Pakistan Resolution" — though the word "Pakistan" was not used in the original text.
  • Jinnah's famous statement: "Hindus and Muslims belong to two different religious philosophies, social customs, literatures."
BPSC Must-Know: The Pakistan Resolution (Lahore, 23 March 1940) was the first formal demand for a separate Muslim state. It was passed under the presidency of Muhammad Ali Jinnah. The resolution was moved by Fazlul Huq. This marked the beginning of the formal demand for Pakistan.

Jinnah's Two-Nation Theory

  • Jinnah argued that Hindus and Muslims were two distinct nations — with different religions, cultures, and histories.
  • They could not live together in a single nation without one dominating the other.
  • Therefore, Muslims needed a separate homeland — Pakistan.
  • The Congress rejected the Two-Nation Theory — it stood for a secular, united India.
  • The British, after World War II, gradually accepted the Muslim League's demand — leading to Partition.

Events Leading to Partition (1945–1947)

Wavell Plan and Simla Conference (1945)

  • Lord Wavell became Viceroy in 1943.
  • The Simla Conference (June–July 1945) was convened to discuss India's political future.
  • The Congress and the Muslim League failed to agree on representation — the League demanded that it alone represent all Muslims.
  • The conference failed — the deadlock continued.

1946 Elections

  • Elections were held in early 1946 — to the Central and Provincial Legislative Assemblies.
  • The Congress won most of the general (non-Muslim) seats.
  • The Muslim League won most of the Muslim-reserved seats — demonstrating its hold over the Muslim electorate.
  • The League's success strengthened its claim to be the sole representative of Indian Muslims.

Cabinet Mission (1946)

  • The Cabinet Mission was sent to India in March 1946 — by the British government.
  • Members: Lord Pethick-Lawrence (Secretary of State for India), Sir Stafford Cripps, A.V. Alexander.
  • The Mission proposed a federal India with provinces grouped into three sections:
    • Section A — Hindu-majority provinces (Madras, Bombay, UP, Bihar, etc.).
    • Section B — Muslim-majority provinces in the northwest (Punjab, Sindh, NWFP).
    • Section C — Muslim-majority provinces in the east (Bengal, Assam).
  • The Congress and the Muslim League both accepted the plan — but with different interpretations.
  • The League wanted the right to secede after 10 years — the Congress opposed this.
  • Jinnah called for "Direct Action Day" (16 August 1946) — leading to massive communal riots in Calcutta and elsewhere.
  • The Cabinet Mission plan ultimately failed — the Congress and the League could not agree on the future of India.
Exam Tip: The Cabinet Mission (March 1946) proposed a federal India with three sections — A (Hindu-majority), B and C (Muslim-majority). The plan failed after Jinnah called for Direct Action Day (16 August 1946). This was the last major attempt to keep India united.

Direct Action Day (16 August 1946)

  • Called by Jinnah and the Muslim League after the failure of the Cabinet Mission plan.
  • Declared as a day of "direct action" to achieve Pakistan.
  • Massive communal riots broke out in Calcutta — over 4,000 people were killed in one day.
  • Rioting spread to Noakhali (Bengal), Bihar, and other regions.
  • The Bihar riots (October–November 1946) — particularly in Patna, Gaya, Bhagalpur, and other districts — were among the worst. Thousands died; Mahatma Gandhi visited Bihar to restore peace.

Interim Government (1946)

  • An interim government was formed on 2 September 1946 — with Jawaharlal Nehru as Vice-President of the Executive Council (de facto Prime Minister).
  • The Muslim League joined the interim government in October 1946 — but with the aim of obstructing it.
  • The League demanded a separate Muslim state — the deadlock continued.

Constituent Assembly (1946)

  • The Constituent Assembly was formed in December 1946 — to draft a constitution for India.
  • First meeting: 9 December 1946 — chaired by Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha (temporary chairman, from Bihar).
  • On 11 December 1946, Dr. Rajendra Prasad was elected permanent President of the Constituent Assembly.
  • The Muslim League boycotted the Constituent Assembly — demanding a separate Pakistan.
  • After Partition, the Constituent Assembly of India continued to draft the Constitution — adopted on 26 November 1949 and came into force on 26 January 1950.

Mountbatten Plan (3 June 1947)

The Mountbatten Plan was the final blueprint for the partition of India. It was announced by Lord Mountbatten — the last Viceroy of India — on 3 June 1947.

Key Provisions

  • Partition of India into two independent nations — India and Pakistan.
  • The Muslim-majority provinces of Punjab, Bengal, Sindh, Baluchistan, and the North-West Frontier Province (NWFP) would go to Pakistan.
  • The princely states were free to join either India or Pakistan — or remain independent.
  • The Radcliffe Line would demarcate the boundary between India and Pakistan.
  • Both nations would be Dominions within the British Commonwealth (until they became republics).
  • The Constituent Assemblies of both countries would have the right to secede from the Commonwealth.

Referendum in NWFP

  • The North-West Frontier Province (NWFP) held a referendum in July 1947.
  • The result was in favour of joining Pakistan.
  • Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan — the "Frontier Gandhi" — opposed the decision and supported a united India.

Sylhet Referendum (1947)

  • A referendum was held in Sylhet (Assam) — a Muslim-majority district.
  • The result was in favour of joining East Pakistan.
  • Sylhet was transferred from Assam to East Bengal.
BPSC Must-Know: The Mountbatten Plan (3 June 1947) was the final blueprint for Partition. It provided for the partition of India, the Radcliffe Line, and the right of princely states to join either nation. It was passed by the British Parliament as the Indian Independence Act, 1947.

Indian Independence Act, 1947

  • Passed by the British Parliament on 18 July 1947.
  • Gave effect to the Mountbatten Plan.
  • Key provisions:
    • Two independent Dominions — India and Pakistan — to be created on 15 August 1947.
    • Each Dominion would have a Governor-General — appointed by the British Crown.
    • The British Government would cease to have any control over Indian affairs after 15 August 1947.
    • The Constituent Assemblies of both nations would act as their legislatures until new constitutions were framed.
    • The princely states were free to join either Dominion or remain independent.
    • The office of the Secretary of State for India was abolished.
    • The Paramountcy of the British Crown over the princely states was terminated.

Radcliffe Line (17 August 1947)

  • Named after Sir Cyril Radcliffe — a British lawyer who was appointed as the Chairman of the Boundary Commission.
  • The Radcliffe Line was drawn to divide the provinces of Punjab and Bengal between India and Pakistan.
  • Radcliffe had never visited India before — he was given just five weeks to draw the boundary of a territory with millions of people.
  • The Radcliffe Line was published on 17 August 1947 — after India had already become independent.
  • The line divided:
    • Punjab — into Indian Punjab (East Punjab) and Pakistani Punjab (West Punjab).
    • Bengal — into Indian Bengal (West Bengal) and Pakistani Bengal (East Bengal, later East Pakistan and now Bangladesh).
  • The Radcliffe Line was drawn hastily — leading to confusion and disputes over several territories.
Exam Tip: The Radcliffe Line (17 August 1947) was drawn by Sir Cyril Radcliffe — a British lawyer who had never been to India. He was given only five weeks to divide Punjab and Bengal. The line was published after India's independence — leading to chaos and mass migration.

Partition — The Human Tragedy

The Partition of India was one of the largest and most tragic migrations in human history.

Scale of the Tragedy

  • Over 15 million people were displaced — the largest mass migration in human history.
  • Over 1 million people (some estimates say 2 million) were killed in communal violence.
  • 75,000+ women were abducted, raped, or killed.
  • Millions became refugees overnight — Hindus and Sikhs moved from Pakistan to India; Muslims moved from India to Pakistan.
  • Trains filled with dead bodies crossed the border — an image that haunted the subcontinent for generations.
  • Families were divided; property was lost; entire communities were uprooted.

Impact on Punjab and Bengal

  • Punjab — the worst affected province; Lahore, Amritsar, and other cities saw horrific violence.
  • Bengal — Calcutta, Dacca, and other cities saw massive rioting and migration.
  • Bihar — the Bihar riots (October–November 1946) killed thousands; Gandhi visited Bihar to restore peace.
  • Delhi — millions of refugees arrived; refugee camps were set up across North India.

Gandhi's Role in Restoring Peace

  • Gandhi did not participate in the independence celebrations on 15 August 1947 — he was in Calcutta, trying to stop the communal violence.
  • He walked barefoot through the riot-torn districts of Noakhali (Bengal) and Bihar — appealing for Hindu–Muslim unity.
  • His efforts were the last great act of his life — he was assassinated on 30 January 1948 by Nathuram Godse.

Independence Day — 15 August 1947

  • India became independent at the stroke of midnight on 14–15 August 1947.
  • Jawaharlal Nehru delivered his famous "Tryst with Destiny" speech in the Constituent Assembly.
  • The tricolour flag was hoisted at the Red Fort in Delhi.
  • Lord Mountbatten became the first Governor-General of independent India — he served until 1948.
  • Jawaharlal Nehru became the first Prime Minister of India.
  • Dr. Rajendra Prasad became the first President of the Constituent Assembly (and later the first President of India in 1950).
  • Pakistan became independent on 14 August 1947 — one day before India.
  • Muhammad Ali Jinnah became the first Governor-General of Pakistan.
BPSC Must-Know: India became independent on 15 August 1947. Nehru's famous "Tryst with Destiny" speech was delivered at midnight. Lord Mountbatten became the first Governor-General; Jawaharlal Nehru became the first Prime Minister. Pakistan became independent on 14 August 1947.

Integration of Princely States

After Partition, India had to integrate over 565 princely states into the Indian Union. This was one of the greatest challenges of the new nation.

Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel's Role

  • Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel — as the first Deputy Prime Minister and Home Minister — led the integration of princely states.
  • Called the "Iron Man of India" for his role in unifying the nation.
  • His secretary V.P. Menon played a key role in negotiating with the princes.
  • The Instrument of Accession was signed by most princely states — agreeing to join India on matters of defence, foreign affairs, and communications.
  • By 15 August 1947, most states had acceded to India.

Hyderabad — Operation Polo (1948)

  • The Nizam of Hyderabad — Mir Osman Ali Khan — was one of the richest men in the world and wanted Hyderabad to remain independent.
  • He had a large army and was supported by Razakars (a private militia).
  • India launched Operation Polo in September 1948 — Hyderabad was integrated into India within 5 days.
  • The Nizam signed the Instrument of Accession.
  • Hyderabad became a state of India — later divided into Andhra Pradesh, Karnataka, and Maharashtra.

Jammu & Kashmir

  • Maharaja Hari Singh was initially reluctant to join either India or Pakistan.
  • In October 1947, Pakistan-backed tribal raiders invaded Kashmir.
  • The Maharaja signed the Instrument of Accession to India on 26 October 1947.
  • Indian troops were airlifted to Srinagar to defend Kashmir.
  • The First Kashmir War (1947–48) ended with a ceasefire — Kashmir was divided into Indian-administered and Pakistan-administered areas.
  • The UN was involved — but the dispute remains unresolved to this day.
BPSC Must-Know: Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel led the integration of 565+ princely states. Key cases: Junagadh (plebiscite, joined India), Hyderabad (Operation Polo, 1948), Jammu & Kashmir (Instrument of Accession, 26 October 1947).

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • Bihar's role in the Constituent Assembly — Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha (from Bihar) was the temporary chairman of the Constituent Assembly's first meeting (9 December 1946).
  • Dr. Rajendra Prasad — from Zeradei (Siwan, Bihar) — was elected permanent President of the Constituent Assembly (11 December 1946). He later became the first President of India (1950–1962).
  • Sri Krishna Sinha — "Bihar Kesari" — from Bihar; became the first Chief Minister of Bihar (1946).
  • Anugrah Narayan Sinha — "Bihar Vibhuti" — from Bihar; became the first Deputy Chief Minister and Finance Minister of Bihar.
  • Bihar Riots (October–November 1946) — after Direct Action Day, Bihar saw some of the worst communal riots of the pre-Partition period. Thousands were killed. Mahatma Gandhi visited Bihar (November 1946 – March 1947) to restore peace.
  • Jayaprakash Narayan — from Sitabdiara (Saran, Bihar) — was a key socialist leader; worked to restore peace during the Bihar riots.
  • Bihar and the Partition — Bihar was not partitioned (unlike Punjab and Bengal), but the state received a large number of refugees from East Pakistan (later Bangladesh).
  • Bihar's role in the freedom struggle — from the Champaran Satyagraha (1917) to the Quit India Movement (1942), Bihar was one of the strongest centres of the nationalist movement.
  • Bihar's contribution to the Indian Constitution — several Bihari leaders played key roles in the Constituent Assembly:
    • Dr. Rajendra Prasad — President of the Constituent Assembly.
    • Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha — temporary chairman.
    • Brajkishore Prasad — member.
    • Anugrah Narayan Sinha — member.
    • Sri Krishna Sinha — member.
BPSC Must-Know: Bihar was not partitioned — but the state suffered from severe communal riots in 1946. Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha (Bihar) chaired the first meeting of the Constituent Assembly; Dr. Rajendra Prasad (Bihar) became its permanent President and later the first President of India. Bihar's contribution to the making of the Constitution is immense.
