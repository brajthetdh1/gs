Basic Concepts of Economics — GDP, GNP & National Income: Complete Guide

15 Oct 2026 · 13 min read · BPSC SSC

Before you dive into Banking, Budget, GST or Planning, you must understand the basic concepts of economics — GDP, GNP, NNP, per capita income, inflation and the Human Development Index. These concepts form the foundation of the entire Economy section for BPSC and SSC. Almost every Economy question — direct or indirect — relies on these terms. This post gives you a clean, exam-ready foundation.

What is Economics?

Economics is the study of how individuals, businesses and governments allocate scarce resources to satisfy unlimited wants.

Branches of Economics

BranchFocusKey Thinker
MicroeconomicsIndividual units — consumers, firms, prices in a single marketAlfred Marshall
MacroeconomicsAggregate economy — national income, inflation, unemploymentJ.M. Keynes

Types of Economies

  • Capitalist Economy — market-driven, private ownership (USA).
  • Socialist Economy — state-controlled, public ownership (former USSR, Cuba).
  • Mixed Economy — both public and private sectors (India).

India is a mixed economy — the public sector coexists with the private sector. This model was adopted after Independence (Industrial Policy Resolution, 1948 & 1956).

Exam Tip: India is a mixed economy — a hallmark of the Indian economic model since 1948. Under the 1991 LPG reforms (Liberalisation, Privatisation, Globalisation), the role of the private sector expanded significantly.

National Income — Core Concepts

National income is the total value of all goods and services produced in a country during a financial year. It is measured by the Central Statistics Office (CSO) — now the National Statistical Office (NSO) under the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI).

The Key Aggregates

ConceptFull FormDefinition
GDPGross Domestic ProductTotal market value of all final goods and services produced within the domestic territory of a country in a year
GNPGross National ProductGDP + Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA)
NDPNet Domestic ProductGDP – Depreciation
NNPNet National ProductGNP – Depreciation
NNP at Factor CostNational IncomeNNP at Market Price – Indirect Taxes + Subsidies
Per Capita Income—National Income ÷ Total Population

Formulas to Remember

  • GDP = C + I + G + (X – M)
    where C = Consumption, I = Investment, G = Government Spending, X = Exports, M = Imports.
  • GNP = GDP + NFIA
  • NNP = GNP – Depreciation
  • NNP at Factor Cost (National Income) = NNP at Market Price – Net Indirect Taxes
  • Per Capita Income = National Income / Population
Remember: GDP measures production within India's borders — regardless of who owns the producing unit. GNP measures production by Indian residents — regardless of where they are located. The difference is NFIA (Net Factor Income from Abroad).

GDP vs GNP — A Practical Example

  • If a Japanese car company produces cars in India, that production counts in India's GDP — but not in India's GNP.
  • If an Indian working in the USA sends money to India, that income counts in India's GNP — but not in India's GDP.
  • India's GNP is generally lower than its GDP because NFIA is negative for India (more foreign investment in India than Indian investment abroad).

Methods of Measuring National Income

MethodHow It Works
Production / Value-Added MethodSum of value added by all producing units in the economy
Income MethodSum of all factor incomes — rent, wages, interest, profit
Expenditure MethodSum of all final expenditures — C + I + G + (X – M)

All three methods should, in theory, produce the same result — because production, income and expenditure are three sides of the same coin.

GDP at Market Price vs GDP at Factor Cost

Note: Since 2015, India has shifted to reporting GDP at Market Price as the headline number — with a new base year of 2011–12.

Exam Tip: India's current GDP base year is 2011–12. The base year is revised periodically by the National Statistical Office (NSO). Earlier base years were 1950–51, 1960–61, 1970–71, 1980–81, 1993–94, 1999–2000, 2004–05, and 2011–12.

GDP Deflator

The GDP Deflator is a measure of the overall price level — calculated as:

GDP Deflator = (Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP) × 100

It is the broadest measure of inflation — covering all goods and services produced in an economy.

Key Terms You Must Know

Depreciation

  • The wear and tear of capital goods used in production.
  • Also called Consumption of Fixed Capital (CFC).
  • Gross values include depreciation; Net values exclude it.

Net Factor Income from Abroad (NFIA)

  • Income earned by Indians abroad minus income earned by foreigners in India.
  • For India, NFIA is negative — because foreign investors earn more in India than Indians earn abroad.

Transfer Payments

  • Payments made without any productive service in return — e.g. pensions, scholarships, unemployment allowances.
  • Not included in national income — because they do not add to production.

Subsidies

  • Government payments to producers to reduce the cost of production.
  • Added to national income (because they are part of factor incomes).

Indirect Taxes

  • Taxes on goods and services — GST, customs duty, excise duty.
  • Subtracted from national income to arrive at factor cost.

Inflation — Basic Concepts

Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level — reducing the purchasing power of money.

Types of Inflation by Rate

TypeAnnual Rate
Creeping InflationUp to 3%
Walking Inflation3% – 7%
Running Inflation10% – 20%
Galloping Inflation / HyperinflationAbove 20%

Types of Inflation by Cause

  • Demand-Pull Inflation — demand exceeds supply.
  • Cost-Push Inflation — rise in input costs (wages, raw materials, fuel).
  • Structural Inflation — due to structural bottlenecks in the economy.
  • Imported Inflation — rise in prices of imported goods.

Related Concepts

  • Deflation — sustained fall in the general price level (opposite of inflation).
  • Disinflation — a fall in the rate of inflation (prices still rise, but more slowly).
  • Stagflation — high inflation + high unemployment + stagnant growth.
  • Reflation — deliberate policy to raise prices to stimulate the economy.

Price Indices in India

IndexReleased ByBase YearUse
CPI (Consumer Price Index)NSO (MoSPI)2012Retail inflation; used by RBI for monetary policy
WPI (Wholesale Price Index)Office of Economic Adviser (DPIIT)2011–12Wholesale inflation
IIP (Index of Industrial Production)NSO2011–12Industrial growth

Note: Since 2014, the RBI uses CPI (Combined) as its key inflation indicator — replacing WPI. The RBI's inflation target is 4% ± 2%.

BPSC Must-Know: The RBI uses CPI (not WPI) for monetary policy decisions since 2014. The RBI's flexible inflation targeting framework sets a target of 4% with a tolerance band of ±2% — i.e. between 2% and 6%. This is enshrined in the RBI Act through the 2016 amendment.

Human Development Index (HDI)

The Human Development Index is published annually by the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP). It was developed by the Pakistani economist Mahbub ul Haq in 1990 — with the Indian Nobel laureate Amartya Sen playing a key role.

Three Components of HDI

ComponentIndicator
HealthLife expectancy at birth
EducationMean years of schooling; expected years of schooling
Standard of LivingGNI per capita (PPP)

HDI Categories

  • Very High Human Development — HDI ≥ 0.800
  • High Human Development — HDI 0.700–0.799
  • Medium Human Development — HDI 0.550–0.699
  • Low Human Development — HDI < 0.550

India's rank: India is placed in the Medium Human Development category. India's HDI rank has improved steadily over the years — from 130 in 2016 to around 134 in recent reports.

Other Important Economic Concepts

Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)

  • An economic theory that compares different countries' currencies through a "basket of goods" approach.
  • Used by the IMF and World Bank for international comparisons.
  • India is the third-largest economy in the world by PPP — after the USA and China.
  • By nominal GDP, India is the fifth-largest economy in the world.

Gross Value Added (GVA)

  • GVA = GDP – Indirect Taxes + Subsidies
  • GVA measures the value of output produced by an economy.
  • Since 2015, the RBI uses GVA as the key indicator for its projections.
  • GVA at basic prices is the headline measure used by the NSO.

Business Cycle

The economy moves in cycles:

  1. Expansion — economic growth, rising output and employment.
  2. Peak — highest point of the cycle.
  3. Recession — falling output, rising unemployment.
  4. Trough — lowest point of the cycle.
  5. Recovery — the economy begins to grow again.

Recession vs Depression

  • Recession — a fall in GDP for two consecutive quarters.
  • Depression — a prolonged and severe recession.
  • India's only recorded technical recession was during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020).

Economic Sectors of India

SectorDescriptionContribution to GDP
PrimaryAgriculture, forestry, fishing, mining~18%
SecondaryManufacturing, construction, electricity~25%
TertiaryServices — banking, IT, trade, transport~55%

Key insight: The service sector contributes the most to India's GDP. But the primary sector employs the largest share of the workforce (~45%). This is a structural anomaly — a sign of underemployment in agriculture.

Organised vs Unorganised Sector

  • Organised sector — registered, follows labour laws, provides social security.
  • Unorganised sector — unregistered, no job security, no social security.
  • Over 80% of India's workforce is in the unorganised sector.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

Bihar's Economy at a Glance

  • Bihar's GSDP is one of the fastest-growing among Indian states in recent years — with a growth rate above the national average.
  • Bihar's economy is agriculture-heavy — around 70% of the population depends on agriculture for livelihood.
  • Bihar's per capita income is one of the lowest in India — a major developmental challenge.
  • Bihar's service sector has grown rapidly — but the state still lags behind the national average in industrialisation.
  • Remittances from Bihari migrants working in other states and abroad are a significant source of income for the state.

Bihar's HDI Position

  • Bihar has historically ranked low on the HDI — among the bottom states in India.
  • However, Bihar has shown significant improvement in recent years — in health, education and per capita income.
  • Life expectancy in Bihar has increased; infant mortality has declined.
  • Bihar's literacy rate has improved — from 47% in 2001 to 61.8% in 2011, and further in recent years.

Key Bihar Government Initiatives

  • Bihar Industrial Investment Promotion Policy — to attract industrial investment.
  • Jeevika (Bihar Rural Livelihoods Project) — women's self-help groups and rural livelihoods.
  • Mukhyamantri Udyami Yojana — support for young entrepreneurs.
  • Bihar Startup Policy — promoting startups and innovation in the state.
  • Saat Nishchay (Seven Resolves) — the state government's flagship development programme covering infrastructure, education, health, and employment.
  • Bihar Skill Development Mission — skilling youth for employment.
BPSC Must-Know: Bihar's economy is agriculture-dominated — with ~70% of the population dependent on agriculture. Despite rapid GSDP growth, Bihar's per capita income and HDI remain low. Remittances from migrants are a critical source of household income. Key state initiatives include Jeevika, Saat Nishchay, and the Bihar Industrial Investment Promotion Policy. These facts are frequently asked in BPSC.
