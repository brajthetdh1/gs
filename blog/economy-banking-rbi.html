Banking System & RBI: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 14 min read · BPSC SSC

The Indian banking system is one of the largest and most dynamic in the world. It is regulated by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) — the central bank — which controls monetary policy, issues currency, and supervises all banks. For BPSC and SSC, banking is a high-yield topic: questions on RBI functions, monetary policy tools, nationalisation of banks, NPA, and types of banks are asked regularly. This post gives you a clean, exam-ready overview.

Structure of the Indian Banking System

The Indian banking system can be broadly classified into organised and unorganised sectors. The organised sector is further divided into Reserve Bank of India, commercial banks and cooperative banks.

Organised Banking Sector — Structure

CategoryInstitutions

Cooperative BanksUrban cooperative banks, rural cooperative banks (short-term & long-term)
Development BanksNABARD, SIDBI, EXIM Bank, NHB
Specialised BanksPayments Banks, Small Finance Banks, Local Area Banks

Unorganised Banking Sector

  • Indigenous bankers (Mahajan, Sahukar, Seth)
  • Money lenders
  • Chit funds, Nidhis (mutual benefit funds)
  • Pawn brokers
Exam Tip: The RBI is the apex of the Indian banking system — all banks operate under its supervision. The banking sector is regulated by the RBI, while the cooperative banking sector has dual regulation — RBI and NABARD for rural cooperatives.

RESERVE BANK OF INDIA (RBI)

Establishment

  • Established on 1 April 1935 under the RBI Act, 1934.
  • Based on the recommendation of the Hilton Young Commission (1926).
  • Nationalised on 1 January 1949.
  • Initially headquartered in Kolkata — shifted to Mumbai in 1937.
  • The RBI is a statutory body — established by an Act of Parliament.

Composition

  • Central Board of Directors — the apex body, chaired by the Governor.
  • Governor — appointed by the Government of India for up to 3 years (can be reappointed).
  • Deputy Governors — normally four in number.
  • Independent Directors — nominated by the Government of India.
  • Government Officials — representatives from the Ministry of Finance.

Functions of RBI

The RBI performs several critical functions — broadly classified into traditional and developmental functions.

Traditional Functions

1. Issuer of Currency

  • RBI issues all currency notes except ₹1 notes and coins — these are issued by the Government of India.
  • The ₹1 note bears the signature of the Finance Secretary; all other notes bear the signature of the RBI Governor.
  • RBI maintains a Minimum Reserve System — ₹200 crore, of which ₹115 crore in gold and ₹85 crore in foreign securities.
  • The Department of Currency Management handles currency issuance.

2. Banker to the Government

  • Acts as banker, agent and adviser to the Central Government and State Governments.
  • Collects taxes, makes payments, manages public debt, and provides short-term credit (Ways and Means Advances).
  • Manages the Consolidated Fund and Contingency Fund of the Government.

3. Banker's Bank

  • Acts as banker to all commercial banks — accepts deposits, provides loans.
  • Acts as the lender of last resort — provides emergency credit to banks.
  • Supervises and regulates all banks through the Department of Banking Supervision.
  • Conducts periodic inspections of banks.

4. Controller of Credit / Monetary Authority

  • Formulates and implements the monetary policy of India.
  • Uses quantitative and qualitative tools to control credit (detailed below).
  • The Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) — set up in 2016 — decides policy rates.
  • Inflation target: 4% ± 2%.

5. Custodian of Foreign Exchange Reserves

  • Maintains the Foreign Exchange Reserves of India.
  • Manages the Foreign Exchange Management Act (FEMA), 1999.
  • Intervenes in the foreign exchange market to stabilise the rupee.

6. Clearing House Functions

  • Operates the clearing house system for banks.
  • Manages the Real Time Gross Settlement (RTGS) and National Electronic Funds Transfer (NEFT) systems.

Developmental / Promotional Functions

  • Promotion of banking habit — through branch expansion and financial inclusion.
  • Provision of agricultural credit — through NABARD.
  • Provision of industrial credit — through IDBI, SIDBI, IFCI.
  • Development of cooperative credit — through NABARD and cooperative banks.
  • Regulation of the money market — through treasury bills, commercial papers.
  • Collection and publication of economic data — through the RBI's various departments.

Monetary Policy of RBI

Monetary policy refers to the policy of the RBI in controlling the money supply and credit in the economy to achieve specific objectives like price stability, full employment, and economic growth.

Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)

  • Set up in 2016 — through the RBI (Amendment) Act, 2016.
  • Composition — 6 members: 3 from RBI (including the Governor) and 3 external members appointed by the Government.
  • Chairperson — the RBI Governor.
  • Meetings — at least 4 times a year.
  • Decision — by majority vote; the Governor has a casting vote in case of a tie.
  • Inflation Target — 4% ± 2% (set by the Government of India in consultation with RBI).

Quantitative Tools of Monetary Policy

1. Bank Rate

  • The rate at which the RBI lends long-term funds to commercial banks.
  • Also called the discount rate.
  • Currently the MSF (Marginal Standing Facility) is above the repo rate.

2. Repo Rate

  • The rate at which the RBI lends short-term funds to commercial banks against government securities.
  • Currently around 6.5% (as of 2024–25).
  • An increase in repo rate reduces liquidity; a decrease increases liquidity.

3. Reverse Repo Rate

  • The rate at which the RBI borrows from commercial banks.
  • Usually below the repo rate.
  • An increase in reverse repo rate absorbs liquidity from the market.

4. Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR)

  • The percentage of net demand and time liabilities that banks must keep as cash reserves with the RBI.
  • Currently 4% (reduced from 4.5% in 2020).
  • CRR does not earn interest for banks.
  • An increase in CRR reduces liquidity; a decrease increases liquidity.

5. Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR)

  • The percentage of net demand and time liabilities that banks must maintain in liquid assets — cash, gold, or government securities.
  • Currently 18%.
  • SLR earns returns for banks — unlike CRR.
  • An increase in SLR reduces liquidity; a decrease increases liquidity.

6. Open Market Operations (OMO)

  • The buying and selling of government securities by RBI in the open market.
  • Buying securities → injects liquidity into the system.
  • Selling securities → absorbs liquidity from the system.

7. Marginal Standing Facility (MSF)

  • A facility under which banks can borrow overnight funds from RBI against approved government securities.
  • Rate is usually above the repo rate.
  • MSF rate = Repo Rate + 0.25% (typically).

Qualitative Tools of Monetary Policy

  • Margin Requirements — RBI specifies the margin that banks must maintain while lending against certain securities.
  • Consumer Credit Regulation — RBI regulates the credit extended for consumer goods.
  • Moral Suasion — RBI persuades banks to follow its directives through informal discussions.
  • Direct Action — RBI can penalise banks for non-compliance.
  • Selective Credit Control — RBI directs credit to specific sectors (e.g. priority sector lending).
  • Publicity — RBI publishes information to influence bank lending.
BPSC Must-Know: Repo Rate — RBI lends short-term to banks. Reverse Repo Rate — RBI borrows from banks. CRR — cash reserves with RBI (no interest). SLR — liquid assets with banks (earn interest). MSF — overnight borrowing by banks. All these are quantitative tools of monetary policy.

Types of Banks in India

Commercial Banks

  • Public Sector Banks (PSBs) — majority stake held by the Government of India (e.g. SBI, PNB, Canara Bank).
  • Private Sector Banks — majority stake held by private individuals (e.g. HDFC Bank, ICICI Bank, Axis Bank).
  • Foreign Banks — incorporated outside India, but operating in India (e.g. Citibank, HSBC, Standard Chartered).
  • Regional Rural Banks (RRBs) — set up under the RRB Act, 1976 — to serve rural areas.

Cooperative Banks

  • Urban Cooperative Banks (UCBs) — serve urban areas.
  • Rural Cooperative Banks — short-term (State Cooperative Banks, District Central Cooperative Banks, Primary Agricultural Credit Societies) and long-term (State Cooperative Agriculture and Rural Development Banks, Primary Cooperative Agriculture and Rural Development Banks).

Specialised Banks

  • Payments Banks — can accept deposits up to ₹2 lakh per customer; cannot lend; e.g. Paytm Payments Bank, India Post Payments Bank, Airtel Payments Bank.
  • Small Finance Banks — provide financial inclusion to small businesses, farmers, and unorganised sector; e.g. AU Small Finance Bank, Equitas Small Finance Bank.
  • Local Area Banks — set up in the 1990s to serve specific rural and semi-urban areas.

Regional Rural Banks (RRBs)

  • Established under the Regional Rural Banks Act, 1976.
  • Shareholding — Central Government (50%), State Government (15%), Sponsor Bank (35%).
  • Objective: provide credit to small and marginal farmers, agricultural labourers, artisans and small entrepreneurs.
  • First RRB — Prathama Bank (Moradabad, UP) — set up on 2 October 1975.
  • Currently, there are around 43 RRBs in India (after amalgamation).
Exam Tip: Regional Rural Banks (RRBs) were set up in 1976 on the recommendation of the Narasimham Working Group (1975). The first RRB was Prathama Bank (1975). The shareholding pattern is — Centre 50%, State 15%, Sponsor Bank 35%.

Nationalisation of Banks

First Round (1969)

  • On 19 July 1969, 14 major commercial banks with deposits above ₹50 crore were nationalised.
  • The decision was taken by Prime Minister Indira Gandhi.
  • Objective: to ensure credit flow to agriculture, small industries and the unbanked rural population.

Second Round (1980)

  • On 15 April 1980, 6 more banks with deposits above ₹200 crore were nationalised.
  • Total: 20 banks nationalised.

Merger of Banks (2017–2020)

  • Several PSBs were merged — reducing the number of PSBs from 27 in 2017 to 12 in 2020.
  • Major mergers: Bank of Baroda + Vijaya Bank + Dena Bank (2019); Punjab National Bank + Oriental Bank of Commerce + United Bank of India (2020); Canara Bank + Syndicate Bank (2020); Union Bank of India + Andhra Bank + Corporation Bank (2020).
  • Currently, there are 12 Public Sector Banks in India.

Non-Performing Assets (NPA)

  • A Non-Performing Asset (NPA) is a loan or advance where the borrower has not paid interest or principal for 90 days.
  • Types of NPA:
    • Sub-standard — NPA for up to 12 months.
    • Doubtful — NPA for more than 12 months.
    • Loss Asset — identified as a loss by the bank or auditors.
  • Steps taken to tackle NPAs:
    • SARFAESI Act, 2002 — allows banks to seize assets of defaulters.
    • Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC), 2016 — resolution mechanism for stressed assets.
    • Bad Bank (NARCL) — National Asset Reconstruction Company Limited — set up in 2021 to take over stressed assets.
    • Recapitalisation of PSBs — Government of India has infused capital.
    • Mergers of PSBs — to strengthen weak banks.
BPSC Must-Know: NPA — a loan is classified as NPA if the borrower has not paid interest or principal for 90 days. The IBC (2016) and SARFAESI Act (2002) are key tools to tackle NPAs. The Bad Bank (NARCL) was set up in 2021 to deal with stressed assets.

Financial Inclusion in India

Financial inclusion means providing financial services (banking, credit, insurance) to all sections of society — especially the poor and unbanked.

Priority Sector Lending (PSL)

  • Banks are required to lend a minimum percentage of their credit to priority sectors.
  • Categories: Agriculture, MSMEs, Education, Housing, Export Credit, Social Infrastructure, Renewable Energy, and others.
  • Target for domestic commercial banks: 40% of Adjusted Net Bank Credit (ANBC).
  • Sub-target for agriculture: 18%.
  • Sub-target for small and marginal farmers: 10%.
  • Sub-target for weaker sections: 12%.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

Banking in Bihar

  • Bihar has a lower banking density compared to the national average — a major developmental challenge.
  • The Credit-Deposit (CD) Ratio in Bihar is one of the lowest in India — indicating that deposits from Bihar are not fully utilised for credit in the state.
  • The Bihar State Cooperative Bank is the apex cooperative bank in the state.
  • Regional Rural Banks (RRBs) operating in Bihar include:
    • Madhya Bihar Gramin Bank (sponsored by Punjab National Bank)
    • Dakshin Bihar Gramin Bank (sponsored by Punjab National Bank)
    • Uttar Bihar Gramin Bank (sponsored by Central Bank of India)
  • Several public sector banks have a strong presence in Bihar — especially SBI, PNB, Canara Bank, and Bank of Baroda.

Financial Inclusion in Bihar

  • PMJDY (Pradhan Mantri Jan Dhan Yojana) has been implemented extensively in Bihar — with lakhs of new bank accounts opened.
  • Jeevika — the Bihar Rural Livelihoods Project — promotes financial inclusion through women's self-help groups (SHGs).
  • Bihar Gram Swaraj Yojana — for rural development and financial inclusion.
  • MUDRA Yojana — provides loans to small businesses across Bihar.
  • Digital payments — UPI and mobile banking are increasingly used in urban Bihar; rural adoption is growing.
  • Bihar State Cooperative Bank — provides credit to farmers and cooperatives.
  • NABARD — provides rural credit and development support to Bihar.

Bihar's Key Banking Institutions

  • Bihar State Cooperative Bank — apex cooperative bank.
  • Bihar State Financial Corporation — provides term loans to industries.
  • Bihar Industrial Development Corporation — for industrial development.
  • Regional Rural Banks — 3 main RRBs serving different regions of Bihar.
  • Lead Bank Scheme — every district has a lead bank — which coordinates banking development.

Bihar's Banking Challenges

  • Low CD ratio — Banks in Bihar collect more deposits than they lend within the state.
  • Low branch density — Many rural areas still lack adequate banking facilities.
  • High NPA levels — Some banks in Bihar have high NPAs.
  • Low financial literacy — especially in rural areas.
  • Dependence on informal credit — many rural households still depend on moneylenders.
BPSC Must-Know: Bihar's Credit-Deposit (CD) ratio is among the lowest in India — a major challenge for the state's economic development. Jeevika is Bihar's flagship financial inclusion programme through women's SHGs. Madhya Bihar Gramin Bank, Dakshin Bihar Gramin Bank, and Uttar Bihar Gramin Bank are the three main RRBs operating in the state. These are very high-yield BPSC topics.
