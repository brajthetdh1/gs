Industry & Industrial Policy: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 14 min read · BPSC SSC

India's industrial policy has evolved from the protectionist, public-sector-dominated model of the 1950s–80s to the liberalised, globally integrated model after 1991. Today, the focus is on Make in India, PLI schemes, MSMEs, and industrial corridors. For BPSC and SSC, this topic is high-yield — especially the Industrial Policy Resolutions of 1948, 1956 and 1991, the classification of industries, MSME definitions, and Bihar's industrial landscape.

What is Industrial Policy?

Industrial Policy refers to the government's strategy and action plan to promote and regulate industries — determining the role of the public and private sectors, foreign investment, location of industries, and technology.

Objectives of Industrial Policy in India

  • Rapid industrialisation — building a strong industrial base.
  • Self-reliance — reducing dependence on imports.
  • Balanced regional development — reducing regional disparities.
  • Employment generation — creating jobs outside agriculture.
  • Development of small-scale industries — promoting MSMEs.
  • Prevention of concentration of economic power.
  • Promotion of exports — earning foreign exchange.
  • Modernisation and technology upgradation.

Evolution of Industrial Policy in India

Industrial Policy Resolution, 1948

  • The first industrial policy of independent India.
  • Accepted the mixed economy model — coexistence of public and private sectors.
  • Classified industries into four categories:
    • Exclusive State Monopoly — arms, ammunition, atomic energy, railways.
    • State Monopoly for New Units — coal, iron and steel, aircraft, shipbuilding, minerals, telecom.
    • Regulated Private Sector — 18 industries of national importance.
    • Open to Private Sector — all remaining industries.
  • Emphasised small-scale and cottage industries.

Industrial Policy Resolution, 1956 — "Economic Constitution of India"

Considered the "Magna Carta of Indian Industry" and the "Economic Constitution of India".

  • Based on the Mahalanobis model — focused on heavy and basic industries.
  • Classified industries into three schedules:
    • Schedule A (17 industries) — exclusive responsibility of the State (arms, atomic energy, iron and steel, heavy machinery, etc.).
    • Schedule B (12 industries) — progressively State-owned, with private sector supplementing (mining, aluminium, fertilisers, etc.).
    • Schedule C — all remaining industries — open to private sector.
  • Emphasised regional equality and small-scale industries.
  • Introduced the industrial licensing system.
  • Remained the guiding framework until 1991.
Exam Tip: The Industrial Policy Resolution, 1956 is called the "Economic Constitution of India". It created the three schedules (A, B, C) and gave the public sector a dominant role in heavy industries. The 1991 Industrial Policy dismantled much of this framework — ending the licence raj.

Industrial Policy Statement, 1977

  • Emphasised small-scale and cottage industries.
  • Introduced the concept of District Industries Centres (DICs).
  • Promoted tiny sector — with investment up to ₹1 lakh.
  • Restricted the growth of large industries in certain sectors.

Industrial Policy Statement, 1980

  • Focused on competitiveness of Indian industry.
  • Promoted modernisation and technology upgradation.
  • Encouraged export-oriented industries.

New Industrial Policy, 1991 — The Turning Point

The most significant industrial policy reform in Indian history — part of the LPG reforms (Liberalisation, Privatisation, Globalisation).

  • Abolished industrial licensing — except for 18 industries (later reduced to 5, now 4).
  • Opened the public sector to private and foreign investment.
  • Reduced the number of industries reserved for the public sector — from 17 to 8 (and later to 2 — atomic energy and railways).
  • Disinvestment of public sector undertakings.
  • Liberalised foreign direct investment (FDI).
  • Raised the MRTP limit — freeing large business houses from the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act restrictions.
  • Removed government control over foreign exchange and technology imports.
  • Introduced the automatic route for FDI in many sectors.
  • Established the Foreign Investment Promotion Board (FIPB).

BPSC Must-Know: Industrial Policy Resolution, 1956 = "Economic Constitution of India." New Industrial Policy, 1991 = end of the licence raj and beginning of LPG reforms. Make in India (2014) and PLI Scheme (2020) are the modern-era policies promoting manufacturing and self-reliance.

Note: The MSME classification was revised in 2020 (Atmanirbhar Bharat package) — earlier only investment was the criterion, now turnover is also considered.

On the Basis of Ownership

  • Public Sector — owned by the government.
  • Private Sector — owned by private individuals/companies.
  • Joint Sector — owned jointly by government and private entities.
  • Cooperative Sector — owned by cooperatives.

On the Basis of Raw Materials

  • Agro-based industries — sugar, cotton, jute, textiles.
  • Mineral-based industries — iron and steel, cement, aluminium.
  • Forest-based industries — paper, furniture.
  • Marine-based industries — fish processing.

On the Basis of Product

  • Basic industries — iron and steel, cement, aluminium.
  • Capital goods industries — machinery, machine tools.
  • Intermediate goods industries — chemicals, plastics.
  • Consumer goods industries — textiles, food processing, electronics.

MSME Sector

MSME = Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises. The sector is a major contributor to employment, exports, and GDP.

Contribution of MSMEs

  • ~30% of India's GDP.
  • ~45% of India's exports.
  • ~11 crore jobs — the second-largest employer after agriculture.
  • Over 6 crore MSMEs in India.

Remember: MSMEs contribute ~30% of GDP and ~45% of exports — and employ around 11 crore people. They are the second-largest employer after agriculture. The MUDRA Yojana (2015) provides loans under three categories — Shishu (up to ₹50,000), Kishore (₹50,001–₹5 lakh), Tarun (₹5–10 lakh).

Make in India (2014)

  • Launched on 25 September 2014.
  • Objective: to make India a global manufacturing hub.
  • Focus on 25 sectors — including automobiles, chemicals, IT, pharmaceuticals, textiles, aviation, defence, electronics.
  • Key targets:
    • Increase manufacturing share in GDP to 25%.
    • Create 100 million jobs.
    • Improve Ease of Doing Business ranking.
    • Attract FDI.
  • Complementary initiatives: Skill India, Digital India, Startup India, Stand Up India.

Production Linked Incentive (PLI) Scheme

  • Launched in 2020 — with an outlay of ₹1.97 lakh crore.
  • Provides incentives to manufacturers based on incremental sales.
  • Covers 14 sectors:
    • Mobile manufacturing and specified electronic components.
    • Pharmaceuticals and drugs.
    • Telecom and networking products.
    • Automobiles and auto components.
    • Food products.
    • Textiles.
    • Specialty steel.
    • White goods (ACs and LED lights).
    • Solar PV modules.
    • Advanced chemistry cell battery.
    • Drones and drone components.
    • Pharmaceuticals (bulk drugs).
    • Medical devices.
    • High-efficiency solar PV modules.
  • Aims to boost domestic manufacturing, exports, and self-reliance (Atmanirbhar Bharat).

Atmanirbhar Bharat Abhiyan (2020)

  • Launched in May 2020 — with a package of ₹20 lakh crore (10% of GDP).
  • Five pillars:
    • Economy — quantum jump.
    • Infrastructure — modern and world-class.
    • System — technology-driven.
    • Demography — vibrant.
    • Demand — full utilisation of India's potential.
  • Focus on self-reliance — reducing dependence on imports.
  • Key components:
    • MSME sector — collateral-free loans, equity infusion.
    • Agriculture — infrastructure fund, reforms.
    • FDI reforms — raised FDI limits in defence, telecom, etc.
    • PLI schemes — for manufacturing.

Bihar's connection: The Amritsar-Kolkata Industrial Corridor (AKIC) passes through Bihar — one of the 20 industrial nodes is planned in Bihar (in the Buxar-Bhojpur region).

BPSC Must-Know: Bihar is part of the Amritsar-Kolkata Industrial Corridor (AKIC) — one of the 6 major industrial corridors in India. This is a very high-yield BPSC question. The corridor connects Punjab to West Bengal, passing through Bihar.

Other Key Industrial Initiatives

Startup India (2016)

  • Promotes startups through tax exemptions, funding, and mentorship.
  • India has one of the largest startup ecosystems in the world.

Skill India (2015)

  • Skill development for youth.
  • National Skill Development Corporation (NSDC) — under it.

Digital India (2015)

  • Digitisation of services and governance.
  • Promotes digital payments, e-governance, and IT infrastructure.

National Logistics Policy (2022)

  • Aims to reduce logistics cost and improve efficiency.
  • Target: bring logistics cost from ~13–14% of GDP to below 10%.

National Infrastructure Pipeline (NIP)

  • Launched in 2019.
  • Investment of over ₹100 lakh crore in infrastructure by 2024–25.
  • Covers energy, roads, railways, ports, airports, water, digital infrastructure.

Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs)

  • Maharatna — largest PSUs (e.g. ONGC, IOCL, NTPC, SAIL, BHEL, GAIL, BPCL).
  • Navratna — next tier (e.g. BEL, HAL, NMDC, PFC, REC).
  • Miniratna — Category I and II.
  • Disinvestment — the government sells its stake in PSUs to raise resources.
  • National Asset Monetisation Pipeline — for monetising government assets.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

Bihar's Industrial Landscape

  • Bihar is one of India's least industrialised states.
  • Industrial contribution to GSDP is around 20–25% — lower than the national average.
  • Bihar has limited large-scale industries — most industries are small and medium.
  • Historically, Bihar had industries like:
    • Barauni Refinery (Indian Oil — established 1964).
    • Barauni Thermal Power Station.
    • Bharat Wagon and Engineering (Muzaffarpur).
    • Bata India (Patna — footwear).
    • Hindustan Fertilizer Corporation (Barauni).
    • Sugar mills in north Bihar.
    • Jute mills in Purnia and Katihar.
  • Many of these have closed down or are operating below capacity — a major challenge.

Bihar's Industrial Challenges

  • Low industrial base — after the bifurcation of Bihar and Jharkhand (2000), most mineral and industrial resources went to Jharkhand.
  • Poor infrastructure — roads, power, water, and logistics.
  • Low credit-deposit ratio — banks lend less in Bihar.
  • Small market size — low per capita income limits demand.
  • Land acquisition issues — small landholdings and fragmented holdings.
  • Skilled labour shortage — large-scale out-migration of skilled workers.
  • Political and administrative challenges.

Bihar's Industrial Initiatives

  • Bihar Industrial Investment Promotion Policy — to attract industrial investment.
  • Bihar Startup Policy — to promote startups and entrepreneurship.
  • Bihar Industrial Area Development Authority (BIADA) — for industrial infrastructure.
  • Bihar State Investment Promotion Board (BSIPB) — for investment facilitation.
  • Bihar Industrial Policy, 2016 — incentives for new industries.
  • Bihar Food Processing Policy — for the food processing sector.
  • Bihar Textile and Leather Policy — for textile and leather industries.
  • Bihar Electronics Policy — to promote electronics manufacturing.
  • Bihar Ethanol Production Promotion Policy — for ethanol production.
  • Bihar Agri-Export Policy — for export of agricultural produce.

Bihar Industrial Corridors and Nodes

  • Bihar is part of the Amritsar-Kolkata Industrial Corridor (AKIC).
  • Industrial nodes planned in Bihar — including the Buxar-Bhojpur industrial node.
  • The corridor is expected to boost industrial development in Bihar — but progress has been slow.
  • Bihar also has plans for Industrial Parks and MSME clusters.
  • Bihar Industrial Area Development Authority (BIADA) — manages industrial areas and parks.
  • Bihar Industrial Investment Promotion Policy — to attract investment.
