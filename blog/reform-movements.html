Modern History

Socio-Religious Reform Movements: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 14 min read · BPSC SSC

The 19th century witnessed a remarkable wave of socio-religious reform movements across India. These movements challenged age-old social evils like sati, child marriage, caste discrimination, and the oppression of women, and also responded to the intellectual challenge of Western education. The reformers — from Raja Ram Mohan Roy to Swami Vivekananda — laid the intellectual foundation of Indian nationalism. For BPSC and SSC, the reformers, their organisations, their books, and their contributions are essential — especially because questions on this topic appear every year.

Why Reform Movements Arose

  • British rule — exposed Indians to Western ideas of liberty, equality, and rationalism.
  • Western education — created a new English-educated middle class that questioned old customs.
  • Christian missionaries — their criticism of Hindu practices forced Indians to rethink their own religion.
  • Printing press — made it possible to spread reformist ideas quickly.
  • Rise of nationalism — reformers wanted a strong, modern India free from social evils.
  • Inspiration from Indian heritage — reformers argued that the ancient Indian past was rational and just; the evils were later corruptions.
Exam Tip: The reform movements were of two types — reformist (which worked within the existing framework, like the Brahmo Samaj) and revivalist (which wanted to revive the ancient Indian past, like the Arya Samaj). Understanding this distinction is essential for BPSC and SSC.

1. Brahmo Samaj (1828) — Raja Ram Mohan Roy

Raja Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833) is called the "Father of the Indian Renaissance" and the "First Modern Man of India." He was the pioneer of socio-religious reform in India.

Life & Background

  • Born in Radhanagar, Bengal (1772) into a wealthy Brahmin family.
  • Was educated in Persian, Arabic, Sanskrit, and later English.
  • Worked as a clerk in the East India Company; later became a successful businessman and landlord.
  • Was deeply influenced by the Upanishads, Islam, and Western rationalism.

Reform Work

  • Founded Brahmo Samaj in 1828 — a reformist organisation dedicated to the worship of one God (monotheism) and the rejection of idolatry, caste, and rituals.
  • Abolition of Sati — the greatest achievement of his life. He campaigned relentlessly against Sati and, with the support of Lord William Bentinck, the practice was banned by Regulation XVII of 1829.
  • Advocated women's rights — for the first time, he argued that women should have equal rights in property and education.
  • Opposed child marriage and advocated widow remarriage.
  • Opposed caste discrimination and untouchability.
  • Supported modern education — helped establish the Hindu College (1817) in Calcutta (later Presidency College).
  • Started newspapers — Sambad Kaumudi (in Bengali) and Mirat-ul-Akbar (in Persian).
  • Founded the Atmiya Sabha (1815) — a precursor to the Brahmo Samaj.
  • Championed freedom of the press — opposed press censorship.
  • Visited England in 1830 as an ambassador of the Mughal emperor Akbar II; died in Bristol in 1833.
BPSC Must-Know: Raja Ram Mohan Roy is called the "Father of the Indian Renaissance". He founded the Brahmo Samaj (1828) and was the driving force behind the abolition of Sati (1829). His newspaper Sambad Kaumudi was a landmark in Indian journalism. He is the single most important figure in the socio-religious reform movement.

Later Leaders of Brahmo Samaj

  • Debendranath Tagore (1817–1905) — joined in 1843; reorganised the Brahmo Samaj; father of Rabindranath Tagore.
  • Keshab Chandra Sen (1838–1884) — the most dynamic leader of the Brahmo Samaj after Ram Mohan Roy. His radical views caused a split in 1866 — he founded the Brahmo Samaj of India.
  • The original Brahmo Samaj continued under Debendranath Tagore as the Adi Brahmo Samaj.

2. Prarthana Samaj (1867) — Maharashtra

  • Founded in Bombay in 1867 by Atmaram Pandurang.
  • Was a reformist organisation inspired by the Brahmo Samaj.
  • Mahadev Govind Ranade — a key leader of the Prarthana Samaj — worked extensively for social reform.
  • R.G. Bhandarkar — another prominent leader.
  • The Samaj focused on: abolition of caste, promotion of women's education, widow remarriage, and the upliftment of the depressed classes.
  • Was less radical than the Brahmo Samaj — it did not break away from Hinduism.

3. Arya Samaj (1875) — Swami Dayanand Saraswati

Swami Dayanand Saraswati (1824–1883) founded the Arya Samaj in Bombay in 1875. It was a revivalist movement — it called for a return to the Vedas.

Life

  • Born in Tankara, Gujarat (1824) as Mul Shankar.
  • Was originally a Shaiva Brahmin; left home at the age of 15 to seek spiritual knowledge.
  • Was initiated as a sanyasi by Swami Virajanand — who told him to preach the Vedas.
  • Took the name Dayanand — meaning "one who delights in mercy."

Teachings & Reforms

  • "Back to the Vedas" — the motto of the Arya Samaj.
  • Believed in monotheism — rejected idol worship, ritualism, and the authority of the Puranas.
  • Opposed caste by birth — argued that caste should be determined by merit (karma), not by birth.
  • Opposed child marriage — advocated the marriageable age of 25 for men and 16 for women.
  • Supported widow remarriage and women's education.
  • Opposed untouchability and dowry.
  • Wrote Satyarth Prakash (The Light of Truth) — his most important book.
  • Established a network of schools and colleges — including the Dayanand Anglo-Vedic (DAV) School system.
  • Introduced the Shuddhi movement — a purification ritual to bring converts back to Hinduism.
  • Was a strong critic of Islam and Christianity — which made him controversial.
  • Was assassinated by a religious opponent in 1883 at Ajmer.
Exam Tip: Swami Dayanand Saraswati — founder of the Arya Samaj (1875) — wrote Satyarth Prakash and gave the call "Back to the Vedas." He was the first Indian to advocate caste based on merit, not birth. His DAV schools were a landmark in Indian education.

4. Ramakrishna Mission (1897) — Swami Vivekananda

Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902) — born as Narendranath Datta — was the greatest disciple of Sri Ramakrishna Paramahamsa and one of India's most influential spiritual leaders.

Sri Ramakrishna Paramahamsa (1836–1886)

  • A Bengali mystic and priest at the Dakshineswar Kali Temple.
  • Taught that all religions lead to the same goal — "Jato mat, tato path" ("As many faiths, so many paths").
  • His most famous disciple was Narendranath Datta (Swami Vivekananda).
  • Died in 1886; his disciples founded the Ramakrishna Order in his memory.

Swami Vivekananda

  • Born in Calcutta (1863); was a brilliant student and a graduate of Calcutta University.
  • Met Ramakrishna in 1881 and became his disciple.
  • After Ramakrishna's death (1886), he travelled across India as a wandering monk — visiting temples, villages, and meeting people of all classes.
  • 1893 — The Parliament of Religions at Chicago — his famous address began with "Sisters and Brothers of America" and won worldwide acclaim.
  • Founded the Ramakrishna Mission in 1897 — dedicated to social service, education, and relief work.
  • Preached the message of "Atmano Mokshartham Jagat Hitaya Cha" — "For one's own salvation and for the welfare of the world."
  • Called for man-making education and service to humanity.
  • Wrote Raja Yoga, Jnana Yoga, Karma Yoga, Bhakti Yoga.
  • Was a strong critic of caste, untouchability, and the oppression of women.
  • His message: "Arise, awake, and stop not till the goal is reached."
  • Died in 1902 at Belur Math, near Calcutta.
BPSC Must-Know: Swami Vivekananda founded the Ramakrishna Mission (1897). His speech at the Parliament of Religions, Chicago (1893) made him world-famous. He preached man-making education and service to humanity. His birthday (12 January) is celebrated as National Youth Day in India.

5. Theosophical Society (1875)

  • Founded in New York in 1875 by Madame H.P. Blavatsky and Colonel H.S. Olcott.
  • The Indian headquarters was established at Adyar, Madras (1882).
  • Annie Besant (1847–1933) — an Irishwoman and former socialist — became the leader of the Theosophical Society in India after Blavatsky's death.
  • The Society promoted the study of ancient religions, philosophy, and occultism — especially Hinduism and Buddhism.
  • Annie Besant founded the Central Hindu College at Banaras (1898) — which later became Banaras Hindu University (BHU).
  • Annie Besant later joined the Indian National Congress and became its first woman president in 1917.
  • She also founded the Home Rule League (1916) — with Bal Gangadhar Tilak.

6. Satyashodhak Samaj (1873) — Jyotiba Phule

Jyotiba Phule (1827–1890) was a social reformer from Maharashtra who worked for the upliftment of the lower castes and women.

  • Founded the Satyashodhak Samaj (Truth-Seekers' Society) in 1873.
  • Worked for the upliftment of Shudras and Ati-Shudras (lower castes and untouchables).
  • Was the first Indian to start a school for girls — in Pune in 1848.
  • Also started schools for untouchables.
  • Wrote Gulamgiri (Slavery) — a powerful critique of caste and Brahminical domination.
  • His wife Savitribai Phule was the first woman teacher in India — a pioneer of women's education.
  • Worked for widow remarriage and against child marriage.
  • His work inspired later reformers like B.R. Ambedkar.
Remember: Jyotiba Phule founded the Satyashodhak Samaj (1873) and started the first school for girls in India (1848, Pune). His wife Savitribai Phule was India's first woman teacher. His book Gulamgiri is a landmark text on caste oppression.

7. Aligarh Movement (1875) — Sir Syed Ahmad Khan

Sir Syed Ahmad Khan (1817–1898) was the greatest Muslim reformer of the 19th century. His movement is called the Aligarh Movement — after the city where his educational work was centred.

  • Founded the Muhammadan Anglo-Oriental College (MAO College) at Aligarh in 1875 — which later became Aligarh Muslim University (AMU).
  • His main aim was to promote modern education among Muslims — especially English education.
  • Preached that Muslims should accept modern science and Western education while remaining true to Islam.
  • Was a strong critic of the Wahhabi movement and of religious orthodoxy.
  • Worked for Hindu-Muslim unity in his early years — later, his position became more communal.
  • Founded the Scientific Society (1864) — to translate Western works into Urdu.
  • Wrote Tahzib-ul-Akhlaq (Mohammedan Social Reformer).
  • His movement led to the establishment of several schools and colleges for Muslims across India.
  • Was knighted by the British in 1888.

8. Other Important Reform Movements

Muslim Reform Movements

  • Deoband Movement (1866) — founded by Muhammad Qasim Nanautavi and Rashid Ahmad Gangohi at Deoband (UP). It was a revivalist movement that emphasised the study of Islamic law and the Quran. It remained aloof from politics.
  • Nadwat-ul-Ulema (1894) — founded at Lucknow to promote religious education and reform.
  • Ahmadiyya Movement (1889) — founded by Mirza Ghulam Ahmad in Punjab. It was a revivalist movement within Islam.

Sikh Reform Movements

  • Singh Sabha Movement (1873) — founded at Amritsar by Sikh reformers to purify Sikhism and promote education.
  • Akali Movement (1920) — led by the Akali Dal to reform the management of Sikh gurdwaras.
  • Resulted in the Sikh Gurdwaras Act (1925) — which placed gurdwaras under a representative body.

Parsi Reform Movements

  • Rahnumai Mazdayasnan Sabha (1851) — founded by Parsi reformers to reform Zoroastrianism.
  • Key leaders: Dadabhai Naoroji, Naoroji Furdoonji.
  • Worked for women's education and the abolition of polygamy.

Depressed Classes Movements

  • Jyotiba Phule (1827–1890) — Satyashodhak Samaj; worked for Shudras and women.
  • B.R. Ambedkar (1891–1956) — 20th-century leader of the depressed classes; founded the Bahishkrit Hitakarini Sabha (1924) and led the Mahad Satyagraha (1927).
  • Sri Narayana Guru (1856–1928) — Kerala reformer; gave the message "One Caste, One Religion, One God for Man."
  • Periyar E.V. Ramaswami Naicker (1879–1973) — founder of the Self-Respect Movement (1925) in Tamil Nadu; champion of anti-Brahminism and women's rights.

Women's Reform Movements

  • Raja Ram Mohan Roy — the first to champion women's rights; drove the abolition of Sati (1829).
  • Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar (1820–1891) — the greatest champion of widow remarriage. His efforts led to the Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act, 1856.
  • Jyotiba Phule — started the first school for girls (1848).
  • Savitribai Phule — India's first woman teacher.
  • Pandita Ramabai (1858–1922) — champion of women's education; founded the Sharda Sadan (1889) in Bombay for widows.
  • Ramabai Ranade (1863–1924) — founded the Seva Sadan (1909) for women's welfare; wife of M.G. Ranade.
  • Annie Besant — founded the Central Hindu College (1898).
  • Sarojini Naidu (1879–1949) — the "Nightingale of India"; first Indian woman president of the INC (1925).
  • Begum Rokeya Sakhawat Hossain (1880–1932) — Muslim women's education pioneer; founded the Sakhawat Memorial Girls' School (1911).
Exam Tip: Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar was the greatest champion of widow remarriage — his efforts led to the Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act, 1856. Pandita Ramabai founded the Sharda Sadan (1889). Ramabai Ranade founded the Seva Sadan (1909). These are all frequently asked.

Significance of the Reform Movements

  • Eradicated social evils — sati, child marriage, female infanticide, and untouchability were challenged for the first time.
  • Improved the status of women — women's education, widow remarriage, and property rights became major issues.
  • Challenged caste discrimination — reformers questioned the caste system and worked for the upliftment of the depressed classes.
  • Revived Indian culture — reformers made Indians proud of their ancient heritage.
  • Created national consciousness — the reform movements prepared the ground for the freedom struggle.
  • Spread modern education — schools, colleges, and universities were established across India.
  • Inspired later leaders — Mahatma Gandhi, B.R. Ambedkar, and others built on the work of the 19th-century reformers.
  • Produced a new middle class — English-educated Indians who would lead the nationalist movement.

Limitations of the Reform Movements

  • Limited to urban areas — the reform movements mainly affected the cities; the vast rural population remained untouched.
  • Limited to the middle class — the reformers were mostly from the English-educated middle class.
  • Did not challenge the British — most reformers were loyal to the British and did not demand political freedom.
  • Divisions among reformers — the split between reformists and revivalists weakened the movement.
  • Women's issues were secondary — even among reformers, women were largely passive participants.
  • Religious overtones — the movements often remained within religious frameworks rather than becoming fully secular.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • Bihar Sharif and Maner Sharif — remained major Sufi centres during the reform period.
  • Rajendra Prasad (1884–1963) — born in Zeradei (Siwan, Bihar); became a key leader of the Indian National Congress and the first President of India. He was influenced by the reformist spirit of the age.
  • Anugrah Narayan Sinha (1887–1957) — "Bihar Vibhuti"; key leader of the freedom struggle in Bihar.
  • Dr. Rajendra Prasad and Sri Krishna Sinha — worked for social reform in Bihar alongside the freedom struggle.
  • Bihar's caste reform — the Kayastha, Bhumihar, and Rajput communities saw internal reform movements in the 19th and 20th centuries.
  • Women's education in Bihar — reformers like Smt. Radhika Sinha (wife of Dr. Rajendra Prasad) promoted women's education.
  • Arya Samaj in Bihar — spread in the early 20th century; established schools and promoted social reform.
  • Satyashodhak Samaj influence — in Bihar, the Triveni Sangh (1933) and other organisations worked for the upliftment of backward castes.
  • Bihar's role in national reform movements — Bihar produced several national leaders who carried the reform agenda forward.
BPSC Must-Know: Dr. Rajendra Prasad — born in Zeradei (Siwan, Bihar) — was a leading figure of both the freedom struggle and social reform in Bihar. He became the first President of India (1950–1962). His life and work reflect the reformist spirit of the 19th century applied to Bihar.
