Poverty, Unemployment & Human Development: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 14 min read · BPSC SSC

Poverty, unemployment and human development are the three most critical dimensions of India's socio-economic challenges. Poverty is about deprivation — of income, food, health, education and dignity. Unemployment is about lack of productive work. And human development is about expanding people's choices and capabilities. For BPSC and SSC, this topic is extremely high-yield — especially the Tendulkar and Rangarajan committees, types of unemployment, MGNREGA, and HDI. For Bihar, poverty and unemployment are central to the state's development story.

What is Poverty?

Poverty is a state where a person or household lacks the resources to meet basic needs — food, shelter, clothing, health and education.

Types of Poverty

TypeMeaning
Absolute PovertyIncome below a minimum threshold (poverty line) required for basic needs
Relative PovertyIncome below the average income of society — a measure of inequality
Subjective PovertyBased on people's own perception of their well-being
Chronic PovertyLong-term, persistent poverty across generations
Transient PovertyTemporary poverty due to shocks (illness, disaster, job loss)

Poverty Line

  • The poverty line is the minimum level of income or consumption required to meet basic needs.
  • In India, the poverty line is defined on the basis of calorie intake and consumption expenditure.
  • Traditional calorie norms: 2,400 calories per person per day in rural areas and 2,100 calories in urban areas.
  • The poverty line is updated periodically by the NITI Aayog (formerly the Planning Commission).

Poverty Estimation in India — Committees

Working Group (1962)

  • First official attempt to define the poverty line.
  • Recommended ₹20 per person per month (rural) and ₹25 (urban) at 1960–61 prices.
  • Based on calorie norms.

Dandekar and Rath Committee (1971)

  • Used the calorie norm of 2,250 calories per person per day.
  • Estimated poverty at around 40% of the population in 1968–69.

Alagh Committee (1979)

  • Task Force under Y.K. Alagh.
  • Defined poverty line on the basis of calorie norms — 2,400 calories rural, 2,100 urban.
  • Recommended separate poverty lines for rural and urban areas.

Lakdawala Committee (1993)

  • Chaired by D.T. Lakdawala.
  • Shifted from calorie norms to consumption expenditure as the basis for poverty estimation.
  • Poverty line updated using CPI-IW (Consumer Price Index for Industrial Workers) and CPI-AL (Agricultural Labourers).
  • State-specific poverty lines — adjusted for state-level price differences.
  • Used by the Planning Commission until 2009.

Tendulkar Committee (2009)

  • Chaired by Suresh Tendulkar.
  • Shifted poverty estimation from calorie norms to a broader basket of goods and services — including health and education.
  • Used Mixed Reference Period (MRP) for consumption data.
  • Estimated poverty at 37.2% in 2004–05 (rural: 41.8%, urban: 25.7%).
  • Estimated poverty at 21.9% in 2011–12 — a major decline.
  • Poverty line for 2011–12: ₹816 per month per person (rural) and ₹1,000 (urban).

Rangarajan Committee (2014)

  • Chaired by C. Rangarajan.
  • Revised poverty line upward — with higher expenditure thresholds.
  • Estimated poverty at 29.5% in 2011–12 (higher than the Tendulkar estimate).
  • Poverty line: ₹972 per month (rural) and ₹1,407 (urban).
  • Recommended separate rural and urban poverty lines with independent state-level estimates.
  • The Rangarajan recommendations were not officially accepted by the government.
BPSC Must-Know: The Tendulkar Committee (2009) shifted poverty estimation from calorie norms to a broader basket including health and education. It estimated poverty at 21.9% in 2011–12. The Rangarajan Committee (2014) revised the poverty line upward and estimated poverty at 29.5% in 2011–12 — but its recommendations were not officially accepted.

Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI)

  • Developed by the UNDP and OPHI (Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative).
  • Measures poverty across three dimensions — Health, Education, and Standard of Living.
  • India's MPI has declined significantly — the latest National MPI (NITI Aayog) shows a sharp reduction.
  • NITI Aayog publishes the National Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) for India.

MGNREGA — Landmark Legislation

  • Enacted in 2005 — came into force on 2 February 2006.
  • Also called the "Right to Work" Act.
  • Provides a legal guarantee of 100 days of wage employment in a financial year to every rural household.
  • Work is to be provided within 5 km of the applicant's residence.
  • If work is not provided within 15 days, the applicant is entitled to an unemployment allowance.
  • At least one-third of the beneficiaries must be women.
  • Wages are to be paid within 15 days — through bank/post office accounts.
  • Social audit is mandatory — Gram Sabha has a crucial role.
  • It is the largest employment guarantee programme in the world.
Exam Tip: MGNREGA was enacted in 2005 and came into force on 2 February 2006. It guarantees 100 days of wage employment per rural household per year. If work is not provided within 15 days, an unemployment allowance must be paid. One-third of beneficiaries must be women. This is a very high-yield BPSC and SSC topic.

Unemployment in India

Unemployment is a situation where people who are willing and able to work at the prevailing wage rate cannot find jobs.

Types of Unemployment

TypeMeaning
Structural UnemploymentDue to a mismatch between skills and job requirements; caused by structural changes in the economy
Cyclical UnemploymentDue to a downturn in the business cycle — recession or depression
Frictional UnemploymentTemporary unemployment when people move between jobs
Seasonal UnemploymentDue to seasonal nature of work — especially in agriculture
Disguised UnemploymentMore people employed than actually needed — common in Indian agriculture
Open UnemploymentPeople willing to work but unable to find any work
Educated UnemploymentEducated youth unable to find jobs matching their qualifications
Technological UnemploymentDue to automation replacing human labour
Remember: Disguised unemployment is a key feature of Indian agriculture — more people work on a farm than required, so their marginal productivity is close to zero. Educated unemployment is a serious challenge in India — lakhs of graduates are unable to find jobs matching their qualifications.

Measures of Unemployment in India

MeasureMeaning
Usual Status (US)Based on the reference period of 365 days — the person is classified as employed/unemployed based on activity during the year
Current Weekly Status (CWS)Based on the reference period of 7 days
Current Daily Status (CDS)Based on the reference period of each day of the week

Note: The PLFS (Periodic Labour Force Survey), conducted by the NSO, is the primary source of unemployment data in India. It was started in 2017–18 — replacing the earlier quinquennial Employment-Unemployment Surveys of the NSSO.

Types of Unemployment by Reference Period

  • Usual Principal Status (UPS) — most commonly used measure.
  • Usual Principal and Subsidiary Status (UPSS) — broader; includes subsidiary work.
  • Current Weekly Status (CWS) — 7-day reference.
  • Current Daily Status (CDS) — daily reference; captures underemployment.

Unemployment Rate in India

  • According to the PLFS 2022–23, the unemployment rate in India was around 3.2% (Usual Status).
  • Youth unemployment is much higher — especially in the 15–29 age group.
  • Female labour force participation in India remains low — a major concern.
  • Underemployment is widespread — many people work less than they are capable of, especially in agriculture.

Human Development

Human Development is the process of enlarging people's choices — expanding their capabilities and creating conditions for a decent standard of living.

Human Development Index (HDI)

  • Developed by Mahbub ul Haq (1990) — with Amartya Sen's intellectual contribution.
  • Published annually by the UNDP.
  • Three dimensions:
    • Health — Life expectancy at birth.
    • Education — Mean years of schooling and expected years of schooling.
    • Standard of Living — GNI per capita (PPP).
  • HDI ranges from 0 to 1.
  • Categories: Very High (≥0.800), High (0.700–0.799), Medium (0.550–0.699), Low (<0.550).
  • India is in the Medium Human Development category — with an HDI around 0.644 and a rank around 134 (per latest report).

Other Human Development Indices

Poverty and Unemployment in Bihar (BPSC Focus)

Poverty in Bihar

  • Bihar has historically been one of India's poorest states.
  • According to the Tendulkar Committee (2011–12), Bihar's poverty ratio was around 33.7% — one of the highest among major states.
  • Bihar's poverty has declined significantly in recent decades — but remains above the national average.
  • Bihar's Multidimensional Poverty Index (MPI) has also declined sharply — one of the fastest reductions in India.
  • Key dimensions of poverty in Bihar:
    • Low per capita income.
    • Low literacy and education levels.
    • Limited health infrastructure.
    • Dependence on agriculture — with low productivity.
    • Recurring floods and droughts.
    • High population density.
    • Large-scale out-migration.

Unemployment in Bihar

  • Bihar has one of the highest unemployment rates among Indian states — especially among youth.
  • Educated unemployment is a serious challenge in Bihar — lakhs of graduates and post-graduates are unemployed.
  • Disguised unemployment in agriculture is very high — most farmers have very small landholdings.
  • Seasonal unemployment is common — agriculture provides work only for a few months.
  • Out-migration is very high — lakhs of Bihari workers migrate to other states and abroad for work.
  • Remittances from migrants are a major source of income for Bihar.
  • Low industrialisation limits non-farm employment opportunities.
  • Skill gaps — many youth lack employable skills.

Bihar's Poverty Alleviation and Employment Initiatives

  • Jeevika — Bihar Rural Livelihoods Project — promotes SHGs and rural livelihoods.
  • MGNREGA — implemented across Bihar; lakhs of rural households get employment.
  • Mukhyamantri Udyami Yojana — support for young entrepreneurs.
  • Bihar Student Credit Card Scheme — for higher education.
  • Mukhyamantri Kanya Utthan Yojana — for girls' education and empowerment.
  • Bihar Skill Development Mission — skilling youth for employment.
  • Saat Nishchay — the state's flagship programme for development.
  • Bihar Startup Policy — promoting entrepreneurship.
  • PM Awaas Yojana (Gramin) — housing for the rural poor.
  • Bihar Rural Livelihoods Promotion Society (BRLPS) — implements Jeevika.
  • Public Distribution System (PDS) — food security for the poor.
  • Mukhyamantri Vidyarthi Pratibha Yojana — for meritorious students.

Bihar's HDI Position

  • Bihar has historically ranked low on the HDI — among the bottom states in India.
  • Bihar's HDI has improved in recent years — but remains below the national average.
  • Key improvements:
    • Life expectancy has increased.
    • Infant mortality has declined.
    • Literacy rate has improved.
    • School enrolment has increased.
    • Poverty has declined.
  • Key challenges:
    • Low per capita income — among the lowest in India.
    • High dropout rates in schools.
    • Poor health indicators in rural areas.
    • Low female labour force participation.
    • High out-migration of young workers.
BPSC Must-Know: Bihar has one of the highest unemployment rates among Indian states — especially educated unemployment. Out-migration from Bihar is very high — remittances are a major source of income. Jeevika is Bihar's flagship poverty alleviation and livelihood programme. Bihar's HDI and per capita income remain below the national average — but have improved significantly in recent years. These are frequently asked BPSC topics.
