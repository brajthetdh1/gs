Slave Dynasty (Ghulam Vansh): Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 13 min read · BPSC SSC

The Slave Dynasty (1206–1290 CE) — also called the Mamluk Dynasty or Ghulam Vansh — was the first of the five dynasties of the Delhi Sultanate. Founded by Qutb-ud-din Aibak, a former slave of Muhammad Ghori, it produced remarkable rulers like Iltutmish, Razia Sultan (the only woman to rule Delhi), and Ghiyas-ud-din Balban. For BPSC and SSC, the Slave Dynasty is one of the most important topics in medieval Indian history — especially for questions on administration, the Iqta system, and Indo-Islamic architecture.

Exam Tip: Tabaqat-i-Nasiri by Minhaj-us-Siraj is the most important contemporary source for the Slave Dynasty. He was the court historian of Iltutmish and also served as the Chief Qazi of Delhi under Nasir-ud-din Mahmud.

Foundation of the Delhi Sultanate

  • Muhammad Ghori defeated Prithviraj Chauhan in the Second Battle of Tarain (1192 CE) and laid the foundation of Turkish rule in India.
  • Ghori's trusted slave and general Qutb-ud-din Aibak was left in charge of the Indian conquests.
  • After Ghori's death in 1206 CE, Aibak declared independence and founded the Slave Dynasty — the first dynasty of the Delhi Sultanate.
  • The name "Slave Dynasty" comes from the fact that its early rulers (Aibak, Iltutmish, Balban) were originally slaves (Mamluks) who rose to power through merit.
  • Capital: initially Lahore, later shifted to Delhi by Iltutmish.

Qutb-ud-din Aibak (1206–1210 CE)

Founder of the Slave Dynasty and the Delhi Sultanate. A former slave of Muhammad Ghori who rose to become one of the greatest rulers of his time.

  • Titles: Lakh Baksh ("giver of lakhs" — for his generosity), Sultan.
  • Made Lahore his capital (later shifted to Delhi by Iltutmish).
  • Started construction of the Qutb Minar at Delhi (completed by Iltutmish).
  • Built the Quwwat-ul-Islam Mosque — the first mosque in India (built on the site of a Hindu temple).
  • Built the Adhai Din Ka Jhonpra mosque at Ajmer.
  • Patronised the scholar Hasan Nizami (author of Taj-ul-Maasir).
  • Died in 1210 CE while playing Chaugan (polo) at Lahore — his horse fell on him.
  • Was succeeded by his son Aram Shah, who was soon deposed by Iltutmish.
Remember: Qutb-ud-din Aibak was called "Lakh Baksh" for his generosity. He started the Qutb Minar, but it was completed by his successor Iltutmish. He died in 1210 CE while playing Chaugan (polo).

Iltutmish (1211–1236 CE) — The Real Founder

Son-in-law of Aibak and the greatest ruler of the Slave Dynasty. He is called the "real founder of the Delhi Sultanate" for consolidating Turkish rule in India.

Early Challenges

  • Faced opposition from Aram Shah (Aibak's son), whom he defeated at Delhi in 1211 CE.
  • Faced the threat of Yildiz (Ghori's governor at Ghazni) and Qubacha (governor of Multan and Uch).
  • Defeated Yildiz at the Battle of Tarain (1216 CE) and Qubacha by 1228 CE.
  • Repelled the invasion of Mongol Chengiz Khan (who reached the Indus in 1221 CE) — Iltutmish wisely refused to give shelter to the Khwarizm prince Jalal-ud-din, avoiding Mongol wrath.

Consolidation & Administration

  • Shifted the capital from Lahore to Delhi — Delhi became the permanent seat of the Sultanate.
  • Organised the Iqta system — land assignments (Iqtas) given to nobles (Iqtadars) in return for military service and revenue collection.
  • Created the Chalisa — a group of 40 Turkish nobles who became the most powerful faction at court (later a source of trouble).
  • Introduced the silver Tanka and copper Jital coins — standardised currency.
  • Completed the Qutb Minar (started by Aibak).
  • Built the Tomb of Iltutmish at Delhi — the first domed tomb in India (with the arhai-din-ka-jhonpra style).
  • Patronised Minhaj-us-Siraj, who wrote Tabaqat-i-Nasiri.
  • Appointed his daughter Razia as his successor — a remarkable decision for the time.
Exam Tip: Iltutmish's Iqta system and the Chalisa (group of 40 nobles) are frequently asked. He is called the "real founder of the Delhi Sultanate" because he consolidated Turkish rule and organised its administration. He introduced the silver Tanka and copper Jital.

Architecture under Iltutmish

  • Qutb Minar — completed; the tallest stone tower in India (72.5 metres).
  • Tomb of Iltutmish — the first domed tomb in India; located near the Qutb Minar.
  • Sultan Ghari — tomb of his son Nasir-ud-din Mahmud at Delhi.

Razia Sultan (1236–1240 CE) — The Only Woman Ruler of Delhi

Daughter of Iltutmish and the only woman to ever rule Delhi. Her reign was brief but significant — she faced constant opposition from the Turkish nobles.

  • Appointed as successor by Iltutmish, bypassing her brothers.
  • Assumed the title Jalalat-ud-din Razia.
  • Refused to be called "Sultana" and insisted on being called Sultan.
  • Abandoned the purdah, held open court, and personally led armies — an unprecedented act for a Muslim woman of her time.
  • Appointed Jamal-ud-din Yaqut, an Abyssinian (Ethiopian) noble, as her closest advisor — which angered the Turkish nobles.
  • Faced revolts in Multan, Badaun, Hansi and Lahore.
  • Defeated the rebel governor Kabir Khan and the ruler of Ranthambore.
  • Her growing closeness to Yaqut led to a conspiracy by the Turkish nobles.
  • Deposed and killed in 1240 CE near Kaithal — she was reportedly killed by bandits after being abandoned by her allies.
  • After her death, the Sultanate was ruled by a series of weak rulers controlled by the Chalisa.
BPSC Must-Know: Razia Sultan (1236–1240 CE) was the only woman ruler of the Delhi Sultanate. Her efforts to rule independently angered the Turkish nobles, who conspired against her. She was killed in 1240 CE.

The "Chalisa" and the Period of Chaos (1240–1266 CE)

After Razia's death, the Chalisa (group of 40 Turkish nobles) became the real power behind the throne. They raised and deposed rulers at will.

Ghiyas-ud-din Balban (1266–1287 CE)

The greatest ruler after Iltutmish. He was originally a slave (called "Bahadur") and rose to become Naib (deputy) during Nasir-ud-din Mahmud's reign. He assumed full power in 1266 CE.

Balban's Policies

  • Blood and Iron Policy — ruthless suppression of rebels, especially the Rajputs and the Mongols.
  • Broken the power of the Chalisa — the 40 nobles who had controlled earlier rulers.
  • Restored the prestige of the Sultan — introduced elaborate court etiquette (Sijda and Paibos — prostration and kissing the feet).
  • Declared himself Niyabat-i-Khudai ("deputy of God") and Zill-i-Ilahi ("shadow of God on Earth").
  • Reorganised the army and the spy system (Barid and Munhiyan).
  • Appointed Fakhr-ud-din as the first Diwan-i-Arz (Minister of Military Affairs).
  • Suppressed the Mewati rebels (around Delhi) and the Rajputs of Doab.
  • Defeated the Mongol invasions — his son Muhammad Khan died fighting the Mongols in 1285 CE.
  • Appointed his second son Bughra Khan as governor of Bengal, and his grandson Kaiqubad as governor of Multan.
  • Died in 1287 CE at Delhi; buried in the Tomb of Balban (Delhi) — the first true arch and dome in India.
Exam Tip: Balban's "Blood and Iron" policy and his introduction of Sijda and Paibos (prostration before the Sultan) are frequently asked. He declared himself Zill-i-Ilahi ("shadow of God"). His tomb is the first structure in India with a true arch and dome.

Decline After Balban

  • After Balban's death, his grandson Kaiqubad became Sultan — a weak and pleasure-loving ruler.
  • Real power passed to the nobles, particularly the Khaljis.
  • In 1290 CE, Jalal-ud-din Khalji overthrew Kaiqubad and founded the Khalji Dynasty — ending the Slave Dynasty.

Administration of the Slave Dynasty

Central Administration

  • Sultan — head of state; the office was considered divine (Balban emphasised this).
  • Wazir — Prime Minister; looked after finance and general administration.
  • Diwan-i-Arz — Minister of Military Affairs (first appointed by Balban).
  • Diwan-i-Risalat — Minister of Religious Affairs and Endowments.
  • Diwan-i-Insha — Minister of Correspondence.
  • Qazi — Chief Judge; Mufti — legal advisor.
  • Barid and Munhiyan — intelligence and spy system.

Iqta System

  • Introduced by Iltutmish — the most important administrative innovation of the Slave Dynasty.
  • Iqta — a piece of land assigned to a noble (Iqtadar or Muqti) in return for military service and revenue collection.
  • The Iqtadar collected revenue from the Iqta, kept a part for himself and his troops, and sent the surplus to the Sultan.
  • Iqtadars were transferable — they could be moved from one Iqta to another.
  • The Iqta system later evolved into the Jagirdari system of the Mughals.

Military Administration

  • Standing army maintained by the Sultan — including cavalry, infantry and elephants.
  • Turkish soldiers were the elite; Indian Muslims and Hindus also served.
  • Balban reorganised the army and introduced new ranks.
  • Fortified Delhi and set up military outposts on the Mongol frontier.

Revenue Administration

  • Land revenue was the main source of income — usually 1/5 to 1/2 of produce (Kharaj).
  • Other taxes: Zakat (2.5% on Muslims), Jizya (on non-Muslims), Khams (1/5 of booty).
  • Taxes on trade, cattle, and irrigation.

Art & Architecture of the Slave Dynasty

The Slave Dynasty laid the foundation of Indo-Islamic architecture in India — a blend of Islamic and Indian styles.

Features of Slave Dynasty Architecture

  • Arches and domes — introduced in India; the Tomb of Balban has the first true arch and dome.
  • Calligraphy — Arabic inscriptions used for decoration.
  • Reuse of Hindu temple materials — many early mosques were built with materials from demolished temples (e.g. Quwwat-ul-Islam Mosque).
  • Qutb Minar — 72.5 metres tall; five storeys; blend of Indian and Islamic styles.
  • Arabesque — geometric and floral patterns in stone.
  • Arhai-din-ka-jhonpra (Ajmer) — "two-and-a-half-day hut" — a former Sanskrit college converted into a mosque.
Exam Tip: The Tomb of Balban is the first structure in India to use a true arch and dome. The Qutb Minar was started by Aibak and completed by Iltutmish; it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Society & Culture under the Slave Dynasty

  • Society was cosmopolitan at the top — Turkish nobles, Persian scholars, Indian Muslims, and Hindus.
  • Turkish nobles held the highest positions; Indian Muslims faced discrimination.
  • Hindus were taxed (Jizya) but were often employed in administration and the army.
  • Sufism flourished — the Chishti Order was established in India by Khwaja Muin-ud-din Chishti (Ajmer, c. 1192 CE).
  • Persian literature was patronised — Hasan Nizami, Minhaj-us-Siraj, Amir Khusrau (early period).
  • Amir Khusrau — the "Parrot of India" — began his career under the later Slave rulers and became famous under the Khaljis.
  • Music and dance — Indian traditions blended with Persian ones; Qawwali is attributed to Amir Khusrau.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • Muhammad Ghori's campaigns in India included Bihar — his general Qutb-ud-din Aibak campaigned in the region.
  • Bakhtiyar Khalji (a general of Muhammad Ghori) destroyed Nalanda University and Vikramashila in Bihar around 1193–1205 CE — a major blow to Buddhism in India.
  • Bihar was an important province of the Delhi Sultanate — governed by a Muqti (governor).
  • Iltutmish appointed a governor for Bihar; the region remained under Turkish control throughout the Slave Dynasty.
  • Maner Sharif (near Patna) — a Sufi centre that developed during this period.
  • Bihar Sharif (Nalanda district) — became a major Sufi centre in the 13th century.
  • Balban sent his son Bughra Khan to govern Bengal and Bihar — establishing a strong Turkish hold on the region.
BPSC Must-Know: Bakhtiyar Khalji's destruction of Nalanda University (c. 1200 CE) is a landmark event in Bihar's history. It marked the end of Nalanda as a great centre of learning. Bihar Sharif and Maner Sharif became major Sufi centres during the Slave Dynasty period.

Decline of the Slave Dynasty

  • Weak successors after Balban — Kaiqubad was incompetent and pleasure-loving.
  • Rise of the Khalji faction — the Khalji nobles gradually gained power at court.
  • Rebellions and conspiracies weakened the central authority.
  • Mongol threat continued on the northwest frontier.
  • In 1290 CE, Jalal-ud-din Khalji — the commander of the army — overthrew Kaiqubad and founded the Khalji Dynasty.
