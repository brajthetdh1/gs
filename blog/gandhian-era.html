Gandhian Era: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 16 min read · BPSC SSC

The Gandhian Era (1915–1948) was the most decisive phase of India's freedom struggle. When Mahatma Gandhi returned to India in 1915, the Congress was a middle-class debating society. By the time he left the political stage in 1947, India had won independence. Gandhi transformed the freedom struggle into a mass movement — bringing peasants, workers, women, and the poor into the national mainstream. His tools were Satyagraha (truth-force) and Ahimsa (non-violence). For BPSC and SSC, the Gandhian era is the single most important topic in Modern Indian History — especially the Champaran Satyagraha (1917) which began in Bihar.

South Africa Years (1893–1914)

  • Went to South Africa in 1893 as a lawyer for a case in Durban.
  • Was thrown off a train at Pietermaritzburg for travelling in a first-class compartment reserved for whites — a life-changing experience.
  • Fought against racial discrimination against Indians in South Africa for 21 years.
  • Developed his philosophy of Satyagraha — using truth and non-violence to resist injustice.
  • Founded the Natal Indian Congress (1894).
  • Returned to India in 1915 — by then he was already a well-known leader.
Exam Tip: Gandhi returned to India on 9 January 1915. This day is celebrated as Pravasi Bharatiya Divas (Overseas Indian Day). He was welcomed back by Gopal Krishna Gokhale, who became his political mentor.

Gandhi's Early Campaigns in India (1917–1918)

1. Champaran Satyagraha (1917) — Bihar

The Champaran Satyagraha was Gandhi's first Satyagraha in India. It took place in Champaran, Bihar — one of the most important events in Bihar's history.

  • Cause — the Tinkathia system (also called the Teen Kathia system). Under this system, peasants were forced to grow indigo on 3/20th of their land and sell it to European planters at cheap prices.
  • Peasants were subjected to brutal exploitation by British planters.
  • The peasants were also forced to pay begar (unpaid labour) and illegal taxes.
  • Raj Kumar Shukla — a poor farmer of Champaran — invited Gandhi to visit Champaran.
  • Dr. Rajendra Prasad — later the first President of India — also played a key role in bringing Gandhi to Champaran.
  • Gandhi arrived in Champaran on 10 April 1917 with Raj Kumar Shukla and other leaders.
  • The British ordered Gandhi to leave Champaran — he refused. He was arrested but released. The government had to back down.
  • The Champaran Inquiry Committee was appointed — with Gandhi as a member.
  • The Champaran Agrarian Act, 1918 was passed — it abolished the Tinkathia system.
  • Success — Gandhi's first Satyagraha in India was a complete success.
  • Key supporters: Dr. Rajendra Prasad, Brajkishore Prasad, Maulana Mazharul Haque, Anugrah Narayan Sinha, J.B. Kripalani.
BPSC Must-Know: The Champaran Satyagraha (1917) was Gandhi's first Satyagraha in India. It began in Champaran, Bihar against the Tinkathia system of indigo cultivation. The Champaran Agrarian Act (1918) abolished the system. Key leaders: Raj Kumar Shukla, Dr. Rajendra Prasad, Brajkishore Prasad, Anugrah Narayan Sinha. This is the single most important BPSC topic in Modern Indian History.

2. Ahmedabad Mill Strike (1918)

  • Gandhi intervened in a dispute between mill workers and mill owners in Ahmedabad.
  • The workers were demanding better wages and working conditions.
  • Gandhi undertook a hunger strike (fast unto death) to support the workers.
  • The mill owners relented and the workers got a 35% wage hike.
  • This was Gandhi's first hunger strike in India.

3. Kheda Satyagraha (1918)

  • Kheda district in Gujarat was hit by famine and crop failure.
  • The peasants could not pay the land revenue.
  • Gandhi led a no-tax campaign — the peasants refused to pay revenue.
  • The British government eventually granted remission of revenue.
  • Key supporter: Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel — this was his political debut.
Remember: The Ahmedabad Mill Strike (1918) saw Gandhi's first hunger strike. The Kheda Satyagraha (1918) was a no-tax campaign — where Sardar Patel made his political debut.

Rowlatt Act and Jallianwala Bagh (1919)

Rowlatt Act (1919)

  • Officially called the Anarchical and Revolutionary Crimes Act, 1919.
  • Named after Sir Sidney Rowlatt — the head of the Rowlatt Committee.
  • Gave the government power to arrest and detain anyone without trial.
  • Severely restricted the freedom of the press and the right of association.
  • Was passed despite unanimous opposition from Indian members of the Imperial Legislative Council.
  • Called the "Black Act" by Indians — it was compared to the infamous "Black Bill" of 1719 in England.
  • Gandhi called for a nationwide strike (hartal) on 6 April 1919.

Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (13 April 1919)

  • On 13 April 1919 (Baisakhi day), a crowd gathered at Jallianwala Bagh, Amritsar to protest against the Rowlatt Act.
  • Many were unaware of the ban on public gatherings.
  • General Reginald Dyer ordered his troops to fire on the unarmed crowd without warning.
  • Over 1,000 people were killed and thousands injured — the exact figure is disputed.
  • The firing continued for about 10 minutes — until the ammunition was exhausted.
  • Martial law was imposed on Punjab.
  • Rabindranath Tagore renounced his knighthood in protest.
  • Gandhi returned his Kaisar-i-Hind medal.
  • The Hunter Commission (1919) was appointed to investigate — but it was a sham.
  • The massacre turned millions of Indians against British rule — a turning point in the freedom struggle.
BPSC Must-Know: The Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (13 April 1919) took place at Amritsar. General Dyer ordered firing on an unarmed crowd. Rabindranath Tagore renounced his knighthood in protest. This event was the immediate trigger for the Non-Cooperation Movement.

Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–1922)

The Non-Cooperation Movement was Gandhi's first nationwide mass movement. It transformed the Congress from a debating society into a mass organisation.

Causes

  • Rowlatt Act (1919) — the "Black Act".
  • Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (1919).
  • Khilafat Movement — the Muslims were upset over the treatment of the Ottoman Caliph after World War I; Gandhi allied with the Khilafat leaders to unite Hindus and Muslims.
  • Disappointment with the Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms (1919) — the Government of India Act 1919 fell far short of Indian expectations.
  • Economic distress — high prices, unemployment, and peasant discontent.

Launch

  • Launched by Gandhi on 1 August 1920.
  • Approved by the INC at the Nagpur Session (December 1920).
  • The movement was based on the principle of "non-violent non-cooperation".

Programme

  • Surrender of titles and honours — Gandhi returned his Kaisar-i-Hind medal.
  • Boycott of government schools, colleges, and courts.
  • Boycott of foreign goods — especially British cloth.
  • Boycott of the elections to the legislative councils.
  • Promotion of swadeshi — charkha (spinning wheel), khadi, and Indian-made goods.
  • Establishment of national educational institutions — e.g., Jamia Millia Islamia (1920), Gujarat Vidyapith, Kashi Vidyapith.
  • Non-payment of taxes in some regions.

Spread and Success

  • The movement spread across India — Bengal, Punjab, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Andhra Pradesh, Tamil Nadu.
  • Students left government schools and colleges in large numbers.
  • Lawyers gave up their practice — including C.R. Das, Motilal Nehru, and others.
  • Women participated in large numbers for the first time.
  • Peasants in Awadh, Bengal and other regions joined the movement.
  • Muslims joined in large numbers due to the Khilafat alliance.
  • The Prince of Wales' visit to India (1921) was boycotted.

Withdrawal — Chauri Chaura (1922)

  • On 5 February 1922, a crowd of protesters at Chauri Chaura (Gorakhpur district, UP) clashed with police.
  • The police fired on the crowd; the crowd retaliated by setting fire to the police station.
  • 22 policemen were killed in the fire.
  • Gandhi was deeply distressed — he felt the movement had become violent and was against his principle of non-violence.
  • Gandhi withdrew the Non-Cooperation Movement on 12 February 1922.
  • This decision was criticised by many leaders — including Motilal Nehru, C.R. Das, and Jawaharlal Nehru.
  • Gandhi was arrested and sentenced to 6 years' imprisonment (released in 1924 due to ill health).
Exam Tip: The Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–1922) was withdrawn after the Chauri Chaura incident (5 February 1922) — where 22 policemen were killed. Gandhi withdrew the movement on 12 February 1922. This is one of the most frequently asked questions.

Swaraj Party and the Simon Commission

Swaraj Party (1923)

  • Founded by C.R. Das and Motilal Nehru after the withdrawal of the Non-Cooperation Movement.
  • Decided to enter the legislative councils and oppose the government from within.
  • Aimed to wreck the councils from within.
  • Won a significant number of seats in the 1923 elections.
  • Withdrew from the councils in 1926 — the strategy had failed.

Simon Commission (1928)

  • The British government appointed the Simon Commission in 1927 (under Sir John Simon) to review the Government of India Act 1919.
  • All seven members were British — no Indian was included.
  • The Congress and other Indian parties decided to boycott the Commission.
  • Wherever the Commission went, it was greeted with the slogan "Simon Go Back".
  • On 30 October 1928, during a protest at Lahore, Lala Lajpat Rai was fatally injured in a lathi charge. He died on 17 November 1928.
  • The death of Lajpat Rai led to the revenge killing of John Saunders (a British police officer) by Bhagat Singh, Rajguru, and Sukhdev.

Nehru Report (1928)

  • In response to the Simon Commission, the Congress asked Motilal Nehru to draft a constitution for India.
  • The Nehru Report (1928) recommended:
    • Dominion status for India (not complete independence).
    • Joint electorates (not separate electorates).
    • Fundamental rights and a federal structure.
  • The report was rejected by Jinnah and the Muslim League — they demanded separate electorates and one-third representation.
  • Jinnah's Fourteen Points (1929) was his response to the Nehru Report.
Remember: The Simon Commission (1928) had all-British members — no Indians. The Congress boycotted it with the slogan "Simon Go Back". Lala Lajpat Rai died after a lathi charge during the protest. The Nehru Report (1928) recommended Dominion Status — Jinnah responded with his Fourteen Points.

Lahore Session (1929) — Purna Swaraj

  • Held in December 1929 at Lahore — presided by Jawaharlal Nehru.
  • For the first time, the Congress declared Purna Swaraj (Complete Independence) as its goal.
  • A resolution was passed to celebrate 26 January 1930 as Independence Day across India.
  • The tricolour flag was hoisted on the banks of the Ravi river.
  • The Congress Working Committee was authorised to launch a programme of Civil Disobedience.

Civil Disobedience Movement (1930–1934)

Dandi March (12 March 1930)

  • Gandhi launched the Civil Disobedience Movement with the famous Dandi March (also called the Salt Satyagraha).
  • Started on 12 March 1930 from Sabarmati Ashram with 78 followers.
  • Gandhi walked 240 miles (approx. 385 km) over 24 days to Dandi on the Gujarat coast.
  • On 6 April 1930, Gandhi broke the salt law by picking up a lump of salt — the symbolic act of defiance.
  • The British had a monopoly on salt production and taxed it heavily — even the poorest Indian had to pay the tax.
  • Gandhi's act inspired millions of Indians to break the salt law across the country.

Programme of the Movement

  • Break the salt law — make salt illegally.
  • Boycott foreign cloth — picketing shops selling foreign goods.
  • Non-payment of taxes.
  • Picketing of liquor shops and opium dens.
  • Resignation from government jobs.
  • Non-cooperation with the government.

Spread and Government Response

  • The movement spread rapidly across India — Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh, Bengal, Bihar, UP, Maharashtra, Gujarat.
  • In Peshawar, the Garhwali soldiers refused to fire on the crowd — a landmark event.
  • In Maharashtra, Sarojini Naidu led the Dharsana Salt Satyagraha.
  • In the North-West Frontier Province, Khan Abdul Ghaffar Khan (the "Frontier Gandhi") led the movement with his Khudai Khidmatgars (Red Shirts).
  • The British arrested Gandhi in May 1930 — along with over 60,000 other leaders and activists.
  • The Congress was declared illegal.
BPSC Must-Know: The Dandi March (12 March 1930 – 6 April 1930) launched the Civil Disobedience Movement. Gandhi walked from Sabarmati Ashram to Dandi — 240 miles in 24 days — and broke the salt law on 6 April 1930. This is one of the most iconic events in the freedom struggle.

Round Table Conferences (1930–1932)

The British government held three Round Table Conferences in London to discuss Indian constitutional reforms.

ConferenceYearKey Facts
First Round Table Conference1930–31Congress boycotted; no agreement
Second Round Table Conference1931Gandhi attended; no agreement — Gandhi–Irwin Pact broke down
Third Round Table Conference1932Congress boycotted; only some leaders attended

Gandhi–Irwin Pact (1931)

  • Signed between Gandhi and Lord Irwin (Viceroy) on 5 March 1931.
  • The Congress agreed to suspend the Civil Disobedience Movement.
  • The government agreed to:
    • Release all political prisoners.
    • Remove the ban on the Congress.
    • Allow Indians to make salt for personal use.
    • Return confiscated property.
  • Gandhi attended the Second Round Table Conference (1931) as the sole representative of the Congress.
  • The conference failed — the British refused to grant Dominion Status or a responsible government.
  • Gandhi returned to India and re-launched the Civil Disobedience Movement.

Communal Award and Poona Pact (1932)

  • Communal Award (1932) — announced by British Prime Minister Ramsay MacDonald; introduced separate electorates for Muslims, Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and the Depressed Classes.
  • Gandhi opposed the separate electorate for the Depressed Classes — he saw it as a threat to Hindu unity.
  • Gandhi undertook a fast unto death at Yerwada Jail (Poona) in September 1932.
  • Poona Pact (24 September 1932) — negotiated between Gandhi and B.R. Ambedkar.
  • The Depressed Classes got reserved seats within the general electorate (instead of separate electorates).
  • The number of reserved seats was increased — from 71 to 147.
  • The Poona Pact saved Hindu unity — and ended Gandhi's fast.
Exam Tip: The Poona Pact (24 September 1932) was signed between Gandhi and B.R. Ambedkar. It replaced separate electorates for the Depressed Classes with reserved seats within the general electorate. This ended Gandhi's fast unto death.

Government of India Act, 1935

  • Based on the recommendations of the Simon Commission and the Round Table Conferences.
  • Provided for a federal structure — with provinces and princely states.
  • Introduced provincial autonomy — provinces were given elected governments.
  • Introduced a dyarchy at the centre — but it was never implemented.
  • Provided for a Federal Court (established in 1937).
  • Extended the franchise — about 10% of the population got the right to vote.
  • Provided for separate electorates for Muslims, Sikhs, Christians, and others.
  • The Congress contested the 1937 elections and won in most provinces — forming governments in 8 out of 11 provinces.

Quit India Movement (1942)

The Quit India Movement was the final and most powerful mass movement of the freedom struggle.

Background

  • World War II (1939–1945) — India was dragged into the war without consultation.
  • The Congress resigned from provincial governments in protest (1939).
  • The British sent the Cripps Mission (1942) — but it failed to satisfy Indian demands.
  • The threat of a Japanese invasion of India increased.
  • Gandhi said the British should quit India and leave Indians to defend themselves.

The Movement

  • Launched on 8 August 1942 at the Gowalia Tank Maidan (Bombay).
  • Gandhi gave the famous slogan: "Do or Die" (Karo ya Maro).
  • The Congress demanded complete independence and the immediate withdrawal of the British.
  • The British responded by arresting Gandhi, Nehru, Patel, Maulana Azad and all top Congress leaders overnight.
  • The movement continued without leaders — it became a spontaneous, leaderless rebellion.
  • The British used brutal force — over 1,000 people were killed, and over 60,000 were arrested.

Spread of the Movement

  • Bihar — one of the strongest centres of the Quit India Movement; leaders like Sri Krishna Sinha and Dr. Rajendra Prasad were arrested.
  • Jayaprakash Narayan — from Bihar — escaped from jail and led the underground movement.
  • Ram Manohar Lohia — ran an underground radio station.
  • Aruna Asaf Ali — hoisted the Congress flag at Gowalia Tank Maidan after the leaders were arrested.
  • Usha Mehta — ran the underground Congress Radio.
  • Ballia (UP) — the revolutionaries set up an independent government.
  • Satara (Maharashtra) — the movement was particularly strong.
  • Talcher (Odisha), Midnapore (Bengal), and other regions saw strong protests.
BPSC Must-Know: The Quit India Movement (8 August 1942) was launched at Gowalia Tank Maidan, Bombay. Gandhi gave the slogan "Do or Die." Bihar was one of the strongest centres of the movement. Jayaprakash Narayan (from Bihar) escaped from jail and led the underground movement. This is a critical BPSC topic.

Indian National Army (INA) and Subhash Chandra Bose

Subhash Chandra Bose (1897–1945)

  • Born in Cuttack, Odisha on 23 January 1897.
  • Passed the Indian Civil Service examination in 1920 but resigned to join the freedom struggle.
  • Called "Netaji" by his followers.
  • Was elected president of the INC at the Haripura Session (1938) and the Tripuri Session (1939).
  • Resigned from the Congress in 1939 and founded the Forward Bloc.
  • Escaped from India in 1941 — travelled through Afghanistan, Central Asia, and reached Germany.
  • In Germany, he formed the Free India Legion with Indian prisoners of war.
  • In 1943, he reached Singapore and took command of the Indian National Army (INA) — originally founded by Rash Behari Bose.
  • Set up the Provisional Government of Free India (Azad Hind) on 21 October 1943 at Singapore.
  • Gave the slogan "Give me blood, and I will give you freedom" and "Dilli Chalo" (March to Delhi).
  • His army fought alongside the Japanese against the British in Burma, Manipur, and Nagaland.
  • Was reportedly killed in a plane crash in Taiwan on 18 August 1945 — the circumstances remain a mystery.

Indian National Army (INA)

  • First formed in 1942 by Rash Behari Bose with Indian POWs captured by the Japanese.
  • Reorganised and expanded by Subhash Chandra Bose in 1943.
  • Had a women's regiment called the Rani of Jhansi Regiment — led by Captain Lakshmi Swaminathan (Lakshmi Sahgal).
  • Set up a Provisional Government — recognised by Japan, Germany, Italy, and other Axis powers.
  • Fought alongside the Japanese in the Battle of Imphal and Kohima (1944) — but was defeated.
  • After Bose's death, the INA surrendered.
  • INA Trials (1945–46) — held at the Red Fort, Delhi. Key accused: Shah Nawaz Khan, Prem Kumar Sahgal, Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon — defended by Bhulabhai Desai, Tej Bahadur Sapru, Jawaharlal Nehru.
  • The trials turned the accused into national heroes — and sparked the Royal Indian Navy Mutiny (1946).
BPSC Must-Know: Subhash Chandra Bose founded the Provisional Government of Free India (Azad Hind) in 1943 at Singapore and reorganised the INA. He gave the slogans "Dilli Chalo" and "Give me blood, and I will give you freedom." He is believed to have died in a plane crash on 18 August 1945.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • Champaran Satyagraha (1917) — Gandhi's first Satyagraha in India, fought in Champaran, Bihar against the Tinkathia system of indigo cultivation.
  • Raj Kumar Shukla — a poor farmer of Champaran who invited Gandhi to Bihar.
  • Dr. Rajendra Prasad (1884–1963) — born in Zeradei (Siwan, Bihar); played a key role in the Champaran Satyagraha and later became the first President of India.
  • Brajkishore Prasad — a leading lawyer of Bihar who supported Gandhi at Champaran.
  • Anugrah Narayan Sinha (1887–1957) — "Bihar Vibhuti"; key leader of the Champaran Satyagraha and the freedom struggle.
  • Sri Krishna Sinha (1887–1961) — "Bihar Kesari"; first Chief Minister of Bihar; arrested during the Quit India Movement.
  • Jayaprakash Narayan (1902–1979) — born in Sitabdiara (Saran, Bihar); joined the freedom struggle; escaped from jail during the Quit India Movement and led the underground movement; called "Lok Nayak".
  • Champaran became the model for Gandhi's future satyagrahas across India.
  • Bihar's role in the Quit India Movement (1942) — Bihar was one of the strongest centres of the movement; leaders like Sri Krishna Sinha and Dr. Rajendra Prasad were arrested.
  • Bihar and the Congress — Bihar became a stronghold of the Congress; Bihar produced several national leaders including Dr. Rajendra Prasad, Sri Krishna Sinha, Anugrah Narayan Sinha, and Jayaprakash Narayan.
  • Bihar and the INA Trials — Bihar's leaders (including Dr. Rajendra Prasad) defended the INA accused and supported their cause.
BPSC Must-Know: Champaran (1917) — Gandhi's first Satyagraha — is the most important Bihar-specific topic in Modern Indian History. Dr. Rajendra Prasad (Siwan, Bihar), Sri Krishna Sinha, Anugrah Narayan Sinha, and Jayaprakash Narayan (Saran, Bihar) were the key leaders of the freedom struggle in Bihar.
