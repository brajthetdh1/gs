Union Budget & Fiscal Policy: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 14 min read · BPSC SSC

The Union Budget is the annual financial statement of the Government of India — presented every year on 1 February by the Finance Minister. It is the single most important economic document of the year — setting out the government's revenue, expenditure, and policy priorities. The Fiscal Policy is the government's use of taxation and spending to influence the economy. Together, they form a crucial part of the Economy section for BPSC and SSC — especially the types of deficits, FRBM Act, Appropriation Bill, and Finance Bill.

What is the Union Budget?

  • The Union Budget is the annual financial statement of the government's estimated receipts and expenditure for the coming financial year.
  • Presented under Article 112 of the Constitution.
  • Presented by the Finance Minister in the Lok Sabha.
  • Financial year: 1 April to 31 March.
  • Presented on 1 February (since 2017) — earlier presented on the last day of February.
  • The budget is discussed and voted on by Parliament.
Exam Tip: The Union Budget is presented under Article 112. Since 2017, it is presented on 1 February — merging the Railway Budget with the Union Budget. This is a frequently asked question in BPSC and SSC.

Key Facts About the Budget

  • First Budget of Independent India: 26 November 1947 — presented by R.K. Shanmukham Chetty.
  • Longest Budget Speech: Nirmala Sitharaman (2020) — over 2.5 hours.
  • Shortest Budget Speech: H.M. Patel (1977) — 800 words.
  • First Woman Finance Minister to present the Budget: Indira Gandhi (1970–71) — as PM she held the Finance portfolio. Nirmala Sitharaman is the first full-time woman Finance Minister to present the Budget.
  • Budget presented most times: Morarji Desai (10 times).
Remember: First Budget of Independent India — R.K. Shanmukham Chetty (1947). First Budget of British India — James Wilson (1860). 1991 Budget — Dr. Manmohan Singh — launched LPG reforms. Railway Budget merged with Union Budget in 2017.

The Union Budget is divided into two main accounts:

Borrowings (internal and external)Creation of assets (roads, bridges)

Recovery of loansDefence capital expenditure

Key Differences

  • Revenue Receipts — do not create a liability or reduce an asset.
  • Capital Receipts — either create a liability (borrowing) or reduce an asset (disinvestment).
  • Revenue Expenditure — does not create an asset or reduce a liability.
  • Capital Expenditure — creates an asset or reduces a liability.

Types of Deficits

Deficits are the gap between the government's receipts and expenditure. They are of four main types:

DeficitFormulaMeaning
Fiscal DeficitTotal Expenditure – Total Receipts (excluding borrowings)Total borrowing requirement of the government
Revenue DeficitRevenue Expenditure – Revenue ReceiptsShortfall in revenue account — government spending more on consumption than it earns
Primary DeficitFiscal Deficit – Interest PaymentsFiscal deficit excluding interest on past debt
Effective Revenue DeficitRevenue Deficit – Grants for creation of capital assetsIntroduced in 2012–13 — measures the "true" revenue deficit

Which Deficit is Most Important?

  • Fiscal Deficit — the most important indicator of the government's borrowing requirement.
  • A high fiscal deficit means the government is borrowing heavily — which can lead to inflation, higher interest rates, and crowding out of private investment.
  • The FRBM Act sets targets for fiscal deficit reduction.

Fiscal Deficit Targets

  • FRBM Act (2003) — original target: fiscal deficit at 3% of GDP, revenue deficit at 0%.
  • Targets were repeatedly revised (due to the 2008 global crisis, COVID-19, etc.).
  • FY 2025–26 budget estimate: Fiscal deficit target is around 4.4% of GDP.
  • The government aims to bring fiscal deficit below 4.5% of GDP by 2025–26.
BPSC Must-Know: Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure – Total Receipts (excluding borrowings). Revenue Deficit = Revenue Expenditure – Revenue Receipts. Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit – Interest Payments. The FRBM Act (2003) originally targeted a fiscal deficit of 3% of GDP.

Budget Process in Parliament

Stages of the Budget

  1. Presentation of the Budget — by the Finance Minister in the Lok Sabha.
  2. General Discussion — both Houses discuss the budget.
  3. Scrutiny by Standing Committees — detailed examination of demands for grants.
  4. Voting on Demands for Grants — only in the Lok Sabha.
  5. Appropriation Bill — authorises withdrawal from the Consolidated Fund.
  6. Finance Bill — contains taxation proposals.
  7. Assent by the President — the final step.

Important Budget Concepts

Vote on Account

  • A grant in advance to enable the government to meet expenditure until the Appropriation Bill is passed.
  • Passed by Parliament — usually for two months.
  • Article 116 — provides for votes on account, votes of credit, and exceptional grants.

Appropriation Bill

  • Authorises the government to withdraw money from the Consolidated Fund of India.
  • Can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha — after the Demands for Grants are voted.
  • No amendment can be made that changes the amount or destination of any grant.
  • The President cannot return the Appropriation Bill — must give assent.

Finance Bill

  • Contains the government's taxation proposals — including changes in tax rates and new taxes.
  • A Money Bill — can be introduced only in the Lok Sabha.
  • The Rajya Sabha can only recommend changes (within 14 days).
  • Must be passed within 75 days of its introduction.

Charged Expenditure

  • Certain expenditures are "charged" on the Consolidated Fund of India — they are not voted on by Parliament.
  • Examples: Salaries of the President, Speaker, Judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts; debt charges; CAG's salary.
  • Charged expenditure can be discussed — but not voted on.
Exam Tip: Charged Expenditure is non-votable but can be discussed. Voted Expenditure requires Parliament's approval. Examples of charged expenditure: President's salary, Judges' salaries, CAG's salary, debt charges.

Fiscal Policy

Fiscal Policy is the government's use of taxation and public expenditure to influence the economy. It is the counterpart of monetary policy — which is managed by the RBI.

Objectives of Fiscal Policy

  • Economic Growth — promote GDP growth through investment.
  • Price Stability — control inflation through fiscal measures.
  • Full Employment — generate jobs through public spending.
  • Social Justice — redistribute income through progressive taxation and welfare schemes.
  • Balanced Regional Development — reduce regional disparities.
  • External Balance — maintain a stable balance of payments.

Instruments of Fiscal Policy

  • Taxation — direct taxes (income tax, corporate tax) and indirect taxes (GST, customs).
  • Public Expenditure — government spending on infrastructure, defence, welfare.
  • Public Debt — borrowing to fund deficits.
  • Deficit Financing — financing deficits by borrowing or printing money.
  • Subsidies — financial support to specific sectors.

FRBM Act, 2003

The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003 was enacted to bring fiscal discipline and reduce the government's fiscal deficit.

Key Objectives

  • Eliminate revenue deficit by 2008.
  • Reduce fiscal deficit to 3% of GDP by 2008.
  • Reduce public debt as a percentage of GDP.
  • Ensure inter-generational equity in fiscal management.
  • Bring transparency in fiscal operations.

Key Provisions

  • The Finance Minister must present the Medium-Term Fiscal Policy Statement along with the Budget.
  • The government cannot borrow from the RBI (except under exceptional circumstances) — ending automatic monetisation of deficit.
  • The RBI can buy government securities only in the secondary market.

N.K. Singh Committee (2017)

  • Reviewed the FRBM Act and recommended a debt-to-GDP ratio as the primary anchor.
  • Recommended debt-to-GDP target of 60% for the Centre and 40% for States — total 100% by 2022–23.
  • Recommended fiscal deficit target of 2.5% of GDP by 2022–23.
  • Recommended setting up a Fiscal Council.
BPSC Must-Know: The FRBM Act, 2003 is the cornerstone of India's fiscal discipline framework. Its main objectives: fiscal deficit at 3% of GDP, revenue deficit at 0%. The N.K. Singh Committee (2017) recommended a debt-to-GDP ratio of 60% for the Centre and 40% for States.

Fiscal Consolidation

Fiscal consolidation refers to policies aimed at reducing fiscal deficit and public debt.

Measures for Fiscal Consolidation

  • Expenditure reforms — reducing subsidies, targeting welfare.
  • Tax reforms — broadening the tax base, improving compliance.
  • GST implementation — unified indirect tax regime (2017).
  • Disinvestment — selling stake in public sector enterprises.
  • Rationalising subsidies — through DBT (Direct Benefit Transfer).
  • Better targeting of welfare schemes — through Aadhaar and DBT.

FRBM Act in Bihar

Bihar has its own Bihar Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act — enacted in line with the central FRBM Act.

Objectives of Bihar FRBM Act

  • Ensure prudent fiscal management in Bihar.
  • Reduce the fiscal deficit and revenue deficit of the state.
  • Reduce the debt-to-GSDP ratio.
  • Improve transparency in fiscal operations.
  • Introduce medium-term fiscal framework.

Bihar's Fiscal Position

  • Bihar's fiscal deficit has remained within the FRBM targets — thanks to prudent fiscal management.
  • Bihar's own tax revenue has grown steadily but remains low compared to the national average.
  • Bihar depends significantly on central transfers — through the Finance Commission and centrally sponsored schemes.
  • The state's debt-to-GSDP ratio is within manageable limits.
  • Bihar's capital expenditure has increased in recent years — especially on infrastructure (roads, bridges, electricity).

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

Bihar Budget

  • Bihar's budget is presented annually in the Bihar Vidhan Sabha by the Finance Minister of Bihar.
  • The budget is presented under Article 202 of the Constitution (Annual Financial Statement of a State).
  • Bihar's budget has been presented every year — with a focus on agriculture, education, health, infrastructure, and welfare schemes.
  • Key focus areas of recent Bihar budgets:
    • Saat Nishchay — the state's flagship development programme.
    • Jeevika — rural livelihoods and women's empowerment.
    • Bihar Student Credit Card Scheme — higher education.
    • Mukhyamantri Kanya Utthan Yojana — girl child empowerment.
    • Bihar Industrial Investment Promotion Policy — industrial development.

Bihar's Fiscal Challenges

  • Low per capita income — limits the state's tax base.
  • Low own tax revenue — the state depends heavily on central transfers.
  • High dependence on central grants — through the Finance Commission.
  • Low credit-deposit ratio — banks in Bihar lend less within the state.
  • High poverty and low industrialisation — limits the tax base.
  • Recurring natural disasters — floods in north Bihar and droughts in south Bihar affect the economy.

Bihar's Fiscal Reforms

  • Bihar FRBM Act — enacted to bring fiscal discipline.
  • Bihar State Finance Commission — constituted every 5 years under Article 243I.
  • Goods and Services Tax (GST) — implemented in Bihar since 1 July 2017.
  • Digitisation of tax administration — e-filing, e-payment, e-assessment.
  • Public Financial Management System (PFMS) — for better tracking of funds.
  • Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) — for efficient welfare delivery.
  • Bihar Industrial Investment Promotion Policy — to attract investment and expand the tax base.
