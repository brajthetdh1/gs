Revolt of 1857: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 15 min read · BPSC SSC

The Revolt of 1857 — also called the First War of Independence, the Sepoy Mutiny, or the Great Revolt — was the first major, widespread uprising against British rule in India. It began on 10 May 1857 at Meerut, spread rapidly across northern and central India, and shook the foundations of the British Empire. Although the revolt was suppressed by 1858, it marked a turning point in Indian history — ending Company rule and beginning direct Crown rule. For BPSC, the revolt's Bihar connection (Kunwar Singh of Jagdishpur) is one of the most important topics.

Name & Nature of the Revolt

Different historians have described the 1857 revolt in different ways:

NameGiven ByInterpretation
Sepoy MutinyBritish historians (e.g. John Seeley)A military mutiny — not a national uprising

Religious WarSome British and Indian writersHindu–Muslim religious grievance against the British
Feudal RevoltSome Marxist historiansReaction of dispossessed princes and feudal lords
Great RevoltModern Indian historiansA combination of military, political, social and economic causes
Exam Tip: V.D. Savarkar — in his book The Indian War of Independence (1909) — was the first to describe the 1857 revolt as the "First War of Independence." British historians, on the other hand, called it a "Sepoy Mutiny."

Causes of the Revolt of 1857

The revolt was the result of a combination of long-term and immediate causes — political, economic, social, religious, and military.

1. Political Causes

  • Doctrine of Lapse — introduced by Lord Dalhousie (1848–1856) — annexed several Indian states: Satara (1848), Jaitpur, Sambhalpur (1849), Baghat (1850), Udaipur (1852), Jhansi (1853), Nagpur (1854).
  • Annexation of Awadh (1856) — on the grounds of misgovernment. Awadh was a loyal ally and its Nawab was very popular — this caused deep resentment.
  • Subsidiary Alliance — introduced by Lord Wellesley (1798) — had reduced Indian rulers to puppets.
  • Abolition of titles and pensions — several Indian rulers lost their titles and pensions under Dalhousie.
  • Policy of "effective control" — the British were determined to bring all Indian states under their direct control.

2. Economic Causes

  • Land revenue systems — Permanent Settlement (1793), Ryotwari and Mahalwari systems led to heavy taxation and peasant indebtedness.
  • De-industrialisation — the destruction of Indian handicrafts and textile industries due to British imports.
  • Drain of wealth — the British took vast amounts of wealth from India to Britain.
  • Discriminatory tariff policy — Indian goods were taxed heavily in Britain, while British goods entered India almost duty-free.
  • Loss of livelihood — artisans, weavers and craftsmen were ruined.

3. Social & Religious Causes

  • Fear of forced conversion to Christianity — the activities of Christian missionaries caused alarm.
  • Social reform laws — the abolition of Sati (1829), the Widow Remarriage Act (1856), and the ban on female infanticide were seen as interference in Hindu religion.
  • Racial discrimination — Indians were treated as inferior in their own country.
  • Western education — the new education system was seen as undermining Indian culture and religion.
  • Discontent among the Muslim community — the British had abolished the Mughal emperor's authority; the last Mughal emperor Bahadur Shah Zafar was a British pensioner.

4. Military Causes

  • Discrimination in the army — Indian soldiers (sepoys) were paid less than British soldiers and could not rise above the rank of Subedar.
  • General Service Enlistment Act (1856) — required Indian soldiers to serve overseas, which was against Hindu religious beliefs.
  • Loss of the "foreign service allowance" — when Indian soldiers were posted outside their home regions.
  • Reduction in the number of Indian soldiers — after the Afghan and Sikh wars, many Indian soldiers were dismissed.
  • The "greased cartridge" issue — the immediate trigger. The new Enfield P-53 rifle required soldiers to bite the cartridge — which was greased with cow and pig fat, offending both Hindus and Muslims.
BPSC Must-Know: The immediate cause of the Revolt of 1857 was the introduction of the Enfield P-53 rifle with cartridges greased with cow and pig fat. The soldiers had to bite these cartridges — which was against both Hindu and Muslim religious beliefs.

5. Immediate Cause — The Spark

  • 29 March 1857 — Mangal Pandey, a sepoy at Barrackpore (near Calcutta), attacked his British officers and was executed on 8 April 1857.
  • 10 May 1857 — the revolt began at Meerut — the sepoys mutinied, marched to Delhi, and proclaimed Bahadur Shah Zafar as their leader.
  • The revolt spread rapidly — Delhi, Kanpur, Lucknow, Jhansi, Bareilly, Arrah (Bihar), and many other places.

Bahadur Shah Zafar (1775–1862)

  • The last Mughal emperor — a symbolic figurehead of the revolt.
  • Was proclaimed Emperor of India by the rebels on 11 May 1857.
  • Was captured by the British in September 1857.
  • Was tried and exiled to Rangoon (Burma) — where he died in 1862.
  • His sons were shot dead by the British — the Mughal dynasty formally ended.

Rani Lakshmibai (1828–1858)

  • The Rani of Jhansi — one of the greatest heroines of the revolt.
  • After her husband's death, the British refused to recognise her adopted son as heir — the Doctrine of Lapse was applied and Jhansi was annexed (1853).
  • She led the revolt in Jhansi with great courage.
  • When Jhansi fell, she escaped to Kalpi and then to Gwalior.
  • She died fighting the British at Gwalior on 18 June 1858.
  • Her famous words: "I will not surrender my Jhansi."
BPSC Must-Know: Kunwar Singh (Veer Kunwar Singh) — the 80-year-old Rajput zamindar of Jagdishpur (Bhojpur district, Bihar) — led the revolt in Bihar. He was one of the most formidable leaders of 1857. After his death, his brother Amar Singh continued the fight. This is the single most important Bihar-specific topic in the Revolt of 1857.

Why the Revolt Failed

  • Lack of unity and coordination — the revolt had no central leadership or common plan.
  • Limited territorial spread — the revolt was concentrated in North India; South India, Bengal, Punjab and Sindh remained largely loyal to the British.
  • Lack of support from important rulers — the Sikhs, the Nizam of Hyderabad, the Scindia of Gwalior, the Gaikwad of Baroda and many Rajput rulers supported the British.
  • Superior British military resources — the British had modern weapons, better organisation, and reinforcements from Britain.
  • Lack of modern weapons — the rebels were armed with outdated muskets and swords.
  • No clear political objective — the rebels wanted to restore the old order, not create a new India.
  • Weak leadership — Bahadur Shah Zafar was old and indecisive.
  • Lack of support from the educated middle class — many educated Indians remained loyal to the British.
  • Absence of a national consciousness — India was not yet a nation; regional and caste loyalties were stronger.

Consequences of the Revolt of 1857

1. End of Company Rule

  • The Government of India Act, 1858 (also called the Act for the Better Government of India) ended the rule of the East India Company.
  • India came under the direct rule of the British Crown.
  • The Secretary of State for India was created — a British cabinet minister responsible for Indian affairs.
  • The Governor-General became the Viceroy — the direct representative of the Crown.
  • Lord Canning became the first Viceroy of India (1858–1862).

2. Queen Victoria's Proclamation (1858)

  • Issued on 1 November 1858 by Queen Victoria.
  • Promised non-interference in Indian religious affairs.
  • Promised to respect the rights of Indian princes.
  • Promised equal treatment of all subjects — Indians and Europeans.
  • Promised to admit Indians to government service (though this remained largely theoretical).
  • Became the foundation of Crown rule in India.

3. Reorganisation of the Army

  • The ratio of British to Indian soldiers was increased — from 1:4 to 1:2 in Bengal and 2:5 in Madras and Bombay.
  • All artillery was placed in British hands.
  • Indian soldiers were not allowed to handle modern weapons.
  • The army was reorganised on the basis of caste, religion and region — to prevent unity.
  • Indian soldiers were recruited from "martial races" — Sikhs, Gurkhas, Pathans, Rajputs.
  • British officers were given better training and pay.

4. Policy Towards Indian Princes

  • The British abandoned the Doctrine of Lapse and adopted a policy of conciliation.
  • The Indian princes were recognised as "friends and allies" of the British Crown.
  • Their right to adopt heirs was recognised.
  • Their territories were guaranteed in return for loyalty.
  • The princes became "breakwaters against the storm" — loyal buffers against future revolts.

5. Changes in Administration

  • The Indian Civil Service was reorganised — recruitment was by open competition, but the examination was held in London (making it difficult for Indians).
  • The age limit for the ICS examination was reduced — from 21 to 19 years (in 1859) — making it even harder for Indians.
  • The Indian Councils Act, 1861 introduced Indians as non-official members in the legislative council — a token representation.
  • Financial decentralisation began — provinces were given more control over their own finances.
  • The British adopted a policy of "divide and rule" — exploiting differences between Hindus and Muslims, castes, and regions.

6. Racial Antagonism

  • The revolt deepened racial bitterness between Indians and the British.
  • The British developed a "siege mentality" — treating Indians with suspicion and contempt.
  • Social interaction between the British and Indians declined sharply.
  • The British built separate civil lines and cantonments — away from Indian localities.
  • Racial discrimination became more pronounced in administration and society.

7. Economic Exploitation Increased

  • The British tightened their control over India's economy.
  • Land revenue remained the main source of income.
  • Railways, telegraph and postal services were expanded — mainly for strategic and commercial purposes.
  • British capital investment in India increased — in railways, plantations, and trade.
  • Famines became more frequent and severe.
Exam Tip: The Government of India Act, 1858 ended Company rule and began direct Crown rule. Lord Canning became the first Viceroy. Queen Victoria's Proclamation (1858) promised non-interference in religion and equal treatment of Indians. These are essential facts for BPSC and SSC.

Significance of the Revolt of 1857

  • It was the first major, widespread challenge to British rule in India.
  • It marked the end of the old order — the Mughal dynasty, the Peshwas, and the Nawabs of Awadh all came to an end.
  • It paved the way for the growth of Indian nationalism — future leaders learned from its failures.
  • It exposed the weaknesses of British rule — the British realised they could not rule India by force alone.
  • It changed the character of British rule — from Company rule to Crown rule, and from annexation to conciliation.
  • It inspired future generations of freedom fighters — Rani Lakshmibai, Kunwar Singh, and Tantia Tope became national heroes.
  • It brought Hindus and Muslims together — for the first time, they fought side by side against a common enemy.
  • It marked the beginning of the end of British rule in India — India became independent 90 years later, in 1947.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • Kunwar Singh (Veer Kunwar Singh) — the 80-year-old Rajput zamindar of Jagdishpur (Bhojpur district, Bihar) — was the leader of the revolt in Bihar.
  • He was a member of the Ujjainiya Parmar Rajput clan — a respected zamindar of the region.
  • He led the revolt in Bihar in July 1857 — after the sepoys from Danapur revolted.
  • He fought several battles against the British — including at Arrah, Azamgarh, and Jagdishpur.
  • He was injured in battle — according to legend, he amputated his own arm to prevent gangrene.
  • He died on 26 April 1858 at Jagdishpur.
  • His brother Amar Singh continued the fight against the British.
  • The Battle of Jagdishpur (1858) — one of the last battles of the revolt in Bihar — was fought by Kunwar Singh and Amar Singh against the British under Captain Le Grand.
  • Other Bihar leaders — Peer Ali Khan (Patna), Maulvi Ahmadullah (Faizabad, but active in Bihar), and Nanda Kumar (Tirhut).
  • Danapur — the cantonment near Patna — was a major centre of the revolt in Bihar.
  • Patna — the British suppressed the revolt in Patna with particular brutality; many rebels were hanged.
  • Bihar's contribution to the Revolt of 1857 is one of the most important topics in BPSC exams.
BPSC Must-Know: Veer Kunwar Singh of Jagdishpur (Bhojpur, Bihar) was the leader of the 1857 revolt in Bihar. He fought the British at Arrah (a key battle) and inspired a generation of Bihari freedom fighters. His brother Amar Singh continued the struggle after his death. This is the single most important Bihar-specific topic in Modern Indian History.
