Money, Inflation & Monetary Policy: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 14 min read · BPSC SSC

Money is the medium of exchange that facilitates all economic transactions. When money loses its purchasing power, we call it inflation. And when the RBI intervenes to control money supply and inflation, that is monetary policy. These three concepts — Money, Inflation, and Monetary Policy — are interconnected and form the heart of macroeconomics. For BPSC and SSC, this is one of the most frequently tested areas — especially measures of money supply (M1, M2, M3, M4), inflation types, and the RBI's inflation-targeting framework.

What is Money?

Money is anything that is generally accepted as a medium of exchange, a measure of value, a store of value, and a standard of deferred payment.

Functions of Money

  • Primary Functions:
    • Medium of Exchange — eliminates the need for barter.
    • Measure of Value — provides a common unit for pricing goods.
  • Secondary Functions:
    • Store of Value — money can be saved for future use.
    • Standard of Deferred Payment — for credit transactions.
    • Transfer of Value — money can be transferred across time and space.

Evolution of Money

  1. Commodity Money — cattle, grains, salt, shells.
  2. Metallic Money — gold, silver, copper coins.
  3. Paper Money — currency notes issued by the central bank.
  4. Credit Money — bank deposits, cheques.
  5. Digital Money — UPI, credit/debit cards, e-wallets, CBDC.
Exam Tip: The evolution of money follows the sequence: Commodity → Metallic → Paper → Credit → Digital. India is among the first countries to introduce a Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC) — the Digital Rupee — launched as a pilot in 2022.

Legal Tender Money

Legal tender money is money that must be accepted by law in settlement of a debt.

Types of Legal Tender

TypeDefinitionExample
Limited Legal TenderCan be used only up to a certain limitCoins (up to ₹1,000 in small denominations)
Unlimited Legal TenderCan be used for any amount without limitCurrency notes (₹2 and above)

Note: In India, coins of 50 paise and below are limited legal tender — they can be used only up to ₹10. All notes of ₹2 and above are unlimited legal tender.

Money Supply in India

Money supply refers to the total stock of money in circulation with the public at any given point of time. It is measured in four components — M1, M2, M3, and M4.

Measures of Money Supply

MeasureCompositionAlso Called
M1Currency with the public + Demand deposits + Other deposits with RBINarrow Money
M2M1 + Post Office Savings Bank deposits (excluding NSC)—
M3M1 + Time deposits with banksBroad Money
M4M3 + Total Post Office deposits (excluding NSC)—

Ranking by Liquidity

  • M1 — Most liquid (most readily usable for transactions).
  • M4 — Least liquid (includes long-term deposits).
  • M3 — Most commonly used measure of money supply in India.

Reserve Money (M0)

  • Also called High-Powered Money or Base Money.
  • M0 = Currency in circulation + Bankers' deposits with RBI + Other deposits with RBI.
  • It is the base on which the entire money supply of the economy is built.
  • Controlled directly by the RBI.
BPSC Must-Know: M1 = Narrow Money; M3 = Broad Money; M0 = Reserve Money / High-Powered Money. The most liquid measure is M1. The most commonly used measure of money supply is M3.

Money Multiplier

  • The money multiplier is the ratio of total money supply to the reserve money.
  • Money Multiplier = M3 ÷ M0 (or broadly, Total Money Supply ÷ Reserve Money).
  • It measures how much the banking system can create money from each unit of reserve money.
  • Higher money multiplier = more money creation by banks.

Credit Creation by Banks

Banks create credit through the process of fractional reserve banking — they keep only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lend the rest. This multiplies the money supply in the economy.

What is Inflation?

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services — reducing the purchasing power of money.

Types of Inflation by Rate

TypeAnnual RateFeature
Creeping InflationUp to 3%Mild; considered good for growth
Walking Inflation3% – 7%Moderate; manageable
Running Inflation10% – 20%Rapid; requires policy action
Galloping / HyperinflationAbove 20%Very severe; currency collapses

Types of Inflation by Cause

  • Demand-Pull Inflation — demand exceeds supply of goods and services.
  • Cost-Push Inflation — rise in input costs (wages, fuel, raw materials).
  • Structural Inflation — due to structural bottlenecks in the economy.
  • Imported Inflation — rise in prices of imported goods.
  • Administered Price Inflation — government raises prices of administered goods (fuel, fertiliser).

Types of Inflation by Coverage

  • Headline Inflation — inflation based on all commodities in the basket (CPI or WPI).
  • Core Inflation — excludes volatile items like food and fuel.
  • Food Inflation — inflation in food prices only.
  • Fuel Inflation — inflation in fuel prices.
Exam Tip: Core Inflation excludes food and fuel — because their prices are highly volatile. The RBI monitors core inflation closely to identify underlying price pressures. Headline inflation includes everything in the consumer basket.

Related Concepts to Inflation

TermMeaning
DeflationSustained fall in the general price level — opposite of inflation.
DisinflationA fall in the rate of inflation — prices still rise, but more slowly.
StagflationHigh inflation + high unemployment + stagnant growth.
ReflationDeliberate policy to raise prices to stimulate the economy.
SkewflationInflation in a particular sector (e.g. food) while overall inflation is low.
HyperinflationExtremely high and accelerating inflation — currency becomes nearly worthless.
AgflationInflation in agricultural commodities.

Measuring Inflation in India

Inflation is measured using price indices — which track the change in prices of a basket of goods over time.

Key Price Indices

IndexReleased ByBase YearCoverage
CPI (Consumer Price Index)NSO (MoSPI)2012Retail inflation
WPI (Wholesale Price Index)Office of Economic Adviser (DPIIT)2011–12Wholesale inflation
IIP (Index of Industrial Production)NSO2011–12Industrial growth

CPI Sub-Indices

  • CPI-Rural — for rural consumers.
  • CPI-Urban — for urban consumers.
  • CPI-Combined — the headline CPI used by the RBI for monetary policy.

CPI — Weightage in Combined Index

  • Food & Beverages — ~45.86% (largest weight).
  • Housing — ~10.07%.
  • Fuel & Light — ~6.84%.
  • Clothing & Footwear — ~6.53%.
  • Miscellaneous — ~28.32%.
Remember: Since 2014, the RBI uses CPI (Combined) — not WPI — as its headline inflation measure for monetary policy. Food & Beverages has the highest weight (~45.86%) in the CPI basket. This is why food inflation drives headline CPI significantly.

Effects of Inflation

Negative Effects

  • Reduces purchasing power — the value of money falls.
  • Hurts fixed-income groups — pensioners, salaried employees.
  • Hurts creditors — they get back money worth less than what they lent.
  • Reduces savings — people spend more on consumption.
  • Worsens inequality — the poor suffer more.
  • Widens trade deficit — exports become costlier.
  • Creates uncertainty — discourages long-term investment.

Positive Effects

  • Benefits debtors — they repay loans with money worth less.
  • Stimulates production — mild inflation can boost growth.
  • Reduces real debt burden of the government.
  • Boosts profits of producers (if inflation is demand-driven).

Monetary Policy of India

Monetary Policy is the policy of the RBI to control money supply, credit and interest rates to achieve macroeconomic objectives like price stability, full employment, and economic growth.

Objectives of Monetary Policy

  • Price Stability — control inflation.
  • Full Employment — promote employment generation.
  • Economic Growth — support GDP growth.
  • Exchange Rate Stability — maintain stability of the rupee.
  • Balanced Balance of Payments — support external sector stability.

Monetary Policy Committee (MPC)

  • Set up in 2016 — through the RBI (Amendment) Act, 2016.
  • Statutory body — created by amending the RBI Act.
  • Composition:
    • 3 members from RBI — Governor (Chairperson), Deputy Governor in charge of monetary policy, and one RBI officer.
    • 3 external members — appointed by the Government of India.
  • Tenure of external members: 4 years.
  • Meetings: At least 4 times a year (in practice, 6 times a year).
  • Decisions: By majority vote; the Governor has a casting vote in case of a tie.

Inflation Targeting Framework

  • Set up under the RBI Act (amended in 2016).
  • Target: 4% CPI inflation.
  • Tolerance band: ±2% — i.e. 2% to 6%.
  • If inflation exceeds 6% for 3 consecutive quarters or falls below 2% for 3 consecutive quarters, the RBI must report to the Government.
  • Target is set by the Government of India in consultation with the RBI — revised every 5 years.

Instruments of Monetary Policy

Quantitative Instruments

InstrumentMeaningEffect of Increase
Bank RateRate at which RBI lends long-term funds to banksReduces money supply
Repo RateRate at which RBI lends short-term funds to banks against securitiesReduces money supply
Reverse Repo RateRate at which RBI borrows from banksAbsorbs liquidity
CRRCash Reserve Ratio — % of deposits kept with RBIReduces money supply
SLRStatutory Liquidity Ratio — % kept in liquid assetsReduces money supply
OMOOpen Market Operations — buying/selling of government securitiesSelling reduces money supply
MSFMarginal Standing Facility — overnight lending by RBIReduces money supply

Qualitative Instruments

  • Margin Requirements — RBI specifies the margin for loans against securities.
  • Consumer Credit Regulation — RBI regulates credit for consumer goods.
  • Moral Suasion — RBI persuades banks informally.
  • Direct Action — RBI can penalise banks for non-compliance.
  • Selective Credit Control — directs credit to specific sectors.
  • Publicity — RBI publishes information to influence banks.
BPSC Must-Know: The Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) has 6 members — 3 from RBI and 3 external members. The inflation target is 4% ± 2% — set under the flexible inflation targeting framework. When inflation rises, the RBI increases the repo rate to reduce money supply — a form of contractionary monetary policy.

Money Supply and Inflation — The Relationship

The classical Quantity Theory of Money states:

MV = PT

  • M — Money supply
  • V — Velocity of circulation of money
  • P — Price level
  • T — Volume of transactions

If money supply (M) increases faster than the volume of transactions (T), and velocity (V) remains stable, then prices (P) rise — causing inflation.

Deflation — Causes and Effects

  • Deflation — sustained fall in the general price level.
  • Causes: fall in demand, excess supply, tight monetary policy, credit contraction.
  • Effects:
    • Reduces business profits.
    • Increases real debt burden.
    • Leads to lower investment and employment.
    • Can spiral into depression.
  • Example: The Great Depression (1929–33) — a classic period of deflation.

Money Market Instruments

  • Call Money — very short-term (1 day) borrowing/lending between banks.
  • Treasury Bills (T-Bills) — short-term government securities (91, 182, 364 days).
  • Commercial Papers (CPs) — short-term unsecured promissory notes issued by companies.
  • Certificates of Deposit (CDs) — issued by banks and financial institutions.
  • Commercial Bills — short-term negotiable instruments used in trade.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

Inflation and Bihar

  • Bihar is a price-sensitive state — because a large share of the population spends most of its income on food.
  • Food inflation affects Bihar more severely than most other states — given the high share of food in household consumption.
  • The state government monitors essential commodity prices closely — through the State Food Commission and the Public Distribution System (PDS).
  • PDS reforms in Bihar — including the Bihar Public Distribution System (BPDS) — help cushion the impact of food inflation on the poor.

Money and Banking in Bihar

  • Bihar's Credit-Deposit (CD) ratio is among the lowest in India — indicating that deposits from Bihar are not fully lent within the state.
  • This affects the flow of credit — and reduces the state's capacity to generate income and employment.
  • Jeevika — the Bihar Rural Livelihoods Project — has been crucial in extending credit to rural women through SHGs.
  • Digital payments — UPI, BHIM, and Aadhaar-enabled payments have grown rapidly in Bihar — improving financial inclusion.
  • Direct Benefit Transfer (DBT) — has improved efficiency in welfare delivery and reduced leakages.

Bihar and Financial Inclusion

  • PMJDY — lakhs of accounts opened in Bihar.
  • MUDRA Yojana — provides loans to small businesses in Bihar.
  • Stand Up India — for SC/ST and women entrepreneurs.
  • Atal Pension Yojana — pension for unorganised sector workers.
  • Bihar State Cooperative Bank and RRBs — extending rural credit.
BPSC Must-Know: Bihar's low Credit-Deposit (CD) ratio is a major economic challenge — it means deposits collected in Bihar are often lent outside the state. Food inflation impacts Bihar more severely than most states due to the high share of food in household consumption. Jeevika, PMJDY, and DBT are key financial inclusion tools in Bihar.
