Biodiversity & Conservation: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 14 min read · BPSC SSC

Biodiversity is the variety and variability of life on Earth — encompassing the diversity of genes, species and ecosystems. India is one of the world's 17 mega-diverse countries, home to nearly 8% of all recorded species on the planet. For BPSC and SSC, this topic is extremely high-yield — especially the levels of biodiversity, biodiversity hotspots in India, IUCN Red List categories, and in-situ and ex-situ conservation methods. Bihar's biodiversity — including the Gangetic Dolphin, Vikramshila Sanctuary, and Valmiki Tiger Reserve — is also frequently asked in BPSC.

What is Biodiversity?

Biodiversity (biological diversity) is the variety of life on Earth at all its levels — from genes to ecosystems. The term was popularised by Edward O. Wilson in 1985.

Levels of Biodiversity

LevelDescriptionExample
Genetic DiversityVariation in genes within a single speciesDifferent varieties of rice (Basmati, IR-8); different breeds of dog
Species DiversityVariety of species in a given areaSpecies richness and evenness in a forest
Ecosystem DiversityVariety of ecosystems in a regionForests, grasslands, wetlands, deserts, marine ecosystems
Exam Tip: The three levels of biodiversity are Genetic, Species, and Ecosystem diversity. The term "biodiversity" was popularised by Edward O. Wilson (1985). India is one of the world's 17 mega-diverse countries — remember this for BPSC and SSC.

India's Biodiversity

India — A Mega-Diverse Country

  • India is one of the 17 mega-diverse countries in the world — recognised for its high species richness and endemism.
  • India has about 8% of all recorded species — over 1.8 lakh species of plants and animals.
  • India has 4 of the world's 36 biodiversity hotspots — the highest for any single country.
  • India has 10 biogeographic zones and 25 biogeographic provinces.

BPSC Must-Know: India has 4 of the world's 36 biodiversity hotspots — the Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Western Ghats & Sri Lanka, and Sundaland (Nicobar Islands). India is a mega-diverse country with nearly 8% of global species. These facts are almost guaranteed in every BPSC and SSC paper.

Biodiversity Hotspots

A biodiversity hotspot is a region with a high level of endemic species (species found nowhere else) that is also under significant threat of habitat loss. The concept was introduced by Norman Myers in 1988.

Criteria for a Hotspot

  • Must have at least 1,500 endemic vascular plant species.
  • Must have lost at least 70% of its original primary vegetation.

Global Biodiversity Hotspots

  • 36 biodiversity hotspots globally.
  • Most of these are in tropical regions.
  • They cover only ~2.5% of Earth's land surface — but support over 50% of the world's endemic plant species.
  • India has the highest number (4) of hotspots for any single country — tied with a few others.

IUCN Red List

The IUCN Red List of Threatened Species — published by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) — is the world's most comprehensive inventory of the conservation status of species.

Categories of the IUCN Red List

Endangered (EN)Very high risk of extinction in the wild

Near Threatened (NT)Close to qualifying as threatened

BPSC Must-Know: The IUCN Red List classifies species into 9 categories. The Great Indian Bustard, Gharial, and Indian Vulture are Critically Endangered. The Bengal Tiger, Asiatic Lion, Asiatic Elephant, and Gangetic Dolphin are Endangered. The Snow Leopard is Vulnerable. These classifications are frequently asked.

Causes of Biodiversity Loss

The main causes of biodiversity loss are collectively called the "Evil Quartet" — proposed by Edward O. Wilson.

CauseDescription
Habitat Loss & FragmentationThe most important cause — deforestation, urbanisation, agriculture expansion
Over-exploitationOver-hunting, over-fishing, over-collection of plants and animals
Alien (Invasive) SpeciesIntroduction of non-native species that outcompete natives — e.g. Water Hyacinth, Lantana, Parthenium
Co-extinctionExtinction of one species leads to extinction of dependent species

Other Causes

  • Climate Change — global warming alters habitats.
  • Pollution — air, water, soil pollution.
  • Over-population — increasing pressure on natural resources.
  • Poaching and Illegal Wildlife Trade.
  • Developmental projects — dams, roads, mining.
  • Natural disasters — floods, forest fires.
Exam Tip: The Evil Quartet — proposed by Edward O. Wilson — consists of Habitat Loss, Over-exploitation, Alien Species, and Co-extinction. Habitat loss is the single most important cause of biodiversity loss globally and in India. This is a very frequent question.

Biodiversity Conservation

Biodiversity conservation is the protection, preservation, management and restoration of biodiversity.

Reasons for Conservation

  • Narrowly Utilitarian — direct economic benefits (food, medicine, timber, fibre).
  • Broadly Utilitarian — ecosystem services (climate regulation, pollination, water purification).
  • Ethical — every species has intrinsic value; moral duty to protect them.
  • Aesthetic — beauty of nature; recreation and tourism.

In-situ Conservation

In-situ conservation means conservation of species in their natural habitat.

MethodDescription
National ParksStrictly protected areas — no human activity
Wildlife SanctuariesProtected areas — limited human activity allowed
Biosphere ReservesMultipurpose protected areas — conservation + development
Tiger ReservesSpecial reserves under Project Tiger
Elephant ReservesUnder Project Elephant
Sacred GrovesCommunity-protected forests — traditional conservation
Biosphere ReservesDesignated by UNESCO/MAB Programme
Ramsar SitesWetlands of international importance

Ex-situ Conservation

Ex-situ conservation means conservation of species outside their natural habitat.

MethodDescription
Zoological ParksAnimals kept in captivity for breeding and display
Botanical GardensPlants grown for conservation, research and education
Seed BanksSeeds stored for future use — e.g. Svalbard Global Seed Vault
Gene BanksGenetic material stored for research — e.g. NBPGR (New Delhi)
CryopreservationPreservation at very low temperatures (-196°C)
Tissue CultureGrowing plants from cells in laboratories
Captive BreedingBreeding of endangered species in captivity

In-situ vs Ex-situ Conservation

BasisIn-situEx-situ
LocationNatural habitatOutside natural habitat
CostLowerHigher
Species protectionWhole ecosystem protectedTarget species protected
ExamplesNational Parks, Sanctuaries, Biosphere ReservesZoos, Botanical Gardens, Seed Banks
BPSC Must-Know: In-situ conservation means conservation in the natural habitat — examples: National Parks, Sanctuaries, Biosphere Reserves, Sacred Groves. Ex-situ conservation means conservation outside the natural habitat — examples: Zoos, Botanical Gardens, Seed Banks, Cryopreservation. This distinction is almost always asked.

Project Tiger & Project Elephant

Project Tiger (1973)

  • Launched in 1973 — by the Government of India.
  • Aimed at ensuring a viable population of tigers in India.
  • Administered by the National Tiger Conservation Authority (NTCA) — established in 2006.
  • Currently, India has 54 tiger reserves.
  • India has over 70% of the world's wild tigers.
  • Project Tiger was launched in the Jim Corbett National Park (Uttarakhand) — the first tiger reserve of India.
  • Bihar's only tiger reserve: Valmiki Tiger Reserve (West Champaran).

Project Elephant (1992)

  • Launched in 1992 — to protect elephants and their habitats.
  • Aimed at ensuring the long-term survival of elephants in their natural habitats.
  • Currently, India has 33 elephant reserves.
  • India has the largest population of wild Asian elephants.

Other Conservation Projects

  • Project Crocodile (1975) — for crocodile conservation.
  • Project Snow Leopard (2009) — for snow leopard conservation.
  • Project Gangetic Dolphin — for the National Aquatic Animal.
  • Project Great Indian Bustard — for the Critically Endangered bird.
  • Project Cheetah (2022) — reintroduction of cheetahs in India.

Key Institutions

  • National Biodiversity Authority (NBA) — established in 2003 under the Biological Diversity Act, 2002; headquartered in Chennai.
  • State Biodiversity Boards (SBBs) — in each state.
  • Biodiversity Management Committees (BMCs) — at the local level.
  • National Tiger Conservation Authority (NTCA) — 2006.
  • Central Zoo Authority (CZA) — 1992.
  • Wildlife Crime Control Bureau (WCCB) — 2007.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

Bihar's Biodiversity

  • Bihar's forest cover is around 7.8% of the state's geographical area — well below the national target of 33%.
  • Bihar has one National Park — Valmiki National Park (West Champaran).
  • Bihar has one Tiger Reserve — Valmiki Tiger Reserve (West Champaran).
  • Bihar's only Ramsar site is Kanwar Lake (Kabartal) in Begusarai district.
  • Bihar has 12 wildlife sanctuaries — including Bhimbandh, Kaimur, Rajgir, Gautam Buddha, Nagi Dam, Nakti Dam, Pant, Udaipur, Vikramshila Gangetic Dolphin Sanctuary, Kusheshwar Asthan, Gogabeel, and Barela Salim Ali Zubba Saheni.

Bihar's Forests

  • Bihar's forests are mainly in the southern districts — Kaimur, Rohtas, Gaya, Nawada, Jamui, Munger, Banka.
  • The Terai region along the Nepal border — moist deciduous forests — including Valmiki Tiger Reserve.
  • Kaimur Hills — dry deciduous forests — Bihar's largest forest area.
  • Rajgir Hills — dry deciduous forests, part of the historical Rajgir region.
  • Bihar's forest products include Sal, Teak, Khair, Bamboo, Mahua, Tendu.
  • Bihar's forests support a wide variety of medicinal plants — including Ashwagandha, Sarpagandha, and others.

Bihar's Wetlands

  • Kanwar Lake (Kabartal) — Begusarai — Bihar's only Ramsar site; important bird habitat.
  • Gogabeel — Bhagalpur and Katihar — oxbow lake; important wetland.
  • Kusheshwar Asthan — Darbhanga — a bird sanctuary and wetland.
  • Udaipur Lake — West Champaran — wildlife sanctuary.
  • Nagi and Nakti Dam — Jamui — bird sanctuaries.
  • Vikramshila Gangetic Dolphin Sanctuary — Bhagalpur — a 60 km stretch of the Ganga; the only sanctuary for the Gangetic Dolphin.

Bihar's Conservation Initiatives

  • Valmiki Tiger Reserve — the flagship conservation project in Bihar.
  • Vikramshila Gangetic Dolphin Sanctuary — for the National Aquatic Animal.
  • Bihar State Biodiversity Board (BSBB) — established under the Biological Diversity Act, 2002.
  • Bihar State Pollution Control Board (BSPCB) — for pollution control.
  • Bihar Forest Development Corporation — for forest development.
  • Jal-Jeevan-Hariyali — flagship state programme for water conservation and tree plantation.
  • Social Forestry Programme — for afforestation and community participation.
  • Mission Harit Bihar — for increasing tree cover in the state.
  • Ganga Action Plan / Namami Gange — for cleaning the Ganga, including in Bihar.
BPSC Must-Know: Bihar has 1 National Park (Valmiki), 1 Tiger Reserve (Valmiki), 12 Wildlife Sanctuaries, and 1 Ramsar Site (Kanwar Lake). The Gangetic Dolphin is the National Aquatic Animal (declared 2009) — its only sanctuary is Vikramshila (Bhagalpur). Bihar's forest cover is only ~7.8%. Jal-Jeevan-Hariyali and Mission Harit Bihar are the state's flagship environmental programmes. These are among the most frequently asked BPSC Environment questions.
