External Sector — Trade, BoP & FDI: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 14 min read · BPSC SSC

The External Sector of the Indian economy deals with India's economic interactions with the rest of the world — trade in goods and services, capital flows, foreign exchange, and remittances. Understanding the external sector is essential for BPSC and SSC — especially the Balance of Trade vs Balance of Payments distinction, current and capital account components, foreign exchange reserves, FDI vs FPI, and exchange rate systems. This is a high-yield topic and also links to current affairs — trade agreements, WTO, and India's post-COVID export performance.

What is the External Sector?

The external sector refers to all economic transactions of a country with the rest of the world. It includes:

  • Trade in goods and services.
  • Capital flows — FDI, FPI, external commercial borrowings.
  • Foreign exchange reserves.
  • Remittances from Indians abroad.
  • Exchange rate of the rupee.

Why the External Sector Matters

  • Determines the balance of payments position.
  • Affects the exchange rate and inflation.
  • Influences foreign exchange reserves.
  • Impacts economic growth and employment.
  • Determines international competitiveness of Indian industry.
  • Affects the country's ability to fund its deficits.

International Trade of India

International trade is the exchange of goods and services between countries — comprising exports and imports.

India's Major Exports

  • Petroleum products — refined petroleum.
  • Gems and jewellery — diamonds, gold jewellery.
  • Engineering goods — machinery, transport equipment.
  • Pharmaceuticals — medicines, vaccines.
  • Chemicals and petrochemicals.
  • Textiles and garments.
  • Agricultural products — rice, spices, tea, coffee.
  • Software and IT services — the largest services export.

India's Major Imports

  • Crude petroleum — the largest import by value.
  • Gold and precious stones.
  • Electronic goods — semiconductors, mobile phones.
  • Machinery.
  • Chemicals and fertilisers.
  • Coal and coke.
  • Edible oils.

India's Major Trading Partners

Largest Trading Partner (Overall)USA (in recent years); China (traditionally)

BPSC Must-Know: Crude petroleum is India's largest import item — India imports over 80% of its crude oil requirement. Petroleum products and software services are among India's largest export earners. India's largest trading partner is the USA in recent years — but China remains the largest source of imports.

Balance of Trade (BoT)

  • Balance of Trade = Exports of goods – Imports of goods.
  • It covers only visible goods (merchandise trade).
  • Trade Surplus — Exports > Imports.
  • Trade Deficit — Imports > Exports.
  • India has a persistent trade deficit — mainly due to heavy crude oil and gold imports.
  • India's trade deficit is one of the largest in the world.

Balance of Trade vs Balance of Payments

BasisBalance of Trade (BoT)Balance of Payments (BoP)
ScopeOnly visible goodsAll external transactions — goods, services, capital
ComponentsExports and imports of goodsCurrent account + Capital account + Financial account
NatureNarrow conceptBroad concept
SignificanceIndicates trade performanceIndicates overall external balance
India's PositionTrade deficitBroadly balanced (due to services and remittances)
Exam Tip: Balance of Trade covers only visible goods. Balance of Payments covers all external transactions — goods, services, and capital. India has a trade deficit but a broadly balanced BoP — thanks to services exports and remittances.

Balance of Payments (BoP)

The Balance of Payments is a systematic record of all economic transactions between a country and the rest of the world during a given period — usually one year.

Components of BoP

  • Current Account — trade in goods and services, income, and transfers.
  • Capital Account — capital transfers and acquisition/disposal of non-financial assets.
  • Financial Account — FDI, FPI, external commercial borrowings, banking capital.
  • Errors and Omissions — balancing item.
  • Change in Reserves — adjustment through foreign exchange reserves.

Current Account — Components

ComponentMeaning
Merchandise TradeExports and imports of goods
Services TradeExports and imports of services (software, tourism, banking, insurance)
Primary IncomeIncome from investments — interest, dividends, profits
Secondary Income (Transfers)Remittances, grants, gifts

India's Current Account Deficit (CAD)

  • India has a current account deficit — because imports of goods exceed exports.
  • However, this deficit is partially offset by:
    • Services surplus — especially software exports.
    • Remittances — India is the world's largest recipient of remittances (~$100+ billion annually).
  • India's CAD is usually between 1–3% of GDP — with occasional spikes.
  • A high CAD puts pressure on the rupee and foreign exchange reserves.

Foreign Exchange Reserves

Foreign Exchange Reserves are the assets held by the RBI in foreign currencies — used to manage the exchange rate, meet external obligations, and maintain confidence in the economy.

Components of Forex Reserves

  • Foreign Currency Assets (FCA) — the largest component (USD, Euro, Pound, Yen).
  • Gold — held by the RBI.
  • Special Drawing Rights (SDRs) — from the IMF.
  • Reserve Tranche Position (RTP) — with the IMF.

Key Facts About Forex Reserves

  • India has one of the largest forex reserves in the world — fourth largest, after China, Japan and Switzerland.
  • Total reserves peaked at over $700 billion in 2024.
  • Managed by the RBI.
  • Used for import cover, exchange rate stability, and servicing external debt.
  • India's import cover is typically around 10–12 months.
Remember: India's foreign exchange reserves include Foreign Currency Assets, Gold, SDRs, and Reserve Tranche Position. India is the fourth-largest holder of forex reserves in the world. The reserves are managed by the RBI.

Exchange Rate Systems

The exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another.

Types of Exchange Rate Systems

SystemMeaning
Fixed Exchange RateGovernment fixes the exchange rate — RBI intervenes to maintain it
Floating Exchange RateMarket determines the exchange rate
Managed FloatMarket determines rate, but the Central Bank intervenes to prevent excessive volatility

India's system: India follows a managed float (dirty float) system since 1993 — the rupee is largely market-determined, but the RBI intervenes when needed.

Currency Appreciation vs Depreciation

TermMeaningEffect
AppreciationRupee value risesExports costlier; imports cheaper
DepreciationRupee value fallsExports cheaper; imports costlier
DevaluationGovernment deliberately lowers value under fixed systemExports cheaper
RevaluationGovernment deliberately raises value under fixed systemImports cheaper

Devaluation of the Rupee in India

  • India devalued the rupee in 1949, 1966, and 1991.
  • The 1991 devaluation was a major part of the LPG reforms — to correct the BoP crisis.
  • Since 1993, India has followed a market-determined (managed float) system.

Foreign Direct Investment (FDI)

  • FDI — investment in productive assets (factories, offices, equity in companies) in another country.
  • Long-term — reflects a lasting interest in the target country.
  • Comes with technology, management skills, and market access.
  • FDI routes in India:
    • Automatic Route — no prior approval needed.
    • Government Route — requires approval from the concerned ministry.
  • India is one of the largest recipients of FDI among emerging economies.
  • Top FDI source countries: Mauritius, Singapore, USA, Netherlands, Japan.
  • Top sectors: Services, computer software and hardware, telecommunications, trading, construction.

FDI vs FPI

BasisFDIFPI
Full FormForeign Direct InvestmentForeign Portfolio Investment
NatureLong-termShort-term
InvestmentIn productive assets, equity in companiesIn stocks, bonds, mutual funds
Management ControlYes — investor has management controlNo — investor is a passive investor

BPSC Must-Know: FDI is long-term investment in productive assets — with management control. FPI is short-term investment in stocks and bonds — no management control. FDI is considered more stable and beneficial than FPI for the economy.

Foreign Trade Policy (FTP)

The Foreign Trade Policy (earlier called EXIM Policy) is announced by the Ministry of Commerce and Industry — covering a period of 5 years.

Objectives of FTP

  • To provide a framework for increasing exports.
  • To improve the trade balance.
  • To reduce transaction costs and improve ease of doing business.
  • To promote technology upgradation and Make in India.
  • To promote manufacturing and services exports.
  • To support MSMEs and labour-intensive industries.

Key Schemes Under FTP

Special Economic Zones (SEZs)

  • SEZ — designated areas with special economic regulations that differ from the rest of the country.
  • Aimed at boosting exports, attracting investment, and generating employment.
  • Established under the SEZ Act, 2005.
  • Benefits for units in SEZs:
    • Tax holidays — income tax exemption for a specified period.
    • Duty-free imports of inputs and capital goods.
    • Simplified procedures for approvals.
    • Single window clearance.
  • India's first SEZ — Kandla (Gujarat) — 1965 — earlier called Export Processing Zone (EPZ).
  • Major SEZs: SEEPZ (Mumbai), NSEZ (Noida), Madras SEZ, Falta SEZ (West Bengal).

World Trade Organization (WTO)

  • Established on 1 January 1995 — replacing GATT (General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade).
  • Headquarters: Geneva, Switzerland.
  • 164 member countries — India is a founding member.
  • Objective: liberalise international trade and resolve trade disputes.
  • Principles:
    • Most Favoured Nation (MFN) — non-discrimination between trading partners.
    • National Treatment — equal treatment to domestic and foreign goods.
    • Reciprocity — mutual reduction of trade barriers.
    • Transparency — clear trade rules.
  • Key WTO Agreements: Agriculture (AoA), TRIPS (Intellectual Property), TRIMS (Investment Measures), Services (GATS).
  • India's concerns at WTO:
    • Food security — public stockholding for food security.
    • Agricultural subsidies — reduction of developed country subsidies.
    • Patent issues — access to medicines.
    • Services — movement of professionals.
Exam Tip: The WTO was established on 1 January 1995, replacing GATT. It has 164 members and is headquartered in Geneva. India's key concerns at the WTO are food security (public stockholding), agricultural subsidies, and access to medicines.

Remittances to India

  • India is the world's largest recipient of remittances — receiving over $100 billion annually.
  • Top sources: USA, UAE, Saudi Arabia, UK, Singapore, Kuwait, Qatar.
  • Remittances are a critical source of foreign exchange — helping finance the current account deficit.
  • Indian states with the highest remittance inflows: Kerala, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, Karnataka, Uttar Pradesh, Bihar.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

Bihar's External Sector

  • Bihar is a landlocked state — with no coastline — which limits its direct access to international trade.
  • Bihar's exports are modest — the state's share in India's total exports is small.
  • Key exports from Bihar:
    • Shahi Litchi (Muzaffarpur) — GI-tagged; exported to the Middle East, Europe.
    • Makhana (Fox Nut) — Darbhanga, Madhubani; exported to USA, Europe, Middle East.
    • Rice (especially aromatic varieties).
    • Maize.
    • Jute products.
    • Handloom and handicrafts.
    • Leather products.
    • Vegetables and fruits.
  • Bihar's exports are largely agricultural and labour-intensive — with a small share of manufactured goods.
  • The state has significant potential for food processing exports — especially litchi, makhana, and maize.

Remittances to Bihar

  • Bihar is one of the largest recipients of remittances among Indian states — both from within India and from abroad.
  • Large-scale out-migration from Bihar — to other states (Delhi, Mumbai, Punjab, Haryana) and abroad (Middle East, USA, UK).
  • Remittances are a critical source of household income for lakhs of families in Bihar.
  • Remittances help finance education, housing, healthcare, and consumption.
  • The state's per capita income is boosted significantly by remittances.

Bihar's Trade Infrastructure

  • Inland Container Depot (ICD) at Patna — facilitates containerised movement of goods.
  • Inland Waterways (National Waterway-1 on the Ganga) — passes through Bihar; provides a riverine trade route.
  • Bihar Industrial Area Development Authority (BIADA) — manages industrial parks.
  • Bihar Agri-Export Policy — to promote agricultural exports.
  • Bihar Startup Policy — promotes entrepreneurship and trade.
  • Bihar Industrial Investment Promotion Policy — to attract investment.
  • Dedicated Freight Corridor (Eastern DFC) — passes through Bihar — improves freight movement.
  • Amritsar-Kolkata Industrial Corridor — passes through Bihar — boosts industrial and export activity.

Bihar's External Sector Challenges

  • Landlocked geography — high logistics cost.
  • Low industrial base — limits manufactured exports.
  • Poor infrastructure — roads, warehouses, cold chains.
  • Low credit-deposit ratio — limits investment.
  • Small landholdings — limits agricultural surplus for export.
  • Low skill levels — limits high-value exports.
  • Political and administrative challenges.
  • High out-migration — skilled workers leave the state.

Bihar's External Sector Opportunities

  • Food processing — litchi, makhana, maize, sugarcane, fruits, vegetables.
  • Agri-exports — especially to Middle East, Europe, USA.
  • Handloom and handicrafts — Bhagalpur silk (GI tag), Madhubani paintings (GI tag).
  • Inland waterways — National Waterway-1 on the Ganga.
  • Leather industry — with potential for exports.
  • Tourism — Buddhist circuit — attracts international visitors.
  • IT and services — growing sector in Patna and other cities.

Bihar — GI Tagged Products

  • Shahi Litchi (Muzaffarpur) — GI tag.
  • Makhana — GI tag (Mithila Makhana).
  • Madhubani Paintings — GI tag.
  • Bhagalpur Silk — GI tag (Tussar silk).
  • Jardalu Mango (Bhagalpur) — GI tag.
  • Magahi Paan — GI tag.
  • Khatwa Art — traditional art form.
BPSC Must-Know: Bihar's key exports include Shahi Litchi (Muzaffarpur), Makhana (Darbhanga), rice, maize, jute, and handloom products. Remittances from migrants are a critical source of income for Bihar. The state's external sector is constrained by its landlocked geography and low industrial base — but has significant agri-export potential. National Waterway-1 on the Ganga and the Eastern Dedicated Freight Corridor pass through Bihar. These are frequently asked BPSC topics.
