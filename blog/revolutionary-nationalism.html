Revolutionary Nationalism: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 15 min read · BPSC SSC

While the Congress pursued constitutional methods and Gandhi led mass movements, a parallel stream of nationalism emerged — Revolutionary Nationalism. The revolutionaries believed that British rule could not be overthrown by petitions and peaceful protests — it required armed struggle, secret societies, and personal sacrifice. From the early secret societies of Bengal and Maharashtra to the heroic martyrdom of Bhagat Singh, Rajguru and Sukhdev, revolutionary nationalism played a vital role in keeping the flame of freedom alive. For BPSC and SSC, the revolutionaries, their organisations, and their actions are essential topics.

What Was Revolutionary Nationalism?

  • Believed in the overthrow of British rule by force.
  • Used bombs, assassinations, armed robberies to spread the movement and fund it.
  • Organised secret societies (samitis) and youth groups.
  • Inspired by European revolutionary movements (Irish Republican Brotherhood, Russian Nihilists, Italian Carbonari).
  • Received support from Indian communities abroad — USA, UK, Europe, Southeast Asia.
  • Differed from the Congress's constitutional methods.
  • Attracted young, educated Indians who were impatient with the slow pace of reform.
Exam Tip: Revolutionary nationalism developed in two distinct phases: (1) the early phase (1897–1917) — focused on Bengal, Maharashtra and Punjab; (2) the later phase (1920s–1930s) — marked by the formation of the HSRA and the martyrdom of Bhagat Singh. Understanding these phases is essential.

Phase 1 — Early Revolutionary Nationalism (1897–1917)

Maharashtra — The Chapekar Brothers

  • Damodar Chapekar and his brothers assassinated W.C. Rand — the Plague Commissioner of Poona — in 1897.
  • They were inspired by Tilak's writings and the anti-plague measures that were seen as humiliating to Indians.
  • The Chapekar brothers were caught and hanged — the first martyrs of the revolutionary movement.

Maharashtra — Abhinav Bharat (1904)

  • Founded by V.D. Savarkar (Vinayak Damodar Savarkar) in 1904 at Nasik.
  • Was a secret society modelled on Giuseppe Mazzini's Young Italy.
  • Spread revolutionary ideas among youth in Maharashtra and beyond.
  • Savarkar wrote The Indian War of Independence (1909) — a historical account of the 1857 Revolt that inspired revolutionaries.
  • The book was banned by the British but was widely circulated.
  • Savarkar was arrested in 1910 and sentenced to 50 years' transportation to the Cellular Jail in the Andamans.
  • He was later released in 1924 and joined the Hindu Mahasabha.

Bengal — Anushilan Samiti (1902)

  • Founded by Barindra Kumar Ghosh and Bhupendranath Datta (brother of Swami Vivekananda) in 1902.
  • Was the most important revolutionary organisation in Bengal — with branches across the province.
  • Received support from Aurobindo Ghosh, who provided ideological direction.
  • The Dhaka Anushilan Samiti was another important branch.
  • Advocated armed struggle and physical training for young men.
  • The Alipore Bomb Case (1908) — key members, including Aurobindo Ghosh, were tried. Aurobindo was acquitted after a famous defense by Chittaranjan Das.
  • Barindra Kumar Ghosh and others were convicted.

Bengal — Muzaffarpur Bombing (1908)

  • Khudiram Bose and Prafulla Chaki were sent to Muzaffarpur to assassinate Douglas Kingsford — a British judge notorious for his harsh sentences.
  • On 30 April 1908, they threw a bomb at a carriage — but mistakenly killed two British women instead of Kingsford.
  • Prafulla Chaki shot himself to avoid capture.
  • Khudiram Bose was arrested and hanged on 11 August 1908 — at the age of just 18.
  • He became a martyr and a symbol of Bengali youth's revolutionary spirit.
Exam Tip: Khudiram Bose was executed at the age of 18 — making him one of the youngest martyrs of the freedom struggle. The Anushilan Samiti (1902) was the most important revolutionary organisation in Bengal. V.D. Savarkar founded Abhinav Bharat (1904) and wrote The Indian War of Independence.

Punjab — The Ghadar Party (1913)

  • Founded in San Francisco, USA in 1913 — by Lala Har Dayal and Sohan Singh Bhakna.
  • Was a revolutionary organisation of Indian immigrants in North America — mostly Sikhs from Punjab.
  • Started the newspaper Ghadar (meaning "revolt") in Urdu and Punjabi — circulated widely among Indian communities abroad.
  • Aimed to overthrow British rule in India through armed revolution.
  • The Ghadar Conspiracy (1915) — an attempt to spark a mutiny in the Indian Army — failed.
  • Many Ghadarites were arrested and executed.
  • The Ghadar Party continued to support the Indian freedom struggle through the 20th century.

Bengal — The Rodda Company Arms Heist (1914)

  • On 26 August 1914, Bengali revolutionaries raided the Rodda Company arms depot at Calcutta.
  • Led by Jatindranath Mukherjee (Bagha Jatin).
  • 50 Mauser pistols and 46,000 rounds of ammunition were seized.
  • This was a major success — but the ammunition was later distributed and most revolutionaries were caught or killed.

Bengal — Bagha Jatin (1915)

  • Jatindranath Mukherjee — known as Bagha Jatin — was one of Bengal's greatest revolutionaries.
  • Organised the Jugantar group — the revolutionary wing of the Anushilan Samiti.
  • Planned a Pan-Indian armed uprising with German support during World War I.
  • Was killed in a battle with British forces on 10 September 1915 near Balasore (Odisha).

Phase 2 — Later Revolutionary Nationalism (1920s–1930s)

After World War I and the Jallianwala Bagh Massacre, a new generation of revolutionaries emerged — inspired by socialist ideas, the Russian Revolution, and the sacrifices of their predecessors.

Hindustan Republican Association (HRA, 1924)

  • Founded by Ram Prasad Bismil, Jogesh Chandra Chatterjee, Sachindranath Sanyal and others.
  • Based in United Provinces (UP), with branches in other parts of India.
  • Aimed to establish a Federal Republic of the United States of India.
  • Published the manifesto The Revolutionary in 1925.
  • Focused on armed struggle, revolutionary propaganda, and organising workers and peasants.

Kakori Conspiracy (1925)

  • On 9 August 1925, HRA revolutionaries looted a train near Kakori (near Lucknow).
  • They stopped the No. 8 Down Train and looted the British treasury's cash chest.
  • The loot was intended to fund revolutionary activities.
  • Key accused: Ram Prasad Bismil, Ashfaqullah Khan, Roshan Singh, Rajendra Lahiri.
  • All four were hanged in 1927.
  • The Kakori Conspiracy trials brought the HRA to national prominence.
  • Ashfaqullah Khan — a Muslim revolutionary — became a symbol of Hindu–Muslim unity in the freedom struggle.
BPSC Must-Know: The Kakori Conspiracy (9 August 1925) was organised by the Hindustan Republican Association (HRA). Key accused: Ram Prasad Bismil, Ashfaqullah Khan, Roshan Singh, Rajendra Lahiri — all hanged in 1927. This was a major event in the revolutionary movement.

Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA, 1928)

  • The HRA was reorganised as the Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA) in 1928.
  • Key leaders: Bhagat Singh, Chandrashekhar Azad, Sukhdev, Rajguru, Batukeshwar Dutt.
  • Added socialism to its goal — the aim was not just independence but a socialist republic.
  • Believed in the overthrow of British rule through armed revolution.
  • Bhagat Singh and his comrades became the most famous revolutionaries in India.
  • The HSRA was involved in several high-profile actions in the late 1920s.

Lala Lajpat Rai's Death and Saunders' Killing (1928)

  • On 30 October 1928, Lala Lajpat Rai was fatally injured in a lathi charge during the protest against the Simon Commission at Lahore.
  • He died on 17 November 1928.
  • To avenge his death, the HSRA decided to kill John Saunders — the Superintendent of Police responsible for the lathi charge.
  • On 17 December 1928, Bhagat Singh, Shivaram Rajguru, and Sukhdev Thapar shot Saunders dead at Lahore.
  • This was the beginning of the HSRA's most active phase.

Central Legislative Assembly Bombing (1929)

  • On 8 April 1929, Bhagat Singh and Batukeshwar Dutt threw bombs into the Central Legislative Assembly in Delhi.
  • The bombs were not intended to kill — they were thrown in the empty part of the chamber to make a point.
  • The aim was to protest against the Public Safety Bill and the Trade Disputes Bill.
  • The revolutionaries raised the slogan "Inquilab Zindabad" (Long Live the Revolution).
  • They could have escaped but chose to be arrested — to use the trial as a platform to spread their message.
  • Their trial became a public spectacle — Bhagat Singh used it to make powerful statements about freedom and socialism.

Bhagat Singh's Trial and Martyrdom

  • Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, and Rajguru were tried for the Saunders murder case.
  • They were also tried for the Central Legislative Assembly bombing.
  • Bhagat Singh conducted a historic hunger strike (1929) in jail — demanding better conditions for political prisoners. It lasted 116 days.
  • The British government refused to treat them as political prisoners.
  • On 23 March 1931, Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, and Rajguru were hanged at Lahore Central Jail.
  • Bhagat Singh was just 23 years old.
  • Their martyrdom sparked nationwide outrage and inspired millions.
BPSC Must-Know: Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev, and Rajguru were hanged on 23 March 1931 — celebrated in India as Shaheed Diwas (Martyr's Day). Bhagat Singh was only 23 years old. His famous slogans: "Inquilab Zindabad" and "They may kill me, but they cannot kill my ideas."

Chandrashekhar Azad (1906–1931)

  • Born Chandrashekhar Tiwari in Bhabhra, Madhya Pradesh in 1906.
  • Joined the HRA at the age of 15 — after the Jallianwala Bagh Massacre.
  • Was involved in the Kakori Conspiracy (1925) and the Saunders' killing (1928).
  • Vowed never to be captured alive by the British.
  • On 27 February 1931, he was surrounded by police at Alfred Park, Allahabad.
  • He fought a gun battle, killed several policemen, and then shot himself — keeping his vow.
  • He was just 24 years old.
  • Alfred Park was renamed Chandrashekhar Azad Park in his memory.

Chittagong Armoury Raid (1930)

  • On 18 April 1930, a group of revolutionaries attacked the police and auxiliary forces' armouries at Chittagong (now in Bangladesh).
  • Led by Surya Sen (called "Masterda").
  • The raiders captured the armouries, cut off telephone and telegraph lines, and disrupted railway communications.
  • They proclaimed a Provisional Revolutionary Government at Chittagong.
  • The British recaptured the armouries — the revolutionaries fled to the hills.
  • Surya Sen was arrested in 1933 and hanged on 12 January 1934.
  • Several women revolutionaries — including Pritilata Waddedar — were involved in the movement.
  • Pritilata Waddedar led an attack on the Pahartali European Club in 1932 and consumed cyanide to avoid capture.

Other Revolutionary Actions

  • Pahartali European Club Attack (1932) — led by Pritilata Waddedar.
  • Writers' Building Attack (1930) — Bengal revolutionaries attacked the Writers' Building in Calcutta.
  • Assembly Bomb Case (1929) — Bhagat Singh and B.K. Dutt.
  • Viceroy's Train Bombing (1929) — an attempt to derail the Viceroy's train near Delhi.

Revolutionary Activities Abroad

India House, London (1905–1910)

  • Founded by Shyamji Krishna Varma in London.
  • Provided scholarships to Indian students and promoted revolutionary ideas.
  • Published the journal The Indian Sociologist.
  • Key members: V.D. Savarkar, Madan Lal Dhingra, V.V.S. Aiyar.
  • Madan Lal Dhingra assassinated Sir Curzon Wyllie in London in 1909 — and was hanged.

Ghadar Party (1913) — USA

  • Founded by Lala Har Dayal and Sohan Singh Bhakna in San Francisco.
  • Published the newspaper Ghadar in Urdu and Punjabi.
  • Worked for the overthrow of British rule in India through armed revolution.
  • The Ghadar Conspiracy (1915) failed — but inspired future revolutionaries.

Berlin Committee (1915)

  • Founded by Virendranath Chattopadhyay and others in Germany during World War I.
  • Worked with the German government to support Indian revolutionaries.
  • Planned to send arms and men to India to spark a revolution.
  • The plan failed — but it showed the international dimension of the freedom struggle.

Indian National Army (INA) and Subhash Chandra Bose

The INA — though not a "revolutionary" organisation in the traditional sense — was a culmination of the armed struggle tradition.

  • First formed in 1942 by Rash Behari Bose — an Indian revolutionary who had lived in Japan for decades.
  • Reorganised and expanded by Subhash Chandra Bose in 1943.
  • Had a women's regiment — the Rani of Jhansi Regiment — led by Captain Lakshmi Swaminathan (Lakshmi Sahgal).
  • Fought alongside the Japanese in Burma, Manipur, and Nagaland — reaching Moirang (Manipur) in 1944.
  • The INA Trials (1945–46) at the Red Fort in Delhi made the INA leaders national heroes.
  • The trials sparked the Royal Indian Navy Mutiny (1946) — a major event that shook British confidence.
Exam Tip: The Indian National Army (INA) was first formed by Rash Behari Bose and reorganised by Subhash Chandra Bose in 1943. The Rani of Jhansi Regiment was the women's wing — led by Lakshmi Sahgal. The INA Trials (1945–46) at the Red Fort made the accused into national heroes.

Impact of Revolutionary Nationalism

  • Kept the spirit of resistance alive — even when the Congress was inactive, the revolutionaries continued the struggle.
  • Inspired the masses — the martyrdom of Bhagat Singh, Rajguru, and Sukhdev electrified the nation.
  • Forced the British to concede — the British realised that they could not rule India by force alone.
  • Brought socialism into the mainstream — the HSRA's demand for a socialist republic influenced later leaders like Jawaharlal Nehru.
  • United Hindus and Muslims — revolutionaries like Ashfaqullah Khan worked side by side with Hindu comrades.
  • Inspired the INA — Subhash Chandra Bose's INA was the culmination of the armed struggle tradition.
  • Shook the British Empire — the INA Trials (1945–46) and the Royal Indian Navy Mutiny (1946) proved that British rule could not continue.
  • Made Independence inevitable — the combined effect of Gandhian mass movements and revolutionary nationalism forced the British to leave India in 1947.

Limitations of Revolutionary Nationalism

  • Limited mass base — the revolutionaries were mostly from the educated middle class; they had little contact with the masses.
  • Lack of clear ideology — until the HSRA, the revolutionaries were unclear about the political system they wanted.
  • Individual acts of heroism — the assassinations and bombings, while heroic, did not seriously threaten British rule.
  • Repression by the British — the British crushed the revolutionary movement with mass arrests and executions.
  • Internal divisions — different revolutionary groups operated independently and often did not coordinate.
  • Ineffective organisation — the secret societies were easily infiltrated by British spies.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • Bihar's role in the revolutionary movement — Bihar produced several revolutionaries and provided support to the movement.
  • Bihar Students' Conference (1906) — the first major political platform for Bihar's youth; spread nationalist ideas.
  • Bihar Provincial Conference (1908) — included revolutionary sympathisers; leaders like Sachchidananda Sinha and others participated.
  • Anushilan Samiti in Bihar — branches of the Anushilan Samiti spread to Bihar; young men from Bihar joined the revolutionary movement.
  • Yogendra Shukla (1896–1960) — a revolutionary from Bihar; worked with the HSRA; imprisoned in the Cellular Jail for his activities; close associate of Bhagat Singh and his comrades.
  • Baikunth Shukla (1907–1934) — a revolutionary from Bihar; executed for his role in the assassination of Phanindra Nath Ghosh — a British approver in the Bhagat Singh case.
  • Gaya Ji (Gaya Prasad) — another Bihar revolutionary involved in the freedom struggle.
  • Anugrah Narayan Sinha and Sri Krishna Sinha — key leaders of the Congress in Bihar who supported the nationalist cause.
  • Jayaprakash Narayan (1902–1979) — born in Sitabdiara (Saran, Bihar); though primarily a socialist, he was influenced by the revolutionary tradition.
  • Bihar and the INA Trials — Bihar's leaders (including Dr. Rajendra Prasad) defended the INA accused and supported their cause.
  • Bihar and the Royal Indian Navy Mutiny (1946) — students in Bihar, including in Patna, supported the RIN mutineers.
  • Bihar's contribution to the Quit India Movement (1942) — Bihar was one of the strongest centres of the movement; Jayaprakash Narayan escaped from jail and led the underground movement.
BPSC Must-Know: Yogendra Shukla and Baikunth Shukla were two important revolutionaries from Bihar. Yogendra Shukla was a close associate of Bhagat Singh and was imprisoned in the Cellular Jail. Baikunth Shukla was executed in 1934 for his role in the assassination of Phanindra Nath Ghosh — the approver in the Bhagat Singh case. Bihar's role in the revolutionary movement is an important but lesser-known aspect of the freedom struggle.
