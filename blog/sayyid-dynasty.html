Sayyid Dynasty: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 11 min read · BPSC SSC

The Sayyid Dynasty (1414–1451 CE) was the fourth dynasty of the Delhi Sultanate. It was founded by Khizr Khan in the political vacuum left behind by Timur's devastating invasion of 1398 CE. The Sayyids ruled for only 37 years and controlled a much smaller territory than their predecessors. Their period is often described as a "twilight" of the Delhi Sultanate — a time of weak rulers, constant rebellions, and the rise of regional powers like the Jaunpur Sultanate, Malwa, and Gujarat. For BPSC and SSC, the Sayyid period is important mainly for the transition from the Tughlaqs to the Lodis.

Exam Tip: Yahiya bin Ahmad Sirhindi's Tarikh-i-Mubarakshahi is the single most important source for the Sayyid Dynasty. It covers the reigns of Khizr Khan, Mubarak Shah and Muhammad Shah in detail.

Background — The Aftermath of Timur's Invasion

  • Timur (Tamerlane) invaded India in 1398 CE and sacked Delhi — the city was plundered, its population massacred, and the Tughlaq Sultanate reduced to a shadow.
  • Before returning to Samarkand, Timur appointed Khizr Khan — a Sayyid noble from Multan — as his governor in the Punjab and Multan region.
  • The Tughlaq Sultan Nasir-ud-din Mahmud Shah fled and later died in 1413 CE.
  • With no effective central authority, Khizr Khan marched on Delhi and occupied it in 1414 CE, founding the Sayyid Dynasty.
  • The Sayyids claimed descent from the Prophet Muhammad through his daughter Fatima — hence the name "Sayyid."

Khizr Khan (1414–1421 CE) — The Founder

Founder of the Sayyid Dynasty. A practical and cautious ruler who preferred to work behind the scenes rather than claim imperial titles.

  • Was a Sayyid (descendant of the Prophet) from a noble family of Multan.
  • Served as governor of Multan and Punjab under Timur's authority after 1398 CE.
  • Occupied Delhi in 1414 CE after the death of the last Tughlaq ruler, ending the Tughlaq dynasty.
  • Did not assume the title of Sultan — he called himself Rayat-i-Ala ("the exalted chief") and continued to acknowledge the nominal suzerainty of Timur's successors (the Timurid rulers of Persia).
  • Struck coins and had the Khutba (Friday sermon) read in the name of the Timurid ruler Shahrukh — not in his own name.
  • His policy of not claiming imperial titles reflects both political prudence and his modest position.
  • Ruled a small territory around Delhi — Punjab, parts of the Doab, and the region around Delhi.
  • Faced constant rebellions from the Rajputs of Mewar, the Khokhars of Punjab, and the Sharqi rulers of Jaunpur.
  • Suppressed the revolt of Har Singh and other local chiefs.
  • Tried to recover lost provinces but had limited success — most of the empire had already become independent.
  • Died in 1421 CE and was succeeded by his son Mubarak Shah.
Remember: Khizr Khan was the founder of the Sayyid Dynasty but he never assumed the title of Sultan. He ruled as Rayat-i-Ala — a deputy of the Timurid ruler Shahrukh. This is a unique feature of the Sayyid Dynasty.

Mubarak Shah (1421–1434 CE)

Son of Khizr Khan; the first Sayyid ruler to assume full royal titles and the most capable ruler of the dynasty.

  • Was the first Sayyid ruler to assume the title of Sultan — he issued coins and had the Khutba read in his own name.
  • Took the title Mubarak Shah and struck his own coins — a break from his father's policy of Timurid suzerainty.
  • Reorganised the administration and tried to restore the prestige of the Sultanate.
  • Faced a major rebellion by Jasrat Khokhar — a powerful Khokhar chief who declared himself Sultan. Mubarak Shah defeated him in 1423 CE.
  • Suppressed the revolt of the Rajputs of Mewar and other local chiefs.
  • Fought against the Sharqi Sultan of Jaunpur, Ibrahim Shah Sharqi — with mixed success.
  • Commissioned a history of his own reign — the Tarikh-i-Mubarakshahi by Yahiya bin Ahmad Sirhindi was written during his reign.
  • Took an interest in literature and scholarship; patronised scholars and poets.
  • Was murdered by his own nobles (including Sarwar-ul-Mulk and Kamal-ul-Mulk) in 1434 CE — a conspiracy by the court elite.
Exam Tip: Mubarak Shah was the first Sayyid ruler to assume the title of Sultan and to strike coins in his own name. He was murdered by his own nobles in 1434 CE. His reign is covered in detail by Tarikh-i-Mubarakshahi.

Muhammad Shah (1434–1445 CE)

Successor of Mubarak Shah and the son of Khizr Khan's brother — a weak ruler whose reign saw the rapid decline of Sayyid authority.

  • Was appointed Sultan by the nobles who had murdered Mubarak Shah — a sign of the growing power of the court elite.
  • Took the title Muhammad Shah.
  • Was a weak and ineffective ruler — real power lay with the nobles and the Wazir.
  • Faced constant rebellions and invasions from neighbouring powers.
  • Rana Kumbha of Mewar — a powerful Rajput ruler — invaded and defeated Muhammad Shah's forces multiple times.
  • Mahmud Khalji, the Sultan of Malwa, invaded Delhi's territory and defeated the Sayyid army.
  • The Sharqi Sultan of Jaunpur also threatened Delhi from the east.
  • By the end of his reign, the Sayyid Sultanate had shrunk to a small area around Delhi.
  • Many provinces — Bengal, Gujarat, Malwa, Jaunpur, Bahmani, Vijayanagara — had become fully independent.
  • Died in 1445 CE, leaving a greatly weakened Sultanate.

Alam Shah (1445–1451 CE) — The Last Sayyid

The last ruler of the Sayyid Dynasty. His short reign ended with the peaceful transfer of power to the Lodis — a rare event in Delhi Sultanate history.

  • Was the son of Muhammad Shah; became Sultan in 1445 CE.
  • Was an incompetent and pleasure-loving ruler — more interested in luxury than in governance.
  • Real power passed to his Wazir and to powerful nobles like Bahlul Lodi (governor of Lahore and Sirhind).
  • Faced constant threats from the Sharqi Sultan of Jaunpur and from regional chiefs.
  • In 1451 CE, Alam Shah voluntarily abdicated the throne in favour of Bahlul Lodi — and retired to Badaun, where he lived until 1478 CE.
  • This ended the Sayyid Dynasty and began the Lodi Dynasty — the fifth and last dynasty of the Delhi Sultanate.
BPSC Must-Know: Alam Shah was the last Sayyid ruler. He voluntarily abdicated in favour of Bahlul Lodi in 1451 CE — one of the rare peaceful transfers of power in Indian history. Bahlul Lodi then founded the Lodi Dynasty.

Administration of the Sayyid Dynasty

  • The Sayyids inherited the administrative structure of the Tughlaqs — but with much-reduced territory.
  • The Sultan was assisted by a Wazir (Prime Minister) and other ministers.
  • The Iqtadari system continued but was less effective due to weak central authority.
  • Land revenue remained the main source of income — usually 1/5 to 1/3 of produce.
  • The army was smaller and less disciplined than under earlier dynasties.
  • Nobles became very powerful — often dominating the Sultan.
  • Coins of the Sayyid period are relatively rare — indicating a weak economy.
  • The Sultanate was confined to Delhi and its surrounding districts — most provinces had become independent.

Society & Culture under the Sayyids

  • The Sayyid period was a time of cultural transition — Persian and Indian traditions continued to blend.
  • Sufism flourished — the Chishti order remained influential in Delhi and across the region.
  • Bhakti movement also gained strength — Kabir, Ravidas and other saints were active in this period.
  • Literature — Persian histories like Tarikh-i-Mubarakshahi were written.
  • Music — Amir Khusrau's tradition continued; the Sayyid court patronised music and poetry.
  • Regional languages — Hindi, Urdu, Bengali, Gujarati and other languages flourished in the independent Sultanates.
  • Architecture — few major monuments were built during the Sayyid period due to weak economy. Notable structures include the Tomb of Mubarak Shah at Delhi.

Architecture of the Sayyid Period

  • The Sayyid period was not a great age of architecture — due to political instability and a weak economy.
  • Few monuments were built; most were tombs of rulers and nobles.
  • Key structures:
    • Tomb of Mubarak Shah (Delhi) — an octagonal tomb with a dome.
    • Tomb of Muhammad Shah (Delhi) — a simple structure in the Lodi style.
    • Several mosques and madrasas in and around Delhi.
  • The architecture shows continuity with the Tughlaq style — simplicity, use of grey stone, minimal decoration.
  • The octagonal tomb became a distinctive feature of Sayyid and Lodi architecture.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • During the Sayyid period, Bihar was largely under the control of the Jaunpur Sultanate — the Sharqi rulers of Jaunpur exercised authority over much of Bihar.
  • The Sharqi Sultanate of Jaunpur was founded by Malik Sarwar (Khwaja Jahan) in 1394 CE — after Timur's invasion, it became the dominant power in the Ganga-Yamuna region, including Bihar.
  • Bihar Sharif and Maner Sharif — continued as major Sufi centres during this period.
  • Bihar's local chiefs (Bhuyians, Rajputs) — many became semi-independent during the Sayyid period.
  • The Sayyid rulers of Delhi tried to assert control over Bihar but had limited success — the Sharqi rulers of Jaunpur were the real power in the region.
  • After the Sayyid period, Bihar passed under the Lodi Dynasty — and then, after 1526 CE, the Mughals.
  • Sufi saints — the Chishti and other Sufi orders continued to spread across Bihar, playing a major role in the region's religious and cultural life.
BPSC Must-Know: During the Sayyid period, most of Bihar was under the control of the Jaunpur Sultanate (Sharqi dynasty). The Sayyid Sultans of Delhi had only nominal authority over Bihar. This is an important transition period in Bihar's medieval history.

Decline of the Sayyid Dynasty

  • Timur's invasion (1398 CE) — the root cause; the Delhi Sultanate never recovered from the devastation.
  • Weak rulers — after Mubarak Shah, no capable ruler emerged.
  • Powerful nobles — the nobility dominated the Sultan and prevented effective governance.
  • Constant rebellions — Rajputs, Khokhars, and other chiefs repeatedly challenged Delhi's authority.
  • Invasions from neighbouring states — Rana Kumbha of Mewar and Mahmud Khalji of Malwa repeatedly invaded.
  • Rise of regional powers — Jaunpur, Gujarat, Malwa, Bengal and the Deccan Sultanates became fully independent.
  • Shrinking territory — by the time of Alam Shah, the Sultanate was limited to Delhi and its immediate surroundings.
  • Rise of the Lodis — Bahlul Lodi, the Afghan governor of Lahore, gradually became the most powerful noble and eventually took over Delhi.
  • In 1451 CE, Alam Shah abdicated in favour of Bahlul Lodi — ending the Sayyid Dynasty.
