Constitutional Development (1773–1950): Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 15 min read · BPSC SSC

The constitutional development of India from 1773 to 1950 is one of the most important topics in Modern Indian History for BPSC and SSC. It traces the evolution of British rule — from the Regulating Act of 1773 (which first brought the East India Company under British government control) to the Indian Independence Act of 1947 and the adoption of the Indian Constitution (1949–50). Every Act — the Charter Acts, the Government of India Acts, and the Indian Councils Acts — introduced reforms that gradually expanded Indian participation in governance, and each was a step towards responsible government and eventual independence. Understanding this evolution is essential for both Prelims and Mains.

Exam Tip: The constitutional history of India has three broad phases: (1) 1773–1858 — Company rule; (2) 1858–1919 — direct Crown rule with slow reforms; (3) 1919–1950 — gradual introduction of responsible government and finally independence. Remember the phases for context.

1. Regulating Act, 1773

Background

  • By the 1770s, the East India Company had become a political power in India after the Battle of Plassey (1757) and Buxar (1764).
  • Corruption and mismanagement were rampant — the Bengal Famine of 1770 killed millions.
  • The Company sought a loan from the British government — which led to the government demanding greater control.
  • The Regulating Act, 1773 was the first step by the British Parliament to control the Company's affairs.

Key Provisions

  • The Governor of Bengal was made Governor-General of Bengal — with authority over Madras and Bombay presidencies.
  • Warren Hastings became the first Governor-General of Bengal (1773–1785).
  • An Executive Council of four members was created to assist the Governor-General.
  • A Supreme Court was established at Calcutta (1774) — with a Chief Justice and three other judges.
  • The Court of Directors (in London) had to submit revenue, civil and military affairs to the British government.
  • The Act forbade the Company's servants from engaging in private trade and accepting bribes.
BPSC Must-Know: The Regulating Act of 1773 was the first Act by which the British Parliament controlled the East India Company. It created the office of the Governor-General of Bengal (Warren Hastings was the first) and established the Supreme Court at Calcutta (1774).

2. Amending Act (Act of Settlement), 1781

  • Also called the Act of Settlement — passed to clarify the powers of the Supreme Court.
  • The Supreme Court was given the power to hear appeals from the provincial courts.
  • The Governor-General and Council were given immunity from the Supreme Court's jurisdiction in certain matters.
  • The Act clarified that the Court would not interfere in the revenue collection of the Company.

3. Pitt's India Act, 1784

  • Introduced by William Pitt the Younger — the British Prime Minister.
  • Established the dual system of control:
    • Board of Control — a British government body that controlled political and military affairs.
    • Court of Directors — the Company's body that controlled commerce.
  • This dual system continued until 1858.
  • The Act gave the British government supreme control over the Company's Indian affairs.
Remember: Pitt's India Act (1784) introduced the dual system of control — Board of Control (political) and Court of Directors (commercial). This system continued until 1858. This is a common exam question.

4. Charter Act, 1793

  • Extended the Company's commercial monopoly for another 20 years.
  • The Company's charter was renewed.
  • The Governor-General was given the power to override his Council in emergencies.

5. Charter Act, 1813

  • Ended the Company's monopoly in Indian trade — except for tea and trade with China.
  • Opened Indian trade to all British merchants.
  • Provided for the education of Indians — ₹1 lakh per year was allocated for education.
  • Allowed Christian missionaries to enter India freely — leading to increased missionary activity.
  • Asserted the sovereignty of the British Crown over Company territories.

6. Charter Act, 1833

  • Also called the Government of India Act, 1833.
  • The Governor-General of Bengal was made Governor-General of India — the first was Lord William Bentinck.
  • This was the first centralisation of legislative power — the Governor-General had exclusive legislative authority for all of British India.
  • The Company's commercial monopoly was completely abolished — it became a purely administrative body.
  • An Indian Law Commission was set up under Macaulay — which later led to the Indian Penal Code (1860).
  • Provided for the appointment of one Indian as a law member — but it was opposed by the Court of Directors and never implemented.
Exam Tip: The Charter Act of 1833 made the Governor-General of Bengal the Governor-General of India (first: Lord William Bentinck). It was the first step towards centralisation of legislative power. It abolished the Company's commercial monopoly completely.

7. Charter Act, 1853

  • The last Charter Act — it did not fix a period for the Company's rule.
  • Separated the legislative and executive functions of the Governor-General's Council — for the first time.
  • Created a separate Legislative Council — with 6 new legislative members.
  • Introduced open competition for the Indian Civil Service (ICS) — the Macaulay Committee (1854) recommended this.
  • The first ICS examination was held in London in 1855.
  • Indians were allowed to compete — but the exam was held in London, making it difficult for Indians.

8. Government of India Act, 1858

Also called the Act for the Better Government of India — it was the most important constitutional change after the Revolt of 1857.

Key Provisions

  • Ended the rule of the East India Company — the Company was abolished.
  • India came under the direct rule of the British Crown.
  • Created the office of the Secretary of State for India — a British cabinet minister responsible for Indian affairs.
  • The Secretary of State was assisted by a Council of India (15 members).
  • The Governor-General became the Viceroy — the direct representative of the Crown.
  • Lord Canning became the first Viceroy of India (1858–1862).
  • The Crown assumed all powers previously held by the Company — including the right to appoint officials.

Queen Victoria's Proclamation (1858)

  • Issued on 1 November 1858 — along with the Government of India Act.
  • Promised non-interference in Indian religious affairs.
  • Promised to respect the rights of Indian princes.
  • Promised equal treatment of Indians and Europeans.
  • Promised to admit Indians to government service (though largely theoretical).
  • Became the foundation of Crown rule in India.
BPSC Must-Know: The Government of India Act, 1858 ended Company rule and began direct Crown rule. It created the office of the Secretary of State for India and made the Governor-General the Viceroy. Lord Canning became the first Viceroy. Queen Victoria's Proclamation (1858) promised non-interference in religion.

9. Indian Councils Act, 1861

  • Introduced Indians as non-official members in the Governor-General's Legislative Council — for the first time.
  • Lord Canning nominated three Indians — Raja of Banaras, Maharaja of Patiala, and Sir Dinkar Rao.
  • Began the association of Indians with the legislative process.
  • Introduced the portfolio system — each member of the Council was given charge of a specific department.
  • Gave the Governor-General the power to issue ordinances — valid for 6 months.
  • Decentralised legislative power — provincial councils were given some authority.

10. Indian Councils Act, 1892

  • Increased the number of non-official members in the Legislative Councils.
  • Introduced the principle of indirect election — members were nominated on the recommendation of certain bodies.
  • Allowed members to discuss the budget — but not vote on it.
  • Allowed members to ask questions — with prior notice.
  • Made the councils more representative — but the real power remained with the British.

11. Indian Councils Act, 1909 (Morley–Minto Reforms)

Introduced by Lord Minto (Viceroy) and John Morley (Secretary of State) — the first major reforms after the rise of Extremism.

Key Provisions

  • Increased the size of the Legislative Councils — at the centre and in the provinces.
  • Introduced separate electorates for Muslims — a major concession to the Muslim League.
  • Indians were given the right to elect their representatives — through a system of indirect elections.
  • Non-official members could ask questions, discuss the budget, and move resolutions.
  • Indians were admitted to the Viceroy's Executive Council — Satyendra Prasad Sinha became the first Indian to be appointed (as Law Member).
  • The Governor-General retained the power to override any decision.
Exam Tip: The Morley–Minto Reforms (1909) introduced separate electorates for Muslims — a step that later led to communal division and Partition. Satyendra Prasad Sinha became the first Indian to join the Viceroy's Executive Council.

12. Government of India Act, 1919 (Montagu–Chelmsford Reforms)

Introduced by Lord Chelmsford (Viceroy) and Edwin Montagu (Secretary of State) — the first reforms after World War I and the emergence of Gandhian mass movements.

Key Provisions

  • Introduced Dyarchy in the provinces — a system of dual government:
    • Transferred subjects — administered by the Governor with the help of Indian ministers responsible to the legislature (education, health, local government, agriculture).
    • Reserved subjects — administered by the Governor with his Executive Council (finance, police, justice, revenue).
  • Introduced a bicameral legislature at the centre — a Council of State and a Legislative Assembly.
  • Introduced direct elections — a significant step towards representative government.
  • Extended the franchise — about 3% of the population got the right to vote.
  • Established the office of the High Commissioner for India in London.
  • Created a Central Public Service Commission (1926) — recommended by the Act.
  • Introduced the principle of communal representation — separate electorates were extended to Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and Europeans.
  • The Act declared that the goal of British policy was responsible government — but did not specify a timeline.

Reaction to the Act

  • The Act was disappointing to Indian nationalists — dyarchy was complicated and the real power remained with the Governor.
  • The Congress demanded Swaraj — leading to the Non-Cooperation Movement (1920).
  • The Act introduced some reforms — but failed to satisfy Indian aspirations.
BPSC Must-Know: The Government of India Act, 1919 introduced Dyarchy in the provinces — a system of dual government (transferred and reserved subjects). It also introduced a bicameral legislature at the centre and direct elections. The Act was disappointing — leading to the Non-Cooperation Movement.

13. Government of India Act, 1935

The most comprehensive constitutional reform before independence — based on the recommendations of the Simon Commission and the Round Table Conferences.

Key Provisions

  • Provided for an All-India Federation — a federal structure with provinces and princely states. (Never came into being — the princely states never joined.)
  • Introduced Provincial Autonomy — provinces were given elected governments with real powers.
  • Introduced Dyarchy at the centre — but it was never implemented.
  • Provided for a Federal Court — established in 1937.
  • Extended the franchise — about 10% of the population got the right to vote.
  • Provided for separate electorates for Muslims, Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and the Depressed Classes.
  • Abolished the Council of India — the Secretary of State was assisted by a team of advisors.
  • Divided the powers between the centre and the provinces into three lists:
    • Federal List — defence, foreign affairs, currency.
    • Provincial List — police, education, health.
    • Concurrent List — criminal law, marriage, forests.

Elections of 1937

  • Elections were held in 1937 under the Government of India Act 1935.
  • The Congress won a majority in most provinces — forming governments in 8 out of 11 provinces.
  • The Congress resigned in 1939 when Britain dragged India into World War II without consultation.
Exam Tip: The Government of India Act, 1935 introduced Provincial Autonomy and provided for an All-India Federation (which never materialised). It established a Federal Court (1937) and provided for separate electorates for several communities. This Act became the basis of many provisions in the Indian Constitution.

14. Indian Independence Act, 1947

  • Passed by the British Parliament on 18 July 1947.
  • Gave effect to the Mountbatten Plan (3 June 1947).
  • Created two independent Dominions — India and Pakistan — from 15 August 1947.
  • Each Dominion would have a Governor-General — appointed by the British Crown.
  • The British Government ceased to have any control over Indian affairs after 15 August 1947.
  • The Constituent Assemblies of both nations were to act as legislatures until new constitutions were framed.
  • The princely states were free to join either Dominion or remain independent.
  • The office of the Secretary of State for India was abolished.
  • The Paramountcy of the British Crown over the princely states was terminated.

15. Making of the Indian Constitution

Constituent Assembly

  • The Constituent Assembly was formed in December 1946 — under the Cabinet Mission Plan.
  • First meeting: 9 December 1946 — chaired by Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha (temporary chairman, from Bihar).
  • On 11 December 1946, Dr. Rajendra Prasad was elected permanent President of the Constituent Assembly.
  • B.R. Ambedkar was the Chairman of the Drafting Committee — called the "Chief Architect of the Indian Constitution."
  • Jawaharlal Nehru moved the Objectives Resolution on 13 December 1946.
  • The Muslim League boycotted the Assembly — demanding a separate Pakistan.

Adoption of the Constitution

  • The Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949 — celebrated as Constitution Day.
  • It came into force on 26 January 1950 — celebrated as Republic Day.
  • The Constituent Assembly became the Provisional Parliament of India (1950–1952).
  • Dr. Rajendra Prasad became the first President of India.
  • The Constitution of India is the longest written constitution in the world.
BPSC Must-Know: The Constituent Assembly first met on 9 December 1946 — chaired by Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha (from Bihar). Dr. Rajendra Prasad was elected permanent President. B.R. Ambedkar was the Chairman of the Drafting Committee. The Constitution was adopted on 26 November 1949 and came into force on 26 January 1950.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha (1871–1950) — from Bihar; was the temporary chairman of the first meeting of the Constituent Assembly (9 December 1946). He was a leading lawyer, educationist, and politician of Bihar.
  • Dr. Rajendra Prasad (1884–1963) — from Zeradei (Siwan, Bihar); was elected permanent President of the Constituent Assembly (11 December 1946) and became the first President of India (1950–1962).
  • Sri Krishna Sinha (1887–1961) — "Bihar Kesari"; first Chief Minister of Bihar (1946–1961); member of the Constituent Assembly.
  • Anugrah Narayan Sinha (1887–1957) — "Bihar Vibhuti"; first Deputy Chief Minister of Bihar; member of the Constituent Assembly.
  • Brajkishore Prasad — leading lawyer and freedom fighter from Bihar; member of the Constituent Assembly.
  • Bihar and the Constituent Assembly — Bihar was well-represented in the Constituent Assembly; its leaders played key roles in drafting the Constitution.
  • The Permanent Settlement (1793) — introduced in Bihar by Lord Cornwallis; the land revenue system that shaped Bihar's agrarian economy for over a century.
  • Bihar and the Government of India Act, 1935 — Bihar became a province with its own legislature; elections were held in 1937; the Congress formed the government in Bihar.
  • Bihar and the Indian Independence Act, 1947 — Bihar became part of the Dominion of India; the state was not partitioned.
  • Bihar and the Constitution — the Constitution of India came into force on 26 January 1950; Bihar became a state of the Indian Union with its own legislature and Chief Minister.
BPSC Must-Know: Dr. Sachchidananda Sinha — from Bihar — chaired the first meeting of the Constituent Assembly. Dr. Rajendra Prasad — also from Bihar — became its permanent President and the first President of India. Bihar's contribution to the making of the Indian Constitution is immense. Remember these for BPSC.
