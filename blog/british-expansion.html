Expansion of British Rule in India: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 15 min read · BPSC SSC

After the Battle of Buxar (1764 CE) and the Treaty of Allahabad (1765 CE), the English East India Company became the real power in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. Over the next ninety years — from 1765 to 1856 — the Company expanded its rule across almost the entire Indian subcontinent. It did this through a mix of war, diplomacy, subsidiary alliances, and the Doctrine of Lapse. This period is essential for BPSC and SSC — questions on the Anglo-Mysore Wars, Anglo-Maratha Wars, Anglo-Sikh Wars, and the annexation policies of Dalhousie are extremely common.

Methods of British Expansion

The East India Company did not conquer India in a single sweep. It used four main methods:

1. Direct Military Conquest (Wars)

  • Anglo-Mysore Wars (1767–1799) — four wars against Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan.
  • Anglo-Maratha Wars (1775–1819) — three wars against the Maratha Confederacy.
  • Anglo-Sikh Wars (1845–1849) — two wars against the Sikh Empire.
  • Anglo-Burmese Wars (1824–1885) — three wars against Burma.
  • Anglo-Afghan Wars (1839–1919) — three wars against Afghanistan.

2. Subsidiary Alliance System

Introduced by Lord Wellesley (1798–1805). Under this system:

  • The Indian ruler accepted the Company as the paramount power.
  • He maintained a British army in his territory and paid for it.
  • He surrendered his foreign relations to the Company.
  • He accepted a British Resident at his court.
  • He could not employ any European without British approval.

Who accepted the Subsidiary Alliance?

  • Hyderabad (1798) — the Nizam was the first to accept.
  • Mysore (1799) — after the Fourth Anglo-Mysore War.
  • Awadh (1801) — Nawab Saadat Ali Khan.
  • Peshwa Baji Rao II (1802) — Treaty of Bassein.
  • Scindia of Gwalior (1803) and Bhonsle of Berar (1803) after the Second Anglo-Maratha War.
Exam Tip: Lord Wellesley's Subsidiary Alliance System was the most effective instrument of British expansion without direct war. Hyderabad (1798) was the first Indian state to accept it. The Nizam's surrender made it easier for the British to defeat Tipu Sultan in 1799.

3. Doctrine of Lapse

Introduced by Lord Dalhousie (1848–1856). Under this doctrine:

  • If an Indian ruler died without a natural heir, his state would "lapse" to the British.
  • The adopted son of the ruler would not be recognised as heir.
  • This was used to annex several states — Satara (1848), Jaitpur and Sambhalpur (1849), Baghat (1850), Udaipur (1852), Jhansi (1853), Nagpur (1854).
  • Karauli was not annexed, because the British had earlier recognised the adopted son.
BPSC Must-Know: The Doctrine of Lapse was applied by Lord Dalhousie. The states annexed under this doctrine were — Satara (1848), Jaitpur and Sambhalpur (1849), Baghat (1850), Udaipur (1852), Jhansi (1853), Nagpur (1854). Awadh (1856) was annexed not by lapse but on the grounds of misgovernment.

4. Annexation on the Ground of Misgovernment

  • Awadh (1856) — annexed by Dalhousie on the grounds of misgovernment, though the real reason was its strategic importance.
  • This annexation was a major cause of the Revolt of 1857 — the Nawab of Awadh was very popular.

Anglo-Mysore Wars (1767–1799 CE)

Mysore, under Haidar Ali and his son Tipu Sultan, was the most formidable Indian power in South India. The four Anglo-Mysore Wars were among the toughest the Company ever fought.

WarYearsKey Facts
First Anglo-Mysore War1767–1769Haidar Ali won; Treaty of Madras (1769)
Second Anglo-Mysore War1780–1784Haidar Ali died (1782); Tipu continued; Treaty of Mangalore (1784)
Third Anglo-Mysore War1790–1792Tipu defeated; Treaty of Seringapatam (1792)
Fourth Anglo-Mysore War1799Tipu Sultan killed at Seringapatam

First Anglo-Mysore War (1767–1769)

  • The English, the Nizam of Hyderabad and the Marathas formed an alliance against Haidar Ali.
  • Haidar Ali cleverly broke the alliance, defeated the English, and marched to the gates of Madras.
  • Treaty of Madras (1769) — the English agreed to help Haidar Ali in case of attack.

Second Anglo-Mysore War (1780–1784)

  • Haidar Ali formed an alliance with the Nizam and the Marathas.
  • Haidar Ali defeated the English at Pollilur (1780).
  • Haidar Ali died of cancer in 1782 — his son Tipu Sultan continued the war.
  • Treaty of Mangalore (1784) — status quo restored; both sides returned captured territories.

Third Anglo-Mysore War (1790–1792)

  • The English (under Cornwallis), the Nizam and the Marathas allied against Tipu.
  • Tipu was defeated and forced to sign the Treaty of Seringapatam (1792).
  • Tipu ceded half his territory — Malabar, Dindigul, Baramahal and Coorg.
  • He also had to pay a huge war indemnity.

Fourth Anglo-Mysore War (1799)

  • Lord Wellesley accused Tipu of anti-British activities and of conspiring with the French.
  • The English, the Nizam and the Marathas invaded Mysore.
  • Tipu Sultan was killed defending Seringapatam on 4 May 1799.
  • Mysore was partitioned — the English took Coorg, the Nizam got some territory, and a Hindu Wodeyar dynasty was restored in Mysore as a British puppet.
Exam Tip: Tipu Sultan — called the "Tiger of Mysore" — died fighting the British at Seringapatam (1799). He was the first Indian ruler to use iron-cased rockets in war. The Fourth Anglo-Mysore War (1799) ended Mysore's independence.

Anglo-Maratha Wars (1775–1819 CE)

The Marathas were the strongest Indian power in the late 18th century — their confederacy included the Peshwas, Scindias, Holkars, Gaikwads and Bhonsles. The three Anglo-Maratha Wars destroyed their power.

WarYearsKey Facts
First Anglo-Maratha War1775–1782Treaty of Salbai (1782) — status quo; English gained Salsette
Second Anglo-Maratha War1803–1805Marathas defeated; Treaty of Bassein (1802); British control of Delhi
Third Anglo-Maratha War1817–1819Peshwa Baji Rao II defeated; Maratha power ended

First Anglo-Maratha War (1775–1782)

  • Fought over the succession dispute in Pune.
  • The English supported Raghunath Rao against the Peshwa's party.
  • The English were defeated at Wadgaon (1779) — a major humiliation.
  • Treaty of Salbai (1782) — status quo; the English retained Salsette and got Madhav Rao Narayan recognised as Peshwa.
  • This treaty gave the English 20 years of peace with the Marathas — which they used to defeat Mysore.

Second Anglo-Maratha War (1803–1805)

  • The Maratha chiefs (Scindia, Holkar, Bhonsle, Gaikwad) were fighting among themselves.
  • Treaty of Bassein (1802) — Peshwa Baji Rao II accepted the Subsidiary Alliance.
  • Scindia and Bhonsle refused to accept the treaty — they fought and were defeated by Lord Lake and Arthur Wellesley.
  • By the Treaty of Deogaon and the Treaty of Surji Arjungaon, the British gained control of Delhi, the Doab, Orissa and large parts of western India.
  • The Mughal emperor became a British pensioner.

Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817–1819)

  • The Peshwa and other Maratha chiefs made a final attempt to regain independence.
  • They were decisively defeated by the English under Lord Hastings.
  • The Peshwa Baji Rao II was pensioned off to Bithur (near Kanpur).
  • His adopted son Nana Saheb later became a leader of the 1857 revolt.
  • The Maratha Confederacy was dissolved — its territories were annexed or brought under British control.
BPSC Must-Know: The Third Anglo-Maratha War (1817–1819) ended Maratha power in India. The Peshwa Baji Rao II was exiled to Bithur — his adopted son Nana Saheb became a key figure in the Revolt of 1857.

Anglo-Sikh Wars (1845–1849 CE)

The Sikh Empire, founded by Maharaja Ranjit Singh, was the last major Indian power to fall to the British.

Background

  • Maharaja Ranjit Singh (1799–1839) built a powerful Sikh state — with its capital at Lahore.
  • He maintained friendly relations with the British — signed the Treaty of Amritsar (1809) with the English.
  • After his death in 1839, the Sikh Empire was weakened by internal rivalries and political chaos.
  • The British took advantage of this instability to launch a war.

First Anglo-Sikh War (1845–1846)

  • Fought between the Sikh Empire and the English East India Company.
  • The Sikhs were defeated at Mudki, Ferozeshah and Sobraon.
  • Treaty of Lahore (1846) — the Sikhs ceded Kashmir to the British, and a British Resident was stationed at Lahore.
  • Treaty of Bhairowal (1846) — a British Resident was appointed to guide the Sikh ruler during his minority.

Second Anglo-Sikh War (1848–1849)

  • The Sikhs revolted against British interference — led by Sher Singh and Chattar Singh.
  • The English under Lord Dalhousie and Sir Hugh Gough defeated the Sikhs.
  • Battle of Chillianwala (1849) — a costly British victory.
  • Battle of Gujrat (1849) — decisive British victory.
  • Maharaja Dalip Singh (the young Sikh ruler) was pensioned off to England.
  • Punjab was annexed to the British Empire in 1849 CE.
  • This completed the British conquest of India — only Sindh (already annexed in 1843) and Awadh (1856) remained.
Exam Tip: The Second Anglo-Sikh War (1848–1849) ended the Sikh Empire and led to the annexation of Punjab in 1849 CE by Lord Dalhousie. Maharaja Dalip Singh, the last Sikh ruler, was pensioned off to England.

Annexation of Sindh (1843 CE)

  • Sindh was ruled by the Talpur Amirs — a Muslim dynasty.
  • The British wanted to control Sindh because of its strategic location on the Indus and its proximity to Afghanistan.
  • The First Anglo-Afghan War (1839–1842) exposed the importance of Sindh as a base.
  • In 1843, Sir Charles Napier attacked Sindh and defeated the Talpur Amirs at the Battle of Miani (1843).
  • Sindh was annexed to the British Empire.
  • Napier famously reported his conquest in a single Latin word: "Peccavi" — meaning "I have sinned" (a pun on "I have Sindh").

Annexation of Awadh (1856 CE)

  • Awadh was a wealthy kingdom in northern India — its capital was Lucknow.
  • The Nawab of Awadh, Wajid Ali Shah, was a patron of music and dance but was accused of misgovernment.
  • Lord Dalhousie annexed Awadh in 1856 CE on the grounds of misgovernment — the last major annexation before 1857.
  • The annexation caused deep resentment — the Nawab was very popular, and Awadh had been a loyal ally of the British.
  • This was one of the major causes of the Revolt of 1857.
BPSC Must-Know: Awadh (1856) was annexed by Lord Dalhousie on the grounds of misgovernment. This was the last major annexation before the Revolt of 1857 and one of its main causes.

Anglo-Burmese Wars (1824–1885 CE)

Burma (present-day Myanmar) was the scene of three Anglo-Burmese Wars — which brought Burma under British rule.

WarYearsResult
First Anglo-Burmese War1824–1826Treaty of Yandabo (1826) — Burma ceded Assam, Arakan, Tenasserim
Second Anglo-Burmese War1852Lower Burma annexed
Third Anglo-Burmese War1885Upper Burma annexed; Burma became a province of British India

Anglo-Afghan Wars (1839–1919 CE)

Afghanistan was strategically important to the British because of the "Great Game" — the rivalry with Russia in Central Asia.

WarYearsResult
First Anglo-Afghan War1839–1842British disaster; only one survivor reached Jalalabad
Second Anglo-Afghan War1878–1880Treaty of Gandamak (1879); British control of Afghan foreign policy
Third Anglo-Afghan War1919Treaty of Rawalpindi (1919); Afghanistan independent
Remember: The First Anglo-Afghan War (1839–1842) was a catastrophe for the British — only Dr. William Brydon survived the retreat from Kabul. The Third Anglo-Afghan War (1919) ended British control over Afghanistan's foreign policy.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • Battle of Buxar (1764) — fought in Bihar; secured British dominance over Bengal, Bihar and Orissa.
  • Treaty of Allahabad (1765) — gave the English the Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. Bihar became a part of the Bengal Presidency.
  • Bihar's role in the 1857 Revolt — Bihar played a major role in the revolt, especially Jagdishpur (Arrah) under Kunwar Singh and Amar Singh.
  • Kunwar Singh (Veer Kunwar Singh) — the 80-year-old Rajput zamindar of Jagdishpur (Bhojpur district) who led the revolt in Bihar.
  • Bihar's economy under Company rule — the Permanent Settlement (1793) was introduced in Bihar, changing the land revenue system.
  • Munger (Monghyr) — an important British military station in Bihar.
  • Patna — an important administrative and commercial centre in the Bengal Presidency.
BPSC Must-Know: The Battle of Buxar (1764) and the Treaty of Allahabad (1765) gave the British the Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa. Bihar's role in the Revolt of 1857 was led by Kunwar Singh of Jagdishpur (Bhojpur district) — one of the greatest heroes of Bihar's freedom struggle.
