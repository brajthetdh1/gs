Judiciary — Supreme Court & High Courts: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 15 min read · BPSC SSC

The Indian Judiciary is a single, integrated system with the Supreme Court at the apex, followed by High Courts and subordinate courts. Unlike the USA, India has a single unified judiciary — one system of courts enforcing both Union and State laws. The judiciary is the guardian of the Constitution and the protector of Fundamental Rights. For BPSC and SSC, this topic is high-yield — especially the composition of the Supreme Court, the collegium system, judicial review, and the landmark judgments.

Features of the Indian Judiciary

  • Single integrated system — one hierarchy of courts for the entire country.
  • Independence of the judiciary — secured by provisions on appointment, tenure, salary, and removal.
  • Judicial review — the power to declare laws unconstitutional.
  • Judicial activism — proactive role in protecting rights and enforcing accountability.
  • Public Interest Litigation (PIL) — allows any citizen to approach the court on behalf of the disadvantaged.
  • Appointment by executive + collegium — a blend of presidential appointment and judicial consultation.
  • Fixed tenure — judges have security of tenure; removal only by impeachment.
  • No appeal beyond the Supreme Court — it is the final court of appeal.

SUPREME COURT OF INDIA

Establishment

  • Established on 28 January 1950 — two days after the Constitution came into force.
  • Inaugurated in the Chamber of Princes in the Parliament House (now the Supreme Court has its own building).
  • The first Chief Justice of India was H.J. Kania (1950–1951).
  • Governed by Articles 124 to 147 of the Constitution.

Composition (Article 124)

  • Originally 8 judges — 1 Chief Justice + 7 other judges.
  • Current strength: 34 judges — 1 Chief Justice + 33 other judges.
  • The number can be increased by Parliament by law.
  • Every judge is appointed by the President — but through the collegium system.

Qualification of Supreme Court Judges (Article 124)

  • Must be a citizen of India.
  • Must have been a judge of a High Court for 5 years — OR —
  • Must have been an advocate of a High Court for 10 years — OR —
  • Must be a distinguished jurist in the opinion of the President.
  • There is no minimum age for appointment.

Tenure of Supreme Court Judges

  • Holds office until attaining the age of 65 years.
  • Can resign by writing to the President.
  • Can be removed by impeachment (Article 124(4)).

Removal of a Judge (Impeachment)

  • Grounds: Proved misbehaviour or incapacity.
  • A motion must be signed by 100 members of the Lok Sabha or 50 members of the Rajya Sabha.
  • The motion must be passed by a special majority in each House — two-thirds of members present and voting + majority of total membership.
  • The President then issues the order for removal.
  • Only one judge has been impeached in India: Justice V. Ramaswami (1993) — but the motion failed in the Lok Sabha.
Exam Tip: The Supreme Court was established on 28 January 1950. The first Chief Justice of India was H.J. Kania. Currently, the Supreme Court has 34 judges. The retirement age is 65 years for Supreme Court judges and 62 years for High Court judges.

Jurisdiction of the Supreme Court

1. Original Jurisdiction (Article 131)

  • Disputes between the Government of India and one or more States.
  • Disputes between the Government of India and any State on one side and other States on the other.
  • Disputes between two or more States.
  • The Supreme Court has exclusive original jurisdiction in these cases — they cannot be heard by any other court.

2. Writ Jurisdiction (Article 32)

  • The Supreme Court can issue five writs — Habeas Corpus, Mandamus, Prohibition, Certiorari, Quo Warranto.
  • Only for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights.
  • Called the "heart and soul of the Constitution" by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar.

3. Appellate Jurisdiction (Article 132–134)

  • Appeals from High Courts in:
    • Constitutional matters (Article 132).
    • Civil matters (Article 133).
    • Criminal matters (Article 134).
  • Appeals can be on questions of law or fact.

4. Advisory Jurisdiction (Article 143)

  • The President can refer a question of law or fact to the Supreme Court for its opinion.
  • The Court may or may not give its opinion.
  • The opinion is not binding on the President.

5. Special Leave Petition (Article 136)

  • The Supreme Court can grant special leave to appeal from any court or tribunal in India.
  • This is a discretionary power.

6. Court of Record (Article 129)

  • The Supreme Court is a court of record — its proceedings are preserved for evidence.
  • It can punish for contempt of court.

7. Power of Judicial Review

  • The Supreme Court can declare a law unconstitutional if it violates the Constitution.
  • This power is derived from Articles 13, 32, 131–136, 143, 226, 246, 251, 254.
  • Judicial review is part of the basic structure — cannot be taken away by amendment.

HIGH COURTS

Establishment

  • The first High Court in India was established at Calcutta in 1862 (followed by Bombay and Madras).
  • Governed by Articles 214 to 231 of the Constitution.
  • There is a High Court for each State — or a common High Court for two or more States.
  • Currently, there are 25 High Courts in India.

Composition (Article 216)

  • Each High Court consists of a Chief Justice and such other judges as the President may from time to time appoint.
  • The number of judges is not fixed by the Constitution — it varies from High Court to High Court.

Qualification of High Court Judges (Article 217)

  • Must be a citizen of India.
  • Must have held a judicial office in India for 10 years — OR —
  • Must have been an advocate of a High Court for 10 years.
  • Must have completed 62 years of age (this is the retirement age, not the qualification age).

Tenure

  • Holds office until attaining the age of 62 years.
  • Can resign by writing to the President.
  • Can be removed by impeachment — same procedure as for Supreme Court judges.

Jurisdiction of High Courts

  • Original Jurisdiction — for revenue matters, matrimonial disputes, and constitutional cases within its territorial jurisdiction.
  • Writ Jurisdiction (Article 226) — can issue writs for enforcement of Fundamental Rights and other legal rights.
  • Appellate Jurisdiction — appeals from subordinate courts.
  • Supervisory Jurisdiction (Article 227) — superintendence over all courts and tribunals in its jurisdiction.
  • Transfer of Cases (Article 228) — can transfer certain cases from subordinate courts to itself.

The High Court's writ jurisdiction (Article 226) is wider than the Supreme Court's — because it covers both Fundamental Rights and other legal rights. Judicial Review

The power of the judiciary to review the constitutionality of legislative and executive actions.

  • Derived from Articles 13, 32, 131–136, 143, 226, 246, 251, 254.
  • The Supreme Court can declare a law or executive action unconstitutional if it violates the Constitution.
  • The power is part of the basic structure — cannot be taken away by amendment (Kesavananda Bharati case, 1973).
  • Based on the principle of "procedure established by law" — borrowed from the Japanese Constitution.

Public Interest Litigation (PIL)

  • A judicial innovation — not mentioned in the Constitution.
  • Allows any citizen to approach the Supreme Court or High Court on behalf of the disadvantaged or for matters of public interest.
  • Developed in the late 1970s and 1980s — pioneered by Justice P.N. Bhagwati and Justice V.R. Krishna Iyer.
  • Introduced the concept of "epistolary jurisdiction" — a letter or telegram to the court could be treated as a petition.
  • Used for cases involving environment, human rights, prison reforms, labour rights, consumer protection.

Landmark PIL Cases

  • Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979) — landmark case on prison reforms and the right to speedy trial. It was a PIL filed in the Supreme Court on behalf of undertrial prisoners in Bihar — led to the release of thousands of prisoners.
  • M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1986) — environmental protection; evolved the "absolute liability" principle.
  • Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan (1997) — guidelines against sexual harassment at workplace.
  • S.P. Gupta v. Union of India (1981) — first judges case; established the collegium system.
BPSC Must-Know: Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979) is one of the most important PIL cases in Indian legal history. It was filed on behalf of undertrial prisoners in Bihar — and led to sweeping prison reforms and recognition of the right to speedy trial as a Fundamental Right under Article 21.

Collegium System

The collegium system is the mechanism for the appointment of judges of the Supreme Court and High Courts.

Evolution

CaseYearRuling
First Judges Case (S.P. Gupta v. Union of India)1981Executive has primacy; Chief Justice's opinion not binding
Second Judges Case (Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India)1993Collegium system established; CJI's opinion has primacy
Third Judges Case (In re: Special Reference No. 1 of 1998)1998Collegium = CJI + 4 senior-most judges of the Supreme Court
Fourth Judges Case (Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record Association v. Union of India)201599th Amendment (NJAC) struck down; collegium system restored

Composition of the Collegium

  • Supreme Court Collegium: Chief Justice of India + 4 senior-most judges of the Supreme Court.
  • High Court Collegium: Chief Justice of the High Court + 2 senior-most judges of that High Court.
  • Recommendations are made to the President, who appoints the judges.

NJAC (National Judicial Appointments Commission)

  • Established by the 99th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2014 and the NJAC Act, 2014.
  • Would have consisted of: Chief Justice of India, 2 senior-most judges of the Supreme Court, the Union Law Minister, and 2 eminent persons.
  • Struck down by the Supreme Court in 2015 (Fourth Judges Case) — held to be unconstitutional as it violated judicial independence.
  • The collegium system was restored.

Judicial Activism

  • The proactive role of the judiciary in protecting rights and enforcing accountability.
  • Emerges when the legislature and executive fail to act.
  • Examples: Environmental protection, prison reforms, food security, right to education, women's rights.
  • Criticism: Overreach; judicial legislation; interference in policy matters.

Judicial Restraint

  • The opposite of judicial activism — the judiciary limits itself to its constitutional role.
  • Emphasises that the judiciary should not encroach on the powers of the legislature and executive.
  • Advocates respect for the separation of powers.

Other Important Aspects

Advisory Jurisdiction (Article 143)

  • The President can refer a question of law or fact to the Supreme Court.
  • The Supreme Court may or may not give its opinion.
  • The opinion is not binding.
  • Examples: Berubari Union case (1960), Cauvery Water Dispute (1992), Ayodhya case (1993).

Contempt of Court

  • Article 129 — the Supreme Court is a court of record; can punish for contempt.
  • Article 215 — High Courts are courts of record; can punish for contempt.
  • Two types: Civil contempt (wilful disobedience of a court order) and Criminal contempt (scandalising the court, obstructing justice).
  • Governed by the Contempt of Courts Act, 1971.

Judicial Independence

  • Security of tenure — judges cannot be removed easily.
  • Fixed salaries and allowances — not subject to vote in Parliament.
  • Appointment by collegium — reduces executive interference.
  • Prohibition on practice after retirement in the same court (for Supreme Court judges).
  • Contempt power — protects the dignity of the judiciary.
  • Separation of judiciary from executive (Article 50).

Judicial Reforms — Recent Developments

  • National Judicial Data Grid (NJDG) — tracks pendency of cases.
  • e-Courts Project — digitisation of courts.
  • Fast Track Courts — for quick disposal of cases.
  • Commercial Courts Act, 2015 — for commercial disputes.
  • All India Judicial Service — proposed but not yet implemented.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • Patna High Court — established on 1 March 1916, making it one of the oldest High Courts in India. Its jurisdiction covers the entire state of Bihar.
  • Patna High Court — First Chief Justice: Sir Justice Edward Maynard Des Champs Chamier (1916).
  • First Indian Chief Justice of Patna High Court: Justice Sir Syed Sultan Ahmed (1943).
  • Justice K. Subba Rao — former Chief Justice of India (1966–1967) — was born in Rajahmundry (Andhra Pradesh), but several Supreme Court judges have had strong Bihar connections.
  • Justice M.H. Beg — former Chief Justice of India (1977–1978) — was born in Meerut but had connections to Bihar.
  • Justice R.M. Sahai — a Supreme Court judge — was from Bihar.
  • Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979) — the most famous PIL case from Bihar. It led to prison reforms across India and the recognition of the right to speedy trial under Article 21.
  • Bihar has 40 Lok Sabha seats and 16 Rajya Sabha seats — the state's litigants frequently approach the Supreme Court for matters relating to reservation, land reforms, and criminal justice.
  • Patna High Court has been the springboard for many Supreme Court judges — including several Chief Justices of India.
  • Bihar's litigation load is among the highest in India — with a large number of pending cases in the Patna High Court and subordinate courts.
BPSC Must-Know: The Patna High Court was established on 1 March 1916 — one of the oldest High Courts in India. Hussainara Khatoon v. State of Bihar (1979) is the most landmark PIL case from Bihar — it led to prison reforms and recognition of the right to speedy trial under Article 21. This is a very high-yield BPSC topic.
