Foundation of Indian National Congress: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 13 min read · BPSC SSC

The Indian National Congress (INC) was founded on 28 December 1885 at Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College, Bombay. It was the first all-India political organisation and became the principal vehicle of the Indian freedom struggle. The INC's foundation marked the beginning of organised nationalism in India. For BPSC and SSC, the INC's foundation, its early sessions, the Moderate phase (1885–1905), and the safety valve theory are essential topics.

Precursors to the Congress

  • December 1884 — the Theosophical Society Convention at Madras passed a resolution to hold an Indian National Union conference.
  • March 1885 — a notice was issued by A.O. Hume announcing the first session of the Indian National Union at Poona.
  • Plague outbreak in Poona — the venue was shifted to Bombay.
  • 28 December 1885 — the first session of the Indian National Congress was held at Bombay. The name "Congress" was suggested by Dadabhai Naoroji.

Allan Octavian Hume (1829–1912) — The Founder

A.O. Hume was a retired British civil servant (ICS officer) who founded the Indian National Congress. He is called the "Father of the Indian National Congress."

  • Served as a British ICS officer in India (1849–1882); was Secretary to the Government of India in the Revenue and Agriculture Department.
  • Was a member of the Theosophical Society and had contacts with Indian nationalist leaders.
  • Founded the INC in 1885 with the help of Indian leaders like Dadabhai Naoroji, W.C. Bannerjee, Surendranath Banerjee, Pherozeshah Mehta and others.
  • After the INC's foundation, Hume returned to England and continued to support the Indian cause from there.
  • Was a strong critic of British exploitation of India.
  • Died in 1912 in London.
BPSC Must-Know: A.O. Hume founded the Indian National Congress in 1885. He is called the "Father of the Indian National Congress." The name "Congress" was suggested by Dadabhai Naoroji, and the first president was W.C. Bannerjee.

The Safety Valve Theory

The Safety Valve Theory is one of the most discussed — and debated — explanations for the foundation of the INC.

What Is the Theory?

  • The theory states that the INC was founded by A.O. Hume with the support of the British government — as a "safety valve" to release the growing discontent of Indians.
  • According to this theory, the British wanted to prevent a violent revolution by providing Indians a peaceful platform to express their grievances.
  • The theory was supported by early Indian nationalists and historians like R.P. Dutt and Bipan Chandra (who later modified it).

Arguments Against the Theory

  • The theory is based on a letter written by Hume — but the letter has never been found.
  • Indian leaders like Dadabhai Naoroji, Surendranath Banerjee and W.C. Bannerjee played a major role in founding the Congress — not just Hume.
  • The INC's early demands were genuinely nationalist — they were not pro-British.
  • The theory is now largely rejected by modern historians.
Exam Tip: The Safety Valve Theory — that the INC was founded as a "safety valve" to release Indian discontent — is largely rejected by modern historians. The INC was genuinely founded by Indian leaders with A.O. Hume's help. Know both sides of the debate for BPSC and SSC.

Objectives of the Indian National Congress

  • To unite Indians of all regions, religions and communities.
  • To educate Indians about their rights and the nature of British rule.
  • To present Indian demands to the British government in a peaceful, constitutional manner.
  • To promote national feeling among Indians.
  • To seek reforms in the administration — not independence, at least in the early years.

Moderate Phase (1885–1905)

The early leaders of the INC are called the Moderates — because of their moderate, constitutional methods. They believed in gradual reform and had faith in British justice.

Key Moderate Leaders

  • Dadabhai Naoroji (1825–1917) — called the "Grand Old Man of India"; first Indian MP in the British House of Commons (1892); author of Poverty and Un-British Rule in India (Drain Theory).
  • W.C. Bannerjee (1844–1906) — first president of the INC.
  • Surendranath Banerjee (1848–1925) — called the "Indian Burke"; founder of the Indian Association (1876).
  • Pherozeshah Mehta (1845–1915) — called the "Lion of Bombay".
  • Gopal Krishna Gokhale (1866–1915) — called the "Mahatma" of the Moderates; mentor of Mahatma Gandhi; founded the Servants of India Society (1905).
  • Romesh Chunder Dutt (1848–1909) — economic critic; wrote Economic History of India.
  • Badruddin Tyabji (1844–1906) — first Muslim president of the INC; lawyer.
  • Anand Mohan Bose (1847–1906) — co-founder of the Indian Association; first Indian Wrangler at Cambridge.

Methods of the Moderates

  • Constitutional agitation — petitions, memorials, resolutions, and delegations.
  • Annual sessions — presenting demands to the government in a peaceful manner.
  • Public meetings and speeches — mobilising public opinion.
  • Newspapers and journals — writing articles and letters to spread nationalist ideas.
  • Delegations to England — sending Indian leaders to Britain to present India's case.
  • Faith in British justice — they believed the British would grant reforms once they knew the facts.

Demands of the Moderates

  • Indian representation in the Legislative Councils.
  • Indianization of the civil services — holding ICS exams in India and raising the age limit.
  • Reduction in military expenditure.
  • Reduction in land revenue and protection for peasants.
  • Abolition of the India Council in London.
  • Separation of the judiciary from the executive.
  • Freedom of the press.
  • Repeal of the Arms Act.
  • Better treatment of Indians in South Africa.

Achievements of the Moderates

  • Created political awareness among Indians — they educated the public about the nature of British rule.
  • Exposed the economic exploitation of India — Dadabhai Naoroji's Drain Theory, R.C. Dutt's economic history.
  • Led to reforms — Indian Councils Act (1892), simultaneous ICS exams, appointment of Indians to the civil service.
  • Built a national organisation — the INC became the platform for all-India political activity.
  • Created a moderate, secular nationalism — they brought together Hindus, Muslims, Parsis, and Sikhs.
  • Inspired the next generation — the Extremists and later Gandhi were inspired by their work.

Limitations of the Moderates

  • Limited social base — the Moderates were mostly from the English-educated middle class; they had little contact with the masses.
  • Faith in British justice — they believed the British would grant reforms voluntarily.
  • No demand for independence — they only demanded reforms within the British Empire.
  • Slow progress — 20 years of moderate politics produced only limited reforms.
  • Economic demands only — they focused on economic and administrative reform, not political freedom.
  • Rise of Extremism — by 1905, many Indians were dissatisfied with moderate methods and demanded more radical action.

The Drain Theory — Dadabhai Naoroji

Dadabhai Naoroji — in his book Poverty and Un-British Rule in India (1901) — developed the "Drain Theory" to explain how Britain was draining India's wealth.

  • "Home charges" — India paid for the salaries of British officials, pensions, and other expenses incurred in Britain.
  • Military expenditure — India paid for the British army stationed in India.
  • Remittances by British officials — they sent their savings back to Britain.
  • Interest on British capital — India paid interest on British investments in railways, plantations, etc.
  • Trade surplus — India exported more than it imported, but the surplus went to Britain as "unrequited exports."
BPSC Must-Know: Dadabhai Naoroji — the "Grand Old Man of India" — developed the Drain Theory in his book Poverty and Un-British Rule in India. He was the first Indian to be elected to the British House of Commons (1892). His work laid the foundation of Indian economic nationalism.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • Bihar's early political activity — the Bihar Provincial Conference (1908) and the Bihar Students' Conference (1906) were important early political platforms.
  • Bihar and the INC — Bihar was part of the Bengal Presidency until 1912; INC sessions in Calcutta drew delegations from Bihar.
  • Sachchidananda Sinha (1871–1950) — a leading lawyer and politician from Bihar; attended several INC sessions; later became the first president of the Constituent Assembly (1946).
  • Dr. Rajendra Prasad (1884–1963) — born in Zeradei (Siwan, Bihar); joined the INC in 1911; became a key leader of the freedom struggle in Bihar and the first President of India.
  • Sri Krishna Sinha (1887–1961) — "Bihar Kesari"; first Chief Minister of Bihar; a major figure of the freedom struggle.
  • Anugrah Narayan Sinha (1887–1957) — "Bihar Vibhuti"; key leader of the INC in Bihar.
  • Rajendra Prasad and the Champaran Satyagraha (1917) — Dr. Rajendra Prasad invited Mahatma Gandhi to Champaran (Bihar), which became a turning point in the freedom struggle.
  • Bihar's contribution to the INC — Bihar produced several national leaders who played major roles in the freedom struggle and in the Constituent Assembly.
BPSC Must-Know: Dr. Rajendra Prasad — from Zeradei (Siwan, Bihar) — was a key leader of the INC and became the first President of India. Sri Krishna Sinha and Anugrah Narayan Sinha were also major INC leaders from Bihar. Bihar played a crucial role in the nationalist movement.
