Fundamental Rights (Articles 12–35): Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 14 min read · BPSC SSC

Fundamental Rights are enshrined in Part III (Articles 12–35) of the Indian Constitution. They are called the "Magna Carta of India" and are the most powerful guarantee of individual liberty in the Constitution. Dr. B.R. Ambedkar described Article 32 — the Right to Constitutional Remedies — as the "heart and soul of the Constitution." For BPSC and SSC, Fundamental Rights are one of the most frequently tested topics. Every aspirant must know all six rights, the articles under each, and the landmark judgments.

Features of Fundamental Rights

  • Justiciable — can be enforced in a court of law.
  • Part of the Basic Structure — cannot be destroyed by any amendment (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973).
  • Guaranteed to all persons — some rights apply to citizens only; others to all persons (including foreigners).
  • Not absolute — subject to reasonable restrictions imposed by the State.
  • Protection against State action — mostly against the State, not private individuals (with some exceptions like Articles 15(2), 17, 23, 24).
  • Some rights available only to citizens — Articles 15, 16, 19, 29, 30.
  • Some rights available to all persons — Articles 14, 20, 21, 21A, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 28.
Exam Tip: Fundamental Rights are available against the State — defined in Article 12 to include the Government and Parliament of India, State Governments and Legislatures, all local authorities, and other authorities within India or under the control of the Government of India.

Remember: The Right to Property was originally a Fundamental Right under Articles 19(1)(f) and 31 — but it was removed by the 44th Amendment Act, 1978. It is now a legal right under Article 300A.

Right to Equality (Articles 14–18)

Article 14 — Equality before Law

  • State shall not deny to any person equality before the law or the equal protection of the laws.
  • Applies to all persons — citizens and foreigners, individuals and corporations.
  • Equality before law (British concept) — no one is above the law.
  • Equal protection of laws (American concept) — like should be treated alike; unlike should not be treated alike without reasonable classification.
  • Exceptions: Article 361 (President/Governor immunity), diplomatic immunity, Article 31C (laws for DPSP).

Article 15 — Prohibition of Discrimination

  • State shall not discriminate on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, or place of birth.
  • No citizen shall be denied access to shops, public restaurants, hotels, places of public entertainment, wells, tanks, roads, etc.
  • Exceptions (special provisions):
    • Article 15(3) — special provisions for women and children.
    • Article 15(4) — special provisions for SEBCs, SCs, STs (added by 1st Amendment, 1951).
    • Article 15(5) — reservation in educational institutions (added by 93rd Amendment, 2005).
    • Article 15(6) — special provisions for EWS (added by 103rd Amendment, 2019).

Article 16 — Equality of Opportunity in Public Employment

  • Equality of opportunity for all citizens in matters of public employment.
  • No discrimination on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex, descent, place of birth, residence.
  • Exceptions:
    • Article 16(3) — residence requirement for certain posts.
    • Article 16(4) — reservation for backward classes not adequately represented.
    • Article 16(4A) — reservation in promotion for SC/ST (77th Amendment, 1995).
    • Article 16(4B) — carry-forward vacancies (81st Amendment, 2000).
    • Article 16(6) — EWS reservation (103rd Amendment, 2019).

Article 17 — Abolition of Untouchability

  • Untouchability is abolished and its practice in any form is forbidden.
  • Enforcement of any disability arising out of untouchability is an offence punishable by law.
  • Implemented through the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955 (originally Untouchability Offences Act, 1955).
  • Applies to both State and private individuals — a rare exception.

Article 18 — Abolition of Titles

  • Prohibits the State from conferring titles (except military and academic distinctions).
  • Citizens cannot accept titles from foreign states.
  • Note: National awards like Bharat Ratna, Padma Vibhushan, Padma Bhushan, Padma Shri are not "titles" — they are decorations and are permitted.
BPSC Must-Know: Article 17 abolishes untouchability — this is implemented through the Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1955. Article 18 prohibits titles but allows Bharat Ratna and Padma awards (they are considered decorations, not titles — clarified by the Supreme Court in Balkrishna v. Union of India, 1996).

Right to Freedom (Articles 19–22)

Article 19 — Six Freedoms

Originally seven freedoms; the Right to Property was removed by the 44th Amendment (1978). Now there are six freedoms:

FreedomReasonable Restrictions
(a) Freedom of speech and expressionSovereignty and integrity of India, security of the State, friendly relations with foreign states, public order, decency or morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement to an offence
(b) Freedom of assemblySovereignty and integrity of India, public order
(c) Freedom of associationSovereignty and integrity of India, public order, morality
(d) Freedom of movementInterests of the general public, protection of interests of Scheduled Tribes
(e) Freedom of residenceInterests of the general public, protection of interests of Scheduled Tribes
(g) Freedom of profession, occupation, trade or businessInterests of the general public, prescription of professional or technical qualifications, state monopoly

Note: Freedom of the press is not separately mentioned — it is included in Article 19(1)(a) as part of freedom of speech and expression.

Article 20 — Protection in Respect of Conviction for Offences

  • No ex-post-facto law — no conviction for an act that was not an offence when committed.
  • No double jeopardy — no person shall be prosecuted and punished for the same offence more than once.
  • No self-incrimination — no person accused of an offence shall be compelled to be a witness against himself.

Article 21 — Protection of Life and Personal Liberty

  • No person shall be deprived of his life or personal liberty except according to procedure established by law.
  • The most important Fundamental Right — expanded by the Supreme Court to include:
    • Right to live with human dignity
    • Right to livelihood
    • Right to clean environment
    • Right to health and medical care
    • Right to education (now Article 21A)
    • Right to privacy (K.S. Puttaswamy, 2017)
    • Right to shelter
    • Right to speedy trial
    • Right to legal aid
    • Right against custodial violence
    • Right to die with dignity (passive euthanasia — Common Cause case, 2018)

Article 21A — Right to Education

  • Free and compulsory education for all children aged 6 to 14 years.
  • Inserted by the 86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002.
  • Implemented through the Right to Education Act (RTE), 2009.

Article 22 — Protection against Arrest and Detention

  • Preventive detention — detention without trial to prevent a future offence.
  • Punitive detention — detention after trial as punishment.
  • Protections for arrested persons:
    • Right to be informed of grounds of arrest.
    • Right to consult and be defended by a lawyer.
    • Right to be produced before a magistrate within 24 hours.
    • Right to be released after 24 hours unless further detention is authorised.
  • Exception: Preventive detention laws (e.g. NSA, UAPA) can extend detention beyond 3 months — but only with an Advisory Board's approval.
Exam Tip: Article 21 is the most expanded Fundamental Right — thanks to judicial interpretation. Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India (1978) is the landmark case that held that the "procedure established by law" must be fair, just and reasonable. K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017) declared the Right to Privacy a fundamental right under Article 21.

Right against Exploitation (Articles 23–24)

Article 23 — Prohibition of Traffic in Human Beings and Forced Labour

  • Prohibits traffic in human beings (slavery, prostitution, human trafficking).
  • Prohibits forced labour (begar and similar forms).
  • Applies to both State and private individuals.
  • Exception: The State may impose compulsory service for public purposes (e.g. military conscription, community service).

Article 24 — Prohibition of Employment of Children

  • No child below the age of 14 years shall be employed in any factory, mine, or other hazardous work.
  • Implemented through the Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986 and the Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Amendment Act, 2016.

Right to Freedom of Religion (Articles 25–28)

Article 25 — Freedom of Conscience and Free Profession, Practice and Propagation of Religion

  • All persons have the right to freedom of conscience and the right to profess, practice and propagate any religion.
  • Subject to public order, morality and health.
  • Does not affect the State's power to regulate secular activities associated with religion.

Article 26 — Freedom to Manage Religious Affairs

  • Every religious denomination has the right to:
    • Establish and maintain institutions for religious and charitable purposes.
    • Manage its own affairs in matters of religion.
    • Own, acquire and administer property.
    • Administer such property in accordance with law.

Article 27 — Freedom from Taxation for Promotion of Religion

  • No person shall be compelled to pay any tax for the promotion or maintenance of any particular religion or religious denomination.

Article 28 — Freedom from Religious Instruction in State Institutions

  • No religious instruction shall be provided in educational institutions wholly maintained by the State.
  • This does not apply to institutions administered by the State but established under an endowment or trust requiring religious instruction.
  • No student in a State-funded institution shall be required to attend religious instruction without their consent.

Cultural & Educational Rights (Articles 29–30)

Article 29 — Protection of Interests of Minorities

  • Any section of citizens with a distinct language, script or culture has the right to conserve it.
  • No citizen shall be denied admission to State-aided institutions on grounds of religion, race, caste, language.

Article 30 — Right of Minorities to Establish and Administer Educational Institutions

  • All minorities — religious or linguistic — have the right to establish and administer educational institutions of their choice.
  • The State shall not discriminate against any minority institution in granting aid.
Remember: Article 29 protects the interests of any section of citizens (not just minorities) — while Article 30 specifically protects the rights of minorities (religious and linguistic) to establish and administer educational institutions.

Right to Constitutional Remedies (Article 32)

  • Guarantees the right to move the Supreme Court for the enforcement of Fundamental Rights.
  • The Supreme Court has the power to issue five types of writs for enforcement.
  • Called the "heart and soul of the Constitution" by Dr. B.R. Ambedkar.
  • Article 32 itself is a Fundamental Right — it cannot be suspended except during a National Emergency (Article 359).
  • Article 226 gives the High Courts similar power — but with wider scope (for Fundamental Rights and other legal rights).

Five Types of Writs

WritMeaningIssued Against
Habeas Corpus"To have the body" — directs the detaining authority to produce the detained personBoth public authorities and private individuals
Mandamus"We command" — directs a public authority to perform its legal dutyPublic authorities only
Prohibition"To forbid" — issued by a higher court to a lower court to prevent exceeding its jurisdictionJudicial and quasi-judicial authorities
Certiorari"To be certified" — issued to quash an order already passed by a lower courtJudicial and quasi-judicial authorities
Quo Warranto"By what authority" — questions a person's authority to hold a public officePublic office holder
Exam Tip: Habeas Corpus can be issued against both public authorities and private individuals — the only writ with this scope. Mandamus cannot be issued against the President, Governor, or a private individual. Quo Warranto can be issued only in respect of a public office of a substantive character.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • Dr. B.R. Ambedkar — Chairman of the Drafting Committee — was elected to the Constituent Assembly from Bihar before representing Bombay. He was the chief architect of Fundamental Rights.
  • Article 17 (Abolition of Untouchability) — has special significance for Bihar, where caste-based discrimination was historically widespread.
  • Bihar's caste-based reservation policies — implemented under Articles 15(4), 16(4) and 16(4A). The Mungeri Lal Commission (1971–1976) and the Karpoori Thakur government implemented OBC reservation in Bihar — a landmark in social justice.
  • Bihar Land Reforms — the Zamindari Abolition Act and land ceiling laws of the 1950s–70s were challenged in courts under the Right to Property (Article 31). These were protected by placing them in the Ninth Schedule.
  • Maneka Gandhi case (1978) — the landmark expansion of Article 21 (right to life and personal liberty) has been used in numerous Bihar cases involving custodial violence, prison reforms, and environmental protection.
  • Environmental cases — the Supreme Court has invoked Article 21 (right to clean environment) in cases related to Ganga pollution in Bihar, especially in Patna and other riverbank cities.
  • K.S. Puttaswamy case (2017) — the Right to Privacy judgment has been cited in cases involving the Bihar government's data collection schemes and Aadhaar-linked benefits.
BPSC Must-Know: Dr. B.R. Ambedkar was elected to the Constituent Assembly from Bihar — he is the chief architect of the Fundamental Rights. Article 17 (Abolition of Untouchability) has special significance for Bihar. The Karpoori Thakur government's OBC reservation in Bihar (1970s) was a landmark in using Articles 15(4) and 16(4) for social justice.
