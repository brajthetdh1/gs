Five Year Plans — Complete Summary for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 12 min read · BPSC SSC

This is a consolidated revision post covering all Twelve Five Year Plans (1951–2017) of India. Each Plan is presented with its period, focus, model used, target growth rate, actual achievement, and key highlights. This is the most exam-ready format for BPSC and SSC — because questions on Five Year Plans are almost guaranteed in every paper. Combined with the Planning & NITI Aayog post, this gives you complete coverage of Indian planning.

Exam Tip: There were 12 Five Year Plans in India — plus two Plan Holidays (1966–69 and 1990–92) and one Rolling Plan period (1978–80). The First Plan was the most successful in meeting its target. The Third Plan was the biggest failure. The Eighth Plan achieved the highest growth rate (6.8%).

Detailed Plan-by-Plan Summary

First Five Year Plan (1951–56)

  • Model: Harrod-Domar model.
  • Focus: Agriculture, irrigation, power, and rehabilitation of refugees after Partition.
  • Target: 2.1% growth; Achieved: 3.6% — most successful.
  • Key Highlights:
    • Launched major irrigation projects — Bhakra Nangal, Hirakud, Damodar Valley.
    • Established the Indian Institutes of Technology (IITs) — starting with IIT Kharagpur (1951).
    • Emphasis on community development programmes.
    • Growth in agriculture was the main driver.

Second Five Year Plan (1956–61)

  • Model: Mahalanobis model — named after P.C. Mahalanobis.
  • Focus: Rapid industrialisation — especially heavy and basic industries.
  • Target: 4.5%; Achieved: 4.27% — moderate success.
  • Key Highlights:
    • Industrial Policy Resolution, 1956 — "Economic Constitution of India".
    • Set up steel plants at Bhilai, Rourkela, Durgapur.
    • Emphasis on the public sector — heavy industries.
    • Poverty remained a concern; agriculture lagged behind.

Third Five Year Plan (1961–66)

  • Focus: Making India a self-reliant and self-generating economy.
  • Target: 5.6%; Achieved: 2.4% — biggest failure.
  • Key Highlights:
    • Failed due to the Chinese aggression (1962) and Indo-Pak War (1965).
    • Drought and food shortage.
    • Led to a Plan Holiday (1966–69).

Plan Holiday (1966–69)

  • Three Annual Plans — no Five Year Plan launched.
  • Reasons: Indo-Pak War (1965), drought, foreign exchange crisis, food shortage.
  • Focus on agriculture — Green Revolution began during this period.
  • Rupee devalued in 1966.

Fourth Five Year Plan (1969–74)

  • Focus: Growth with stability and self-reliance.
  • Target: 5.7%; Achieved: 3.3% — affected by the 1971 war and oil crisis.
  • Key Highlights:
    • Nationalisation of 14 major banks (1969).
    • Green Revolution gained momentum.
    • Family planning programme strengthened.
    • Bangladesh War (1971) and refugee influx.

Fifth Five Year Plan (1974–78)

  • Focus: Poverty removal ("Garibi Hatao") and self-reliance.
  • Target: 4.4%; Achieved: 4.8% — moderate success.
  • Key Highlights:
    • Introduced the Minimum Needs Programme (MNP).
    • Launched the National Rural Employment Programme (NREP).
    • Emphasis on employment and food security.
    • Terminated early in 1978 by the Janata Government.

Rolling Plan (1978–80)

  • Janata Government rejected the Five Year Plan approach and introduced the Rolling Plan.
  • Concept recommended by Gunnar Myrdal.
  • A plan revised every year — with a three-year rolling horizon.
  • Failed due to political instability — the Janata Government fell in 1979.

Sixth Five Year Plan (1980–85)

  • Focus: Direct attack on poverty, employment, and modernisation.
  • Target: 5.2%; Achieved: 5.7% — moderate success.
  • Key Highlights:
    • Introduced the National Rural Employment Programme (NREP) and Rural Landless Employment Guarantee Programme (RLEGP).
    • Focus on Integrated Rural Development Programme (IRDP).
    • Emphasis on family planning and women's welfare.

Seventh Five Year Plan (1985–90)

  • Slogan: "Food, Work, Productivity".
  • Target: 5.0%; Achieved: 6.0% — a success.
  • Key Highlights:
    • Launched the Jawahar Rozgar Yojana (JRY) in 1989.
    • Emphasis on employment generation and poverty alleviation.
    • Focus on food security and agriculture.
    • Growth driven by the services sector.

Plan Holiday (1990–92)

  • Two Annual Plans — no Five Year Plan launched.
  • Reasons: political instability, Balance of Payments (BoP) crisis (1991).
  • India approached the IMF for a bailout in 1991.
  • LPG reforms (Liberalisation, Privatisation, Globalisation) launched in 1991.

Eighth Five Year Plan (1992–97)

  • Focus: Human resource development; rapid industrialisation; LPG reforms.
  • Target: 5.6%; Achieved: 6.8% — a success.
  • Key Highlights:
    • First Plan after LPG reforms — opening up of the economy.
    • Emphasis on human resource development — education, health.
    • Rao-Manmohan model of economic reforms.
    • Growth driven by the private sector.
    • Introduced the concept of "faster growth with equity".

Ninth Five Year Plan (1997–2002)

  • Theme: "Growth with social justice and equity".
  • Target: 6.5%; Achieved: 5.4% — fell short.
  • Key Highlights:
    • Focus on agriculture, employment, and empowerment of women.
    • Emphasis on social justice and regional balance.
    • Launched the Swarnjayanti Gram Swarozgar Yojana (SGSY).
    • Emphasis on IT and services sector.

Tenth Five Year Plan (2002–07)

  • Focus: Double per capita income; reduce poverty by 5% by 2007.
  • Target: 8.0%; Achieved: 7.6% — near success.
  • Key Highlights:
    • Emphasis on monitoring outcomes rather than allocating resources.
    • Launched the National Food for Work Programme (NFWP).
    • Focus on education, health, and infrastructure.
    • Introduced the Bharat Nirman Programme — for rural infrastructure.

Eleventh Five Year Plan (2007–12)

  • Theme: "Faster and More Inclusive Growth".
  • Target: 9.0%; Achieved: 8.0% — partly affected by the 2008 global financial crisis.
  • Key Highlights:
    • Focus on inclusive growth — education, health, rural infrastructure.
    • Launched the Rashtriya Krishi Vikas Yojana (RKVY).
    • Strong emphasis on poverty reduction and employment.
    • 25 monitorable indicators of progress.

Twelfth Five Year Plan (2012–17)

  • Theme: "Faster, More Inclusive and Sustainable Growth".
  • Target: 8.0%; Achieved: ~6.7% — affected by global slowdown.
  • Key Highlights:
    • The last Five Year Plan of India — ended on 31 March 2017.
    • Focus on sustainability and inclusiveness.
    • 25 monitorable targets — including poverty, health, education, infrastructure.
    • Emphasis on Make in India, Skill India, Digital India.
    • India's GDP growth was among the highest in the world during this period.
BPSC Must-Know: The 12th Five Year Plan (2012–17) was the last Five Year Plan of India — ending on 31 March 2017. It was replaced by NITI Aayog's three-year action agenda and 15-year vision document. The theme was "Faster, More Inclusive and Sustainable Growth".

Key Planners and Their Contributions

  • P.C. Mahalanobis — Father of Indian Planning; Mahalanobis model (2nd Plan).
  • Sir M. Visvesvaraya — "Planned Economy for India" (1934).
  • M.N. Roy — People's Plan (1945); first proposed Constituent Assembly idea (1934).
  • Gunnar Myrdal — Rolling Plan concept.
  • V.K.R.V. Rao — First scientific estimate of India's national income (1931–32).
  • Dadabhai Naoroji — First estimate of India's national income (1867–68).

Plan Holiday — A Unique Feature

Plan HolidayPeriodReason
First Plan Holiday1966–69Chinese aggression (1962), Indo-Pak War (1965), drought, food shortage
Second Plan Holiday1990–92Political instability, BoP crisis (1991)

During a Plan Holiday, the government runs Annual Plans — rather than launching a new Five Year Plan.

Key Achievements of Five Year Plans

Economic Achievements

  • India became self-sufficient in food grains — thanks to the Green Revolution (4th Plan onwards).
  • Built a strong industrial base — steel, heavy machinery, chemicals.
  • Life expectancy increased from ~32 years (1950) to ~70 years (2020s).
  • Literacy rate increased from 18% (1951) to ~78% (2020s).
  • Poverty declined from over 50% (1970s) to ~22% (2011–12).
  • Infrastructure — roads, railways, ports, airports, power.
  • Industrial diversification — from agriculture to industry and services.

Social Achievements

  • Expansion of education — schools, colleges, IITs, IIMs.
  • Expansion of healthcare — primary health centres, hospitals.
  • Women's empowerment — reservation in Panchayati Raj, education, employment.
  • Social justice — reservation for SCs, STs, OBCs.
  • Poverty alleviation programmes — IRDP, JRY, MGNREGA.
  • Food security — PDS, NFSA.

Shortcomings of Five Year Plans

  • Regional disparities — some states grew faster than others.
  • Inequality — rich-poor gap increased.
  • Unemployment — job creation lagged behind growth.
  • Agriculture — remained volatile and monsoon-dependent.
  • Bureaucratic delays — slow implementation.
  • Poverty — remained high despite plans.
  • Population growth — offset many development gains.
  • Corruption and leakages — reduced the impact of schemes.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

Bihar and the Five Year Plans

  • Bihar State Planning Board — set up in the 1950s to prepare state plans within the national framework.
  • Gadgil Formula — used for allocating central Plan assistance to states — based on population, per capita income, and other criteria.
  • Bihar, being a low-income state, received higher per capita central assistance under the Gadgil-Mukherjee Formula.
  • Major Plan initiatives in Bihar:
    • Gandak Canal Project — irrigation.
    • Kosi Project — flood control and irrigation.
    • Son Canal Project — irrigation.
    • Sone Command Area Development — agriculture.
    • Barauni Refinery — set up in the 1960s.
    • Barauni Thermal Power Station — power generation.
    • Bihar State Industrial Development Corporation (BSIDC) — for industrial development.
  • The Green Revolution had limited impact in Bihar — unlike Punjab and Haryana.
  • The bifurcation of Bihar (2000) — with the creation of Jharkhand — took away most mineral resources and heavy industries.

Bihar's Development Challenges

  • Low per capita income — despite high growth in recent years.
  • High population density — creates pressure on resources.
  • Recurring floods and droughts — affecting agriculture and infrastructure.
  • Low industrialisation — limits non-farm employment.
  • Low credit-deposit ratio — limits investment.
  • Low human development — despite improvements.
  • Large-scale out-migration — of skilled workers.

Bihar's Post-Planning Development

  • Saat Nishchay (Seven Resolves) — the state government's flagship programme after NITI Aayog replaced the Planning Commission.
  • Jeevika — Bihar Rural Livelihoods Project.
  • Mukhyamantri Udyami Yojana — for young entrepreneurs.
  • Bihar Student Credit Card Scheme — for higher education.
  • Bihar Industrial Investment Promotion Policy — for industrial development.
  • Bihar Skill Development Mission — skilling youth.
  • Bihar Startup Policy — for entrepreneurship.
  • Mukhyamantri Kanya Utthan Yojana — for girls' education and empowerment.

Bihar's Aspirational Districts

  • Bihar has 13 Aspirational Districts under NITI Aayog's ADP — one of the largest shares among Indian states.
  • Districts: Araria, Aurangabad, Banka, Begusarai, Gaya, Jamui, Katihar, Khagaria, Muzaffarpur, Nawada, Purnia, Sheikhpura, Sitamarhi.
  • The ADP focuses on 5 themes — Health & Nutrition, Education, Agriculture, Financial Inclusion, and Basic Infrastructure.
BPSC Must-Know: Bihar State Planning Board was one of the earliest state planning bodies in India. Bihar received central assistance under the Gadgil-Mukherjee Formula. Major Plan-era projects in Bihar include the Gandak Canal, Kosi Project, Son Canal, and Barauni Refinery. The 2000 bifurcation took away most mineral resources. Bihar now has 13 Aspirational Districts under NITI Aayog. These are frequently asked BPSC topics.

Five Year Plans — Key Trends

Growth Rate Trend

  • Growth rates generally increased over time — from ~3% (1st Plan) to ~7% (11th–12th Plans).
  • The 1991 reforms led to higher growth rates in the 8th Plan onwards.
  • The 2000s saw the highest growth rates — driven by services and industry.
  • Global crises (2008 financial crisis, COVID-19) affected some Plans.

Shift in Focus

PeriodFocus
1st–3rd Plans (1951–66)Agriculture → Heavy Industry → Self-reliance
4th–6th Plans (1969–85)Poverty removal, employment, Green Revolution
7th–9th Plans (1985–2002)Employment, food security, LPG reforms, social justice
10th–12th Plans (2002–17)Inclusive growth, human development, sustainability

Shift in Approach

  • Imperative Planning (1st–7th Plans) — government-directed allocation.
  • Indicative Planning (8th Plan onwards) — government provides guidance, markets decide.
  • Bottom-up Planning (NITI Aayog era) — planning from the village level.
Exam Tip: India shifted from imperative planning (government-directed) to indicative planning (market-oriented) with the 8th Five Year Plan (1992–97). This shift followed the 1991 LPG reforms.
