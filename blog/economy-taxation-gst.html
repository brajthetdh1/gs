Taxation in India — Direct, Indirect & GST: Complete Guide

15 Oct 2026 · 14 min read · BPSC SSC

Taxation is the primary source of revenue for any government — it funds public services, infrastructure, defence, welfare schemes and administration. In India, taxes are classified into direct taxes (paid directly by the taxpayer) and indirect taxes (paid on goods and services). The most transformative tax reform in Indian history was the introduction of the Goods and Services Tax (GST) in 2017 — which replaced a complex web of indirect taxes with a single unified tax. For BPSC and SSC, taxation is a high-yield topic — especially the direct vs indirect tax distinction, GST structure and slabs, and the GST Council.

What is a Tax?

A tax is a compulsory payment made by individuals and businesses to the government, without any direct quid pro quo. It is the primary source of government revenue.

Features of a Tax

  • Compulsory — must be paid by law.
  • Legal — levied under a legal authority (Article 265 — no tax without authority of law).
  • Non-quid pro quo — no direct benefit to the taxpayer in return.
  • Periodic — usually paid on a regular basis.
  • Purpose — to fund public expenditure.

Objectives of Taxation

  • Revenue generation — for government expenditure.
  • Redistribution of income — through progressive taxation.
  • Economic growth — through incentives and disincentives.
  • Control of inflation — through fiscal measures.
  • Reduction of inequality — taxing the rich more.
  • Social welfare — funding welfare schemes.
  • Balanced regional development — through tax incentives.

Classification of Taxes

Taxes in India are broadly classified into direct taxes and indirect taxes.

Exam Tip: Direct taxes cannot be shifted — the person who pays is the person who bears the burden. Indirect taxes can be shifted — the seller collects the tax from the consumer and passes it to the government. Income Tax is the largest source of direct tax revenue; GST is the largest source of indirect tax revenue.

Direct Taxes in India

Income Tax

  • Levied on the income of individuals, HUFs, firms and companies.
  • Governed by the Income Tax Act, 1961.
  • Administered by the Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT).
  • Progressive in nature — higher income attracts higher rates.
  • Financial year: 1 April to 31 March.

Note: Under the new regime, the basic exemption limit is ₹3 lakh. Under the old regime, the exemption limit was ₹2.5 lakh — with various deductions (Section 80C, 80D, etc.).

Corporate Tax

  • Levied on the profits of companies.
  • Current rates:
    • Domestic companies — 30% (plus surcharge and cess).
    • New manufacturing companies — 15% (concessional rate).
    • Existing companies opting for lower rate — 22%.
  • Corporate tax accounts for a large share of direct tax collections.

Indirect Taxes in India

Goods and Services Tax (GST)

  • Introduced on 1 July 2017 — through the 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016.
  • Replaced a complex web of indirect taxes — excise duty, VAT, service tax, CST, octroi, entry tax, luxury tax, etc.
  • Called the "One Nation, One Tax" reform.
  • Administered by the GST Council — a constitutional body under Article 279A.
  • Administered by CBIC (Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs).
  • GST is a destination-based tax — tax accrues to the state where the goods or services are consumed.

Note: GST rates have been rationalised over time. Many items have moved to lower slabs. The GST Council periodically reviews rates.

Petroleum Products and GST

  • Petroleum products — petrol, diesel, ATF, natural gas — are currently outside the GST net.
  • The Constitution (101st Amendment) provides that the GST Council will recommend the date from which GST will be levied on petroleum products.
  • Alcohol for human consumption is also outside GST — states have the exclusive right to tax it.
  • Stamp duty, electricity duty, and property tax are outside GST.
BPSC Must-Know: GST was introduced on 1 July 2017. Its four types are CGST, SGST, IGST, and UTGST. The main GST slabs are 0%, 5%, 12%, 18%, and 28%. Petroleum products and alcohol are currently outside the GST net — a frequently asked question in BPSC and SSC.

GST Council — Article 279A

  • Constitutional body — established by the 101st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2016.
  • Chairperson — Union Finance Minister.
  • Members — Union Minister of State for Finance, and one Minister from each State (nominated by the State Government).
  • Functions — recommend GST rates, exemptions, threshold limits, and special provisions.
  • Decision-making — requires a 3/4 majority of the weighted votes of members present and voting.
    • Centre — 1/3 weight (33.33%).
    • States — 2/3 weight (66.67%).
  • Quorum — 1/3 of the total members.
Remember: The GST Council requires a 3/4 majority for decisions — with the Centre having 1/3 weight and States having 2/3 weight. This protects the states' interests — they can pass a resolution only if they are united.

Tax Reforms in India

Chelliah Committee (1991)

  • Recommended reforms in tax structure — simplification, reduction in rates, and broadening the tax base.
  • Led to major reforms in both direct and indirect taxes.

Kelkar Committee (2002)

  • Recommended reforms in direct taxes — simplification, reduction in exemptions, and improvement in tax administration.
  • Led to the introduction of the FRBM Act (2003) and simplification of income tax.

GST Implementation (2017)

  • The biggest indirect tax reform in India since Independence.
  • Replaced 17 different taxes with one unified tax.
  • Reduced the cascading effect of taxes — making Indian goods more competitive.
  • Increased the tax base — more businesses registered under GST.
  • Improved tax compliance through digitisation.

Corporate Tax Rate Reduction (2019)

  • Reduced corporate tax rate from 30% to 22% for existing companies (opting for no exemptions).
  • Reduced to 15% for new manufacturing companies.
  • Aimed at boosting investment and making India a competitive destination.

Tax-to-GDP Ratio

The tax-to-GDP ratio measures the total tax revenue as a percentage of GDP — indicating the tax capacity of an economy.

  • India's tax-to-GDP ratio is around 11–12% (for the Centre) and around 17–18% including states.
  • Compared to OECD countries — where the ratio is over 30% — India's ratio is low.
  • A higher tax-to-GDP ratio indicates better tax compliance and a stronger fiscal base.
Exam Tip: India's tax-to-GDP ratio is significantly lower than developed countries. Increasing this ratio is a key fiscal priority. The GST and digitisation of tax administration are steps towards improving this ratio.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

Bihar's Tax Revenue

  • Bihar's own tax revenue is one of the lowest among Indian states — reflecting the state's low tax base.
  • Key sources of Bihar's tax revenue:
    • SGST — State Goods and Services Tax (after GST implementation).
    • State Excise Duty — on alcohol (Bihar has prohibition — significant revenue impact).
    • Stamp Duty and Registration Fees — on property transactions.
    • Motor Vehicle Tax — on registration of vehicles.
    • Land Revenue — a small share of total revenue.
  • Bihar is highly dependent on central transfers — through the Finance Commission and centrally sponsored schemes.

Bihar and GST

  • GST implemented in Bihar on 1 July 2017 — like all other states.
  • Bihar was one of the first states to ratify the GST Constitutional Amendment (101st Amendment).
  • Bihar's SGST collections have grown steadily since 2017.
  • GST compensation — Bihar received compensation from the Centre for any revenue loss due to GST transition. This compensation ended on 30 June 2022.
  • e-Way Bill — Bihar has implemented the e-way bill system for inter-state movement of goods.
  • Bihar has digitised its GST administration — online registration, filing, and payment.

Bihar and Prohibition — Impact on Tax Revenue

  • Bihar imposed total prohibition on alcohol in April 2016.
  • Before prohibition, state excise duty on alcohol was a significant source of revenue for Bihar (over ₹3,000 crore per year).
  • After prohibition, this revenue was lost — the state had to find alternative sources.
  • However, prohibition has generated social benefits — including improved health, reduced domestic violence, and higher school attendance.
  • Some of the lost revenue has been compensated by higher central transfers and GST collections.
  • Bihar's prohibition is an example of the trade-off between social objectives (Article 47 of the DPSP) and tax revenue.

Bihar's Tax Reforms

  • Digitisation — Commercial Taxes Department has moved to online registration, filing, and payment.
  • GST implementation — simplified the indirect tax structure in Bihar.
  • e-Way Bill — introduced for tracking goods movement.
  • IT-enabled assessment — for faster and more transparent tax administration.
  • Revenue intelligence — strengthened to check evasion.
  • Taxpayer facilitation — help desks and awareness programmes.

BPSC Must-Know: Bihar's own tax revenue is among the lowest in India — the state depends heavily on central transfers.
