Vijayanagara Empire: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 14 min read · BPSC SSC

The Vijayanagara Empire (1336–1646 CE) was one of the greatest empires of medieval South India. Founded by the brothers Harihara and Bukka on the banks of the Tungabhadra river, it stood as a bulwark against the Delhi Sultanate's southward expansion for over three centuries. Its capital, Hampi (Vijayanagara), was one of the richest and most magnificent cities in the world. Under Krishnadevaraya, the empire reached its cultural zenith. For BPSC and SSC, Vijayanagara is essential for questions on medieval Indian history, art and administration.

Exam Tip: Abdur Razzaq (Persian ambassador, 1443 CE) and Domingo Paes (Portuguese traveller, 1520 CE) gave detailed accounts of Vijayanagara. Razzaq called Hampi "such that the pupil of the eye has never seen a place like it." These traveller accounts are frequently asked in exams.

Foundation of the Empire

  • Founded in 1336 CE by Harihara I and Bukka Raya I — brothers of the Sangama dynasty.
  • They were feudatories of the Kakatiyas of Warangal and later served the Kampili kingdom.
  • According to tradition, they were inspired by the sage Vidyaranya (Madhavacharya) to found a Hindu empire to resist Islamic expansion.
  • Capital: Vijayanagara ("City of Victory") — identified with modern Hampi in Karnataka, on the Tungabhadra river.
  • The empire was a confederation of local chiefs, not a centralised monarchy.

Sangama Dynasty (1336–1485 CE)

Harihara I (1336–1356 CE)

  • Co-founder of the empire; ruled from Vijayanagara.
  • Took the title Purvapaschima Samudradhishvara ("Lord of the eastern and western seas").
  • Extended control over the Hoysala and Kampili territories.

Bukka Raya I (1356–1377 CE)

  • Consolidated the empire; moved the capital from Anagondi to Vijayanagara.
  • Defeated the Bahmani Sultanate at several points; signed a treaty in 1368 CE.
  • Patronised Sanskrit and Telugu literature.

Devaraya I (1406–1422 CE)

  • One of the greatest Sangama rulers.
  • Fought the Bahmani Sultanate and the Velama chiefs.
  • Built a strong army with a large contingent of Muslim cavalry — a new feature.
  • Constructed the famous Dam across the Tungabhadra for irrigation.
  • Patronised the Kannada poet Madhura.

Devaraya II (1424–1446 CE)

  • The greatest Sangama ruler — titles Pravata, Gajabetekara ("elephant hunter").
  • Defeated the Bahmani Sultan, the Pandyas, Kerala rulers and the rulers of Sri Lanka.
  • Abdur Razzaq visited Vijayanagara during his reign.
  • Opened administration to Muslims and other communities — a rare example of religious tolerance for the era.
  • Patronised the Kannada poet Chamarasa (author of Prabhulingalile) and the Telugu poet Pothana.
Remember: Devaraya II is called the greatest of the Sangama rulers. He recruited Muslims into his army and administration, and even built a mosque for their worship — evidence of religious tolerance.

Decline of the Sangamas

After Devaraya II, weak rulers and internal dissent (especially between the Saluvas and other noble families) led to the decline of the Sangama dynasty. Saluva Narasimha usurped power around 1485 CE, establishing the Saluva dynasty.

Saluva Dynasty (1485–1505 CE)

  • Saluva Narasimha — a powerful commander who took the throne; annexed the Konkan and parts of Andhra.
  • His son Immadi Narasimha was a weak ruler; real power passed to his commander Narasa Nayaka.
  • Narasa Nayaka's son Vira Narasimha founded the Tuluva dynasty in 1505 CE.

Tuluva Dynasty (1505–1570 CE) — The Golden Age

Vira Narasimha (1505–1509 CE)

  • Founder of the Tuluva dynasty.
  • Continued the expansionist policy; died after a short reign.

Krishnadevaraya (1509–1529 CE) — Greatest Ruler

The most celebrated ruler of Vijayanagara and one of the greatest kings in Indian history.

  • Titles: Kannada Rajya Rama Ramana, Andhra Bhoja, Mooru Rayara Ganda, Karnatakaratna.
  • Conquered Raichur and Udayagiri from the Bahmani successor states.
  • Defeated the Gajapati ruler Prataparudra of Odisha and married his daughter Tirumala Devi.
  • Held the title Yavanarajya Sthapanacharya ("establishment of Yavana kingdom") — referring to his restoration of a Portuguese-allied ruler.
  • Maintained friendly relations with the Portuguese — especially with Governor Albuquerque.
  • Author of the Telugu work Amuktamalyada — a treatise on statecraft and devotion to Vishnu.
  • Patronised the Ashtadiggajas — eight great Telugu poets of his court, including Allasani Peddana (Manucharitram), Nandi Thimmana, Dhurjati, Mallana, and Tenali Ramakrishna.
  • Also patronised the Kannada poet Chatu Vittalanatha.
  • Built the famous Vithala Temple and Hazara Rama Temple at Hampi.
  • Domingo Paes visited during his reign and left a vivid description of the city's splendour.
Exam Tip: Ashtadiggajas — the eight Telugu poets of Krishnadevaraya's court — include Allasani Peddana, Nandi Thimmana, Dhurjati, Mallana, Tenali Ramakrishna, Ayyala Raju Ramabhadrudu, Pingali Surana, Ramarajabhushanudu. This list is a frequently asked exam question.

Achyuta Raya (1529–1542 CE)

  • Brother of Krishnadevaraya; continued his predecessor's policies.
  • Faced repeated conflicts with the Bahmani successor states (especially Bijapur).
  • Weakened the empire's hold on the Raichur doab.

Sadashiva Raya (1542–1570 CE)

  • A young ruler dominated by his minister Rama Raya (Alija Rama Raya).
  • Rama Raya was the real power — a brilliant but overconfident statesman.
  • His policy of playing off the Deccan Sultanates against each other ultimately backfired.

The Battle of Talikota (1565 CE) — The Turning Point

Also called the Battle of Rakshasi-Tangadi or Battle of Bannihatti. One of the most decisive battles in Indian history.

  • Fought on 23 January 1565 CE between Vijayanagara and a coalition of the five Deccan Sultanates (Bijapur, Golconda, Ahmednagar, Bidar, Berar).
  • Vijayanagara was led by Rama Raya (on behalf of Sadashiva Raya).
  • The Sultanates were led by Hussain Nizam Shah of Ahmednagar, with Ali Adil Shah of Bijapur and Ibrahim Qutub Shah of Golconda.
  • Rama Raya was captured and beheaded — his head was displayed on a spear, causing the Vijayanagara army to panic and flee.
  • The victorious army then sacked and destroyed Hampi for several months — the city never recovered.
  • Sadashiva Raya was later killed, and the Tuluva dynasty ended.
BPSC Must-Know: The Battle of Talikota (1565 CE) is often called the "death blow" to Vijayanagara. After the battle, Hampi was pillaged for months. The empire survived in a reduced form under the Aravidu dynasty but never regained its former glory.

Aravidu Dynasty (1570–1646 CE)

  • Founded by Tirumala, brother of Rama Raya, who ruled from Penukonda and later Chandragiri.
  • The Aravidu rulers were called the "kings of Aravidu" — they continued as a diminished power.
  • Venkata II (1586–1614 CE) was the most capable Aravidu ruler — restored some stability.
  • Repeated conflicts with the Nayakas (feudatories), the Bijapur Sultanate and the rising Marathas.
  • The empire gradually disintegrated into successor states — Madurai Nayakas, Thanjavur Nayakas, Mysore Kingdom, etc.
  • The last Aravidu ruler, Sriranga III, was defeated by the Bijapur Sultanate and the Marathas in 1646 CE — ending the empire.

Vijayanagara Administration

  • King — supreme authority, but advised by a council of ministers.
  • Empire divided into Rajyas (provinces) → Nadus → Gramas (villages).
  • Nayakas — military governors holding land grants; they were powerful and often hereditary.
  • Ayagar system — village-level officials (like the Chola period); the village was the basic unit of administration.
  • Amara-Nayaka system — military chiefs were given land (Amaram) in return for maintaining troops. This system later became a source of weakness.
  • Land revenue was the main income — usually 1/6 of produce.
  • Taxes on trade, crafts, and professions (including a tax on prostitution, as recorded by travellers).
  • Military: large army with infantry, cavalry, elephants and artillery (including Portuguese-supplied guns).

Economy

  • Agriculture — backbone of the economy; extensive irrigation through tanks, canals and dams (e.g. the Tungabhadra dam).
  • Trade — both inland and overseas; main exports: cotton textiles, spices, rice, sugar, iron, and diamonds (from Golconda mines).
  • Main ports: Goa, Honavar, Bhatkal, Mangalore on the west coast; Masulipatnam, Pulicat on the east coast.
  • Horses were imported from Arabia and Persia — a major expense.
  • Portuguese, Arab and Chinese traders were active in Vijayanagara ports.
  • Gold coins called Varaha and Pratapa were in circulation.

Art & Architecture — Hampi

The Hampi ruins are a UNESCO World Heritage Site and represent the finest example of Vijayanagara architecture.

Key Features of Vijayanagara Architecture

  • Amman shrine — a separate shrine for the consort of the deity within the temple complex (a Vijayanagara innovation).
  • Kalyana Mandapa — a pillared marriage hall in front of the main shrine.
  • Gopurams — tall entrance gateways became more prominent.
  • Raya Gopurams — massive entrance towers, sometimes taller than the main vimana.
  • Musical Pillars — the Vithala Temple pillars produce musical notes when struck.
  • Stone Chariot — an iconic monolithic chariot at the Vithala Temple.
  • Influence of Indo-Islamic architecture in secular buildings (Lotus Mahal, Elephant Stables).
  • Extensive use of granite as building material (unlike the softer stone of earlier South Indian temples).
Exam Tip: The Stone Chariot of Vithala Temple and the Musical Pillars are iconic Vijayanagara monuments. The Amman shrine and the Kalyana Mandapa are Vijayanagara innovations in temple architecture. Hampi has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1986.

Literature & Culture

  • Telugu literature flourished — Krishnadevaraya's court was called Andhra Bhoja.
  • Ashtadiggajas — Allasani Peddana, Nandi Thimmana, Dhurjati, Mallana, Tenali Ramakrishna, Ayyala Raju Ramabhadrudu, Pingali Surana, Ramarajabhushanudu.
  • Kannada literature — Chamarasa (Prabhulingalile), Chatu Vittalanatha, Kumara Vyasa (Karnataka Bharata Kathamanjari).
  • Sanskrit — Vidyaranya (Madhavacharya) wrote the Sarva Darshana Sangraha and other works.
  • Amuktamalyada — Krishnadevaraya's own Telugu work on statecraft and Vishnu Bhakti.
  • Religion — primarily Vaishnava and Shaiva Hinduism; tolerant of other faiths.
  • Bhakti movement — the Haridasa movement (Purandara Dasa, Kanaka Dasa) flourished under Vijayanagara patronage.
  • Vidyaranya — a key spiritual figure; helped found the empire.

Decline of the Vijayanagara Empire

  • Battle of Talikota (1565 CE) — the immediate and most decisive cause.
  • Sack of Hampi — the capital was destroyed and never rebuilt.
  • Weak successors — no ruler could match Krishnadevaraya's ability.
  • Amara-Nayaka system — the Nayakas became virtually independent, weakening central authority.
  • Constant wars with the Deccan Sultanates, the Marathas and the Nayakas drained resources.
  • Rise of the Marathas under Shivaji in the 17th century.
  • Bijapur Sultanate and later the Mughals invaded the remaining Aravidu territory.
  • By 1646 CE, the empire had ceased to exist as a unified state.
