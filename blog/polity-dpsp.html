Directive Principles & Fundamental Duties: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 13 min read · BPSC SSC

The Directive Principles of State Policy (DPSP) are contained in Part IV (Articles 36–51) of the Constitution. They are the "conscience of the Constitution" — non-justiciable guidelines that the State should follow while making laws and framing policies. The Fundamental Duties are in Part IV-A (Article 51A) — added by the 42nd Amendment (1976) on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee. Together, DPSP and Fundamental Duties form the moral foundation of Indian democracy. For BPSC and SSC, this topic is essential — especially the classification of DPSP, the amendments, and the landmark judgments.

Directive Principles — Features

  • Contained in Part IV (Articles 36–51).
  • Borrowed from the Irish Constitution (which itself borrowed from the Spanish Constitution).
  • Non-justiciable — cannot be enforced in a court of law.
  • Not a source of any right — but the State is expected to follow them.
  • Aim at establishing a welfare state.
  • Provide the socio-economic foundation of Indian democracy.
  • Reflect the objectives of the Constitution as stated in the Preamble.
  • Classified on the basis of ideology — Gandhian, Liberal-Intellectual, Socialist.
  • Total number of DPSP: Originally 14; now 17 (some added by amendments).
Exam Tip: The DPSP were borrowed from the Irish Constitution. They are non-justiciable — meaning they cannot be enforced in a court of law. However, courts can use them as an aid to interpretation when there is ambiguity in a law.

Classification of DPSP

The Directive Principles are generally classified into three categories based on their ideological basis:

1. Socialist Principles

These aim to establish a welfare state with social and economic justice.

DPSP vs Fundamental Rights

The relationship between Fundamental Rights and DPSP has been one of the most debated topics in Indian constitutional law.

BPSC Must-Know: The Minerva Mills case (1980) restored the balance between Fundamental Rights and DPSP. The Court held that the harmony between FR and DPSP is part of the Basic Structure — neither can be given absolute primacy over the other.

Fundamental Duties — Article 51A

Fundamental Duties are the moral obligations of every citizen. They were added by the 42nd Amendment Act, 1976 on the recommendation of the Swaran Singh Committee.

Features of Fundamental Duties

  • Contained in Part IV-A (Article 51A).
  • Originally 10 duties; one more added by the 86th Amendment (2002) — making 11 duties.
  • Borrowed from the USSR (Soviet) Constitution.
  • Fundamental Duties are non-justiciable — like DPSP, they cannot be enforced in a court of law.
  • However, Parliament can enforce them by law (e.g. Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act, 1971).
  • They apply to citizens only — not to foreigners.
  • They are not enforceable by courts directly — but courts can use them for interpretation.

The 11 Fundamental Duties (Article 51A)

It shall be the duty of every citizen of India:

  1. To abide by the Constitution and respect its ideals and institutions, the National Flag and the National Anthem.
  2. To cherish and follow the noble ideals which inspired the national struggle for freedom.
  3. To uphold and protect the sovereignty, unity and integrity of India.
  4. To defend the country and render national service when called upon to do so.
  5. To promote harmony and the spirit of common brotherhood among all the people of India, transcending religious, linguistic and regional or sectional diversities; to renounce practices derogatory to the dignity of women.
  6. To value and preserve the rich heritage of our composite culture.
  7. To protect and improve the natural environment — including forests, lakes, rivers and wildlife — and to have compassion for living creatures.
  8. To develop a scientific temper, humanism and the spirit of inquiry and reform.
  9. To safeguard public property and to abjure violence.
  10. To strive towards excellence in all spheres of individual and collective activity.
  11. (Added by 86th Amendment, 2002) To provide opportunities for education to children between the ages of 6 and 14 years — a duty of every parent or guardian.
Exam Tip: There are 11 Fundamental Duties. The 11th duty was added by the 86th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2002 — it makes it the duty of every parent/guardian to provide education to their children aged 6–14 years. This is a very common BPSC and SSC question.

Significance of Fundamental Duties

  • They serve as a constant reminder to citizens of their responsibilities.
  • They help in the realisation of the objectives enshrined in the Preamble.
  • They are used by courts as an aid to interpretation — while determining the constitutional validity of a law.
  • They inspire a sense of discipline and commitment among citizens.
  • They encourage citizens to contribute to nation-building.
  • They help promote harmony and a scientific temper in society.

Criticism of Fundamental Duties

  • They are non-justiciable — cannot be enforced by courts.
  • They are not exhaustive — some important duties are left out.
  • Some duties are vague and ambiguous — hard to define and interpret.
  • They are repetitive — some duties overlap with DPSP.
  • They are often described as a "code of moral precepts" without legal force.
  • The penalties for violation are not clearly defined.
  • Some duties are not applicable to foreigners — which is a limitation of their universal applicability.

Article 31C — Protection of DPSP Laws

Article 31C is an important provision that gives protection to laws implementing certain DPSP.

  • Inserted by the 25th Amendment Act, 1971.
  • Originally protected laws giving effect to Article 39(b) and 39(c).
  • The 42nd Amendment (1976) extended Article 31C to all DPSP.
  • The Minerva Mills case (1980) struck down the 42nd Amendment's extension.
  • Current position: Article 31C protects only laws giving effect to Article 39(b) and 39(c) — and even those can be challenged if they violate the basic structure.

Article 39(b) and 39(c)

  • Article 39(b): Distribution of ownership and control of material resources to serve the common good.
  • Article 39(c): Operation of the economic system should not result in concentration of wealth and means of production to the common detriment.

Important Cases on DPSP

State of Madras v. Champakam Dorairajan (1951)

  • The Supreme Court held that Fundamental Rights are superior to DPSP.
  • In case of conflict, Fundamental Rights would prevail.
  • The 1st Amendment (1951) was passed in response — adding Article 15(4).

Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala (1973)

  • Both Fundamental Rights and DPSP are part of the Basic Structure.
  • Neither can be given absolute primacy over the other.

Minerva Mills v. Union of India (1980)

  • Struck down the 42nd Amendment's changes to Article 31C.
  • Held that the harmony between FR and DPSP is part of the Basic Structure.

Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981)

  • Basic Structure Doctrine applies to all amendments after 24 April 1973.

I.R. Coelho v. State of Tamil Nadu (2007)

  • Laws placed in the Ninth Schedule can be challenged if they violate the Basic Structure.
  • DPSP laws placed in the Ninth Schedule are not completely immune from judicial review.
Exam Tip: The Supreme Court has consistently held that Fundamental Rights and DPSP must be harmonised — neither can be given absolute primacy. The Minerva Mills case (1980) is the landmark judgment on this balance.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • Article 40 — Panchayati Raj: Bihar has a long history of local self-government. Bihar was the first state to implement 50% reservation for women in Panchayati Raj institutions (2006).
  • Article 44 — Uniform Civil Code: The UCC debate has been active in Bihar; the state has participated in national consultations on the subject.
  • Article 45 — Early childhood care: Bihar has implemented the Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS) extensively, with a focus on child nutrition and education.
  • Article 47 — Prohibition: Bihar implemented total prohibition on alcohol in April 2016 — a landmark implementation of Article 47. This decision has been widely discussed in the context of DPSP implementation.
  • Article 48A — Environment: Bihar has been actively involved in cleaning and protecting the Ganga river — under the Namami Gange programme. The Ganga flows through Bihar, and pollution control is a major focus.
  • Article 39A — Legal aid: Bihar has an active Bihar State Legal Services Authority (BSLSA) — providing free legal aid to the poor and marginalised.
  • Article 46 — SC/ST welfare: Bihar has implemented extensive reservation and welfare schemes for SCs, STs and backward classes. The state has several welfare boards for these communities.
  • Fundamental Duties: The 11 Fundamental Duties are taught in schools across Bihar — with special focus on respect for the National Flag, National Anthem, and the composite culture of India.
BPSC Must-Know: Bihar's total prohibition on alcohol (2016) is one of the most prominent state-level implementations of Article 47 (prohibition of intoxicating drinks). Bihar was the first state to implement 50% reservation for women in Panchayati Raj — implementing Article 40 in a progressive way.
