Lodi Dynasty: Complete Guide for BPSC & SSC

15 Oct 2026 · 13 min read · BPSC SSC

The Lodi Dynasty (1451–1526 CE) was the fifth and last dynasty of the Delhi Sultanate. It was the only Afghan dynasty to rule Delhi and produced capable rulers like Bahlul Lodi and Sikandar Lodi. The dynasty ended with the First Battle of Panipat (1526 CE), when Ibrahim Lodi was defeated and killed by Babur — marking the end of the Delhi Sultanate and the beginning of the Mughal Empire. For BPSC and SSC, the Lodi period is essential for questions on Afghan administration, the rise of regional powers, and the transition to Mughal rule.

Background — The Rise of the Afghans

  • After the Sayyid Dynasty lost authority, the Afghan nobles became the dominant power in north India.
  • Bahlul Lodi — the Afghan governor of Lahore and Sirhind — emerged as the strongest noble.
  • In 1451 CE, the last Sayyid ruler Alam Shah voluntarily abdicated in favour of Bahlul Lodi.
  • Thus began the Lodi Dynasty — the only Afghan dynasty to rule Delhi.
  • The Lodis claimed descent from the Lodi tribe of Afghanistan.

Bahlul Lodi (1451–1489 CE) — The Founder

Founder of the Lodi Dynasty. He was a capable Afghan chief who rebuilt the Sultanate after the chaos of the Sayyid period.

  • Was the governor of Lahore and Sirhind under the Sayyids.
  • Abdicated the throne of Alam Shah in 1451 CE and became Sultan.
  • Was a cautious and patient ruler — he preferred diplomacy and patience over reckless warfare.
  • Reconquered the important province of Jaunpur (Sharqi Sultanate) in 1479 CE — a major achievement.
  • Extended authority over Gwalior, Kalpi, Etawah and other regions.
  • Adopted a policy of conciliation with the Afghan nobles — he treated them as equals and called himself "the first among equals."
  • Followed the practice of Shafqat (brotherhood) — the Sultan would stand up to greet Afghan nobles and share meals with them.
  • Appointed his sons and nobles as governors of different provinces.
  • Did not assume the title of Badshah; called himself Sultan-i-Alam.
  • Died in 1489 CE at Delhi; buried in the Tomb of Bahlul Lodi (Delhi).
Remember: Bahlul Lodi's policy of treating Afghan nobles as equals — the Shafqat system — was a major reason for the stability of his reign. He called himself "the first among equals" and preferred conciliation over confrontation.

Sikandar Lodi (1489–1517 CE) — The Greatest Lodi

Son of Bahlul Lodi; the most powerful and successful ruler of the Lodi Dynasty. His reign is considered the high point of Lodi rule.

Accession & Early Challenges

  • Born as Nizam Khan; was the son of Bahlul Lodi and a Hindu princess.
  • Was elected Sultan by the nobles after Bahlul's death, despite opposition from his brother Barbak Shah.
  • Was a capable and energetic ruler — a complete contrast to his father's cautious approach.
  • Took the title Sikandar Shah — a name associated with Alexander the Great.

Military Campaigns

  • Suppressed the rebellion of his brother Barbak Shah (governor of Jaunpur) and reasserted central authority.
  • Annexed Bihar — a significant achievement. The region was under the control of local Afghan chiefs and the Sharqi rulers.
  • Signed the Treaty of Behar (1494) with the ruler of Bengal — defining the boundary between Bihar and Bengal.
  • Defeated the Rajputs of Gwalior, Dholpur and other princely states.
  • Conquered Malwa briefly (in alliance with the ruler of Mewar) but later lost it.
  • Suppressed the rebellions of Afghan nobles, ensuring tighter central control.

Administrative Reforms

  • Shifted the capital from Delhi to Agra — Agra became the new seat of power (later the Mughal capital).
  • Introduced a comprehensive system of market control — fixed prices of essential goods, appointed officials to supervise markets.
  • Introduced the Gaz-i-Sikandari — a standard yard (approximately 33 inches) for land measurement.
  • Reorganised the land revenue system — based on proper measurement and assessment.
  • Introduced the Dagh (horse branding) and Chehra (descriptive rolls of soldiers) systems to ensure army efficiency.
  • Improved the spy system (Barid and Munhiyan).
  • Appointed trusted nobles to key positions — a break from Bahlul's "first among equals" policy.

Religious Policy

  • Was a strict Sunni Muslim; took the title Sikandar after destroying Hindu temples at Mathura.
  • Destroyed several Hindu temples — including the Vishwanath Temple at Kashi (though this is sometimes attributed to his predecessors).
  • Imposed the Jizya strictly on non-Muslims.
  • Banned the construction of new Hindu temples.
  • Did not allow Hindus to hold high administrative posts — unlike his father.
  • Despite these policies, his reign was economically prosperous.

Society & Economy under Sikandar Lodi

  • Trade and commerce flourished — roads were safe, markets were regulated.
  • Agra grew into a major commercial centre.
  • Agriculture improved with better revenue systems.
  • Sufism continued to be influential — the Chishti order remained popular.
  • The Bhakti movement — Kabir, Nanak and other saints were active during this period.

Death

  • Died in 1517 CE at Agra; buried in the Tomb of Sikandar Lodi (Delhi) — a fine example of Lodi architecture.
  • Was succeeded by his son Ibrahim Lodi.
Exam Tip: Sikandar Lodi shifted the capital from Delhi to Agra, introduced the Gaz-i-Sikandari for land measurement, and was the greatest ruler of the Lodi Dynasty. He is often called the founder of Agra's importance in Indian history.

Ibrahim Lodi (1517–1526 CE) — The Last Sultan

Son of Sikandar Lodi; the last ruler of the Delhi Sultanate. His short reign was marked by internal rebellion and ended with the First Battle of Panipat.

  • Was the son of Sikandar Lodi and ascended the throne in 1517 CE.
  • Was young, ambitious, and determined to centralise power — a policy that alienated the Afghan nobles.
  • Refused to share power with the nobles — unlike his grandfather Bahlul Lodi.
  • Insulted powerful Afghan nobles, including his own relatives, causing widespread resentment.
  • His harsh treatment of nobles led to a series of rebellions.
  • The most serious rebellion came from his own brother Jalal Khan (governor of Kalpi) and his uncle Alam Khan Lodi.
  • Alam Khan Lodi invited Babur — the Mughal ruler of Kabul — to invade India.
  • Rana Sanga of Mewar also invited Babur to invade, hoping to weaken Delhi.
  • Daulat Khan Lodi, the governor of Punjab, also invited Babur.
  • Thus, the disunity among Afghan nobles directly led to Babur's invasion.

The First Battle of Panipat (21 April 1526 CE)

One of the most decisive battles in Indian history — marking the end of the Delhi Sultanate and the beginning of the Mughal Empire.

Background

  • Babur — the ruler of Kabul and descendant of Timur — had long desired to conquer India.
  • He invaded India several times before 1526; his earlier attempts to capture Punjab were unsuccessful.
  • The invitation from disgruntled Afghan nobles and Rana Sanga gave him the perfect opportunity.
  • Babur marched from Kabul with a well-equipped army of about 12,000 soldiers.
  • Ibrahim Lodi commanded a much larger army of about 100,000 soldiers and 1,000 elephants — but with outdated tactics.

The Battle

  • Fought on 21 April 1526 CE at Panipat, about 90 km north of Delhi.
  • Babur used the Tulughma tactic — a flanking manoeuvre to outflank the larger Lodi army.
  • He also used the Araba (cart) tactic — carts tied together in a line to protect his troops.
  • Babur's field artillery (cannons) was decisive — the Lodi army had no answer to it.
  • Ibrahim Lodi's elephants were panicked by the gunfire and turned on their own army.
  • Ibrahim Lodi was defeated and killed on the battlefield — his body was found among the dead.
  • Babur occupied Delhi and Agra — the treasure of the Lodis fell into his hands.

Significance

  • Marked the end of the Delhi Sultanate — a political system that had lasted for 320 years.
  • Marked the beginning of Mughal rule in India — which lasted until 1857 CE.
  • Demonstrated the superiority of artillery and modern tactics over traditional Indian warfare.
  • Opened India to the Mughal dynasty's cultural and architectural legacy.
BPSC Must-Know: The First Battle of Panipat (21 April 1526 CE) was fought between Babur and Ibrahim Lodi. Babur's use of artillery (cannons) and the Tulughma tactic gave him victory against a much larger army. Ibrahim Lodi was killed in the battle — ending the Lodi Dynasty and the Delhi Sultanate.

Administration of the Lodi Dynasty

Central Administration

  • Sultan — the head of state; his authority was more limited than under the Tughlaqs or Khaljis.
  • Wazir — Prime Minister; looked after finance and general administration.
  • Diwan-i-Arz — Minister of Military Affairs.
  • Diwan-i-Insha — Minister of Correspondence.
  • Diwan-i-Risalat — Minister of Religious Affairs.
  • Barid and Munhiyan — intelligence and spy system.

Afghan Nobility & Administration

  • The Lodis relied heavily on the Afghan nobles — many were their kinsmen or tribal chiefs.
  • Bahlul Lodi's policy of treating nobles as equals — Shafqat (brotherhood) — created a loyal nobility.
  • The nobles often received hereditary land grants and controlled large areas.
  • Sikandar Lodi tried to centralise power and reduce the nobles' influence.
  • Ibrahim Lodi's harsh treatment of nobles caused widespread revolt — directly leading to Babur's invasion.
  • The Afghan theory of kingship — the Sultan was "the first among equals" — was different from the divine-right theory of the Turks.

Revenue Administration

  • Land revenue was the main source of income — usually 1/3 to 1/5 of produce.
  • Sikandar Lodi introduced the Gaz-i-Sikandari (standard yard) for land measurement.
  • Revenue was assessed on the basis of actual measurement.
  • Taxes on trade, cattle, irrigation and other economic activities.
  • Jizya was imposed on non-Muslims — more strictly under Sikandar Lodi.

Military Administration

  • Standing army — based on feudal levies from Afghan chiefs and land grants.
  • Dagh (horse branding) and Chehra (descriptive rolls) were introduced under Sikandar Lodi.
  • Elephants and cavalry were the main components of the army.
  • The Lodi army lagged behind in artillery — a key reason for their defeat at Panipat.

Society & Culture under the Lodis

  • Society remained feudal and agrarian — most people were peasants.
  • Afghan nobles were at the top of the social hierarchy; Turkish and Indian Muslims below them.
  • Hindus faced Jizya and other restrictions, especially under Sikandar Lodi.
  • Sufism flourished — the Chishti order remained very influential in Delhi and other centres.
  • Bhakti movement — Kabir, Guru Nanak, Ravidas and other saints were active during this period.
  • Regional languages — Hindi, Urdu, Bengali, Gujarati, Marathi flourished in the independent Sultanates.
  • Persian literature — histories like Tarikh-i-Da'udi and Waqiat-i-Mushtaqi were written.
  • Music — the tradition of Amir Khusrau continued; the Lodi court patronised music.

Lodi Architecture

Lodi architecture is a distinctive style that bridges the Tughlaq and Mughal periods. It is known for its simplicity, elegance, and use of a variety of materials.

Key Features

  • Octagonal tombs — the most distinctive feature of Lodi architecture.
  • Double domes — a dome within a dome; used for the first time in India in Lodi tombs.
  • Sloping walls (batter) — continued from Tughlaq architecture.
  • Use of red sandstone, grey stone, and coloured tiles.
  • Chhatris (small domed pavilions) and kiosks on the roof.
  • Decorative plasterwork and calligraphy.
  • Char Bagh (four-quartered garden) layout around tombs.
  • Pietra dura — inlay work, introduced in this period.

Bihar Connection (BPSC Focus)

  • Sikandar Lodi annexed Bihar in the early 16th century — bringing the region under direct Delhi Sultanate control.
  • The Treaty of Behar (1494 CE) — signed between Sikandar Lodi and the ruler of Bengal — defined the boundary between Bihar and Bengal. This is a landmark event in Bihar's medieval history.
  • Sikandar Lodi appointed his son Jalal Khan as governor of Bihar — indicating the importance of the region.
  • Bihar Sharif and Maner Sharif continued as important Sufi centres during the Lodi period.
  • Sasaram (in Bihar) — the birthplace of Sher Shah Suri — was under Lodi rule. Sher Shah Suri served as a governor under the Lodis before founding the Sur Empire.
  • Darbhanga and other regions of north Bihar were under local Rajput and Bhumihar chiefs — the Lodis had limited control over the interior.
  • After the First Battle of Panipat (1526 CE), Bihar passed to the Mughals — and later, after 1540 CE, to Sher Shah Suri's Sur Empire.
  • Sher Shah Suri — born in Sasaram, Bihar — was a Lodi governor before he established his own empire. He is one of Bihar's greatest historical figures.
BPSC Must-Know: Sikandar Lodi's annexation of Bihar and the Treaty of Behar (1494 CE) are essential BPSC topics. Sher Shah Suri — born in Sasaram, Bihar — served as a governor under the Lodis before founding the Sur Empire. His early career is tied to the Lodi administration of Bihar.

Decline of the Lodi Dynasty

  • Ibrahim Lodi's harsh treatment of nobles — he alienated the Afghan nobility, who had been the backbone of Lodi power.
  • Rise of regional powers — Mewar under Rana Sanga became very powerful; Gujarat, Malwa, Bengal and the Deccan Sultanates were independent.
  • Failure to adopt modern warfare — the Lodis lagged behind in artillery and military technology.
  • Invitation to Babur — disgruntled Afghan nobles (Alam Khan Lodi, Daulat Khan Lodi) invited Babur to invade India.
  • First Battle of Panipat (1526 CE) — Ibrahim Lodi was defeated and killed; the dynasty ended.
  • The Delhi Sultanate itself came to an end — replaced by the Mughal Empire.
